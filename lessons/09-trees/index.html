Lesson 9 · from an alignment to a dated species tree

Building Phylogenetic Trees

New here? Start with the basics of this lesson: a few short blocks with things to try.

A tree is the shape behind most of the earlier lessons: the relative whose genome was used to polish ours, the gene copies that TOGA2 matched across species, the SINE insertions shared by some animals and not others, the split time between two genomes. This lesson builds trees from the ground up. It starts with how to read one (root, clade, branch length, Newick), then turns an alignment into characters, corrects distances for multiple hits with substitution models, and builds trees by the four families of methods in the order they were invented: distance (UPGMA, neighbour joining), parsimony, maximum likelihood (IQ-TREE, RAxML-NG) and Bayesian inference (MrBayes). It then asks how much a branch can be trusted (bootstrap, UFBoot, SH-aLRT, posterior probability), puts time on the tree (clocks, calibrations, BEAST2), and explains why gene trees disagree with the species tree (incomplete lineage sorting, the ABBA-BABA test for gene flow, ASTRAL). It then gives a helper for choosing a method and a checklist for the methods paragraph, and ends with a workbench that follows one small alignment through every method and every kind of support.

Every section has a toy to try: the animals, sequences and branch lengths in the figures are invented so that each idea can be followed by hand, and no result from the lab's own projects is used. Commands and defaults were read from each tool's own manual and source code at the versions named in each section. Hover over a dotted term to see what it means.

01Reading a tree

A phylogenetic tree is the result every method in this lesson produces. Before we ask how to build one, we must be able to read one: which parts of a picture carry information and which are only decoration. A reader who mixes these up will draw conclusions the tree does not support.

The parts of a tree

  • Tips (also called leaves, or taxa, singular taxon) are the things being compared: species, individuals, genes, or DNA sequences. Names are written at the tips.
  • Branches (also called edges) are the lines. A branch joins an ancestor to its descendants.
  • Nodes are the points where branches meet. An inner node stands for an ancestor that split into two (or, in a tree not fully resolved, more) lines of descent.
  • The root is the oldest node. A tree that has one is a rooted tree: every branch has a direction, from older to younger, and time runs from the root to the tips. Without a root, we have an unrooted tree: it shows which tips are close to which, and not which way time runs.

Two words for groups

In a rooted tree, a clade is an ancestor together with all of its descendants. On the picture it is everything you cut off by cutting one branch. Wikipedia's definition is "a group of organisms with a last common ancestor"; we add "and all of its descendants", which is what makes a clade a clade. Two clades that come from the same node are sister groups: each is the closest relative of the other within the tree. Both words describe a rooted tree. On an unrooted tree they have no meaning until a root is chosen, and the next paragraphs and the figure show why.

Where the root comes from

The data used to build a tree, such as differences between DNA sequences, say how similar tips are. They do not say which direction time runs. The usual way to find the root is to add an outgroup: a lineage known from other evidence to have split off before all the others (the ingroup) diverged. Wikipedia describes it as "a more distantly related group of organisms that serves as a reference group when determining the evolutionary relationships of the ingroup", and says it "allows for the phylogeny to be rooted". The tree is built with the outgroup included; the root is then placed on the branch that leads to the outgroup. This is a choice, and a poorly chosen outgroup, one that is not really outside, puts the root in the wrong place. When no outgroup is available, a second approach is midpoint rooting: find the two tips that are farthest apart along the branches, and put the root halfway between them. It assumes that all lineages have changed at the same speed, the same assumption that UPGMA makes in section 04, and it fails when they have not.

Branch lengths

Branch lengths may mean something or nothing, and the figure legend or the methods must say which. Following Wikipedia: a cladogram has "branch lengths [that] do not represent time or relative amount of character change"; a phylogram has "branch lengths proportional to the amount of character change"; a chronogram "explicitly represents time through its branch lengths". A tree computed from DNA is normally a phylogram, and the scale bar then shows the amount of change per site (we say what this means in section 03). A tree can be redrawn as a cladogram by ignoring the lengths, and the figure below lets you do that.

Rotating a node changes nothing

A branching point is like a hinge. Each node can be turned half-way round, which swaps the order of the branches that come from it, and the tree is still the same tree: the same tips are still joined by the same branches. Only the order of the names on the page changes. The Newick text format, below, shows this too. In the words of the PHYLIP documentation, the left-right order of descendants of a node "affects the representation, even though it is biologically uninteresting". It follows that two tips that are next to each other on the page need not be close relatives, and that the tip at the top of a tree is not "first" or "more primitive". Closeness is read from the nodes: how far you must go back along the branches to the node where the two lines meet.

What to take from the experiment. Rotating nodes changes the drawing statements and the Newick text, and nothing else. Moving the root leaves the unrooted tree and the distances along the branches unchanged, but it can change which groups are clades and which are sisters: the same unrooted tree allows as many different rooted trees as it has branches. This is why the choice of the outgroup matters for the biology, and the order of the names on the page does not.

Newick format: a tree as text

Programs exchange trees as text in the Newick format, written by an agreement reached at two meetings in 1986, the second at Newick's restaurant. The rules, quoted from the PHYLIP documentation: "Interior nodes are represented by a pair of matched parentheses. Between them are representations of the nodes that are immediately descended from that node, separated by commas." "The tree ends with a semicolon." A branch length is written "after a node and preceded by a colon". Here is a tiny example.

(A:0.1,B:0.2,(C:0.3,D:0.4):0.5); A and B : tips, with branches of length 0.1 and 0.2 (C,D) : an inner node whose two descendants are C and D :0.5 : the branch from that inner node up to its parent

The text is always a rooted description: the outermost pair of parentheses is the root. To store an unrooted tree, programs pick any node as the starting point; the PHYLIP description of the format says "the convention is simply to arbitrarily root the tree and report the resulting rooted tree". The unrooted toy tree above is therefore often written with three branches at the outermost level, and a reader must know that this last pair of parentheses is not a real root.

(Chicken:0.9,((Human:0.15,Chimp:0.15):0.1,Gorilla:0.3):0.2,(Mouse:0.3,Rat:0.25):0.35);

How many trees are there?

A method that looks for the best tree has to choose among possible trees. How many are there? Take only binary trees, where every inner node splits into exactly two, and give each tip its own name. The number depends on how many tips there are, n, and it grows with a speed that surprises everyone the first time:

rooted trees:   (2n−3)!! = 1 × 3 × 5 × … × (2n−3)
unrooted trees: (2n−5)!! = 1 × 3 × 5 × … × (2n−5)

The double exclamation mark m!! (a double factorial) means "multiply every second number down from m". The Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS, entry A001147) gives these numbers as "the number of unordered full binary trees with n+1 labeled leaves"; the numbers below were checked against the values printed there, and against a program that lists every tree.

Simple explanation with examples: where the formula comes from

Build an unrooted tree by adding the animals one at a time. Three animals can only be joined in one way: three branches meeting in the middle. Now add a fourth. It can be attached to any of the 3 branches, which gives 3 different trees. A tree with 4 tips has 5 branches (4 tip branches and 1 inner one), so the fifth animal can be attached in 5 ways, giving 3 × 5 = 15 trees. A tree with k tips has 2k−3 branches, so the next animal always has 2k−3 choices: 1 × 3 × 5 × 7 …

For a rooted tree with n tips, take any of the unrooted trees and place the root on any of its 2n−3 branches. Each rooted tree arises from exactly one such pair, so the rooted count is the unrooted count times (2n−3). For 4 tips: 3 × 5 = 15 rooted trees. The three unrooted trees for A, B, C, D are the three ways to pair the animals: AB|CD, AC|BD and AD|BC. The button below lists them.

Notice that the number of rooted trees for n tips equals the number of unrooted trees for n+1 tips: an outgroup is just one more tip.

Ten tips already allow more than two million unrooted trees; twenty tips allow about 2 × 1020. No computer can look at them all for the data sets we usually have, with tens or hundreds of tips. The methods in the rest of this lesson differ mainly in how they get around that: some build one tree by a fixed recipe (section 04), others search among trees with a score (sections 05 to 08).

Remember. (1) The order of names on a page means nothing; the nodes mean everything. (2) Clade and sister group need a root; closeness along the branches does not. (3) The root usually comes from an outgroup, which is an assumption. (4) Branch lengths can mean amount of change, time, or nothing: check the legend. (5) A Newick string is one of many equivalent texts for the same tree. (6) The number of possible trees explodes, so every method is a way to avoid trying all of them. Next: how a set of sequences becomes the characters a tree is built from. If you are not sure what makes two sequences "alignable" see alignment as scoring.

02From sequences to characters

A tree-building program does not read your sequences. It reads a table: one row per sequence, one column per position, and in every column the letters that sit in the same place. Each column is called a character and the letter in it is its state. Everything the program concludes comes from comparing states within columns. So the first job is not choosing a tree method. It is deciding which letters belong in the same column, and that decision is a claim about history.

Homology, orthology and paralogy

Two sequences are homologous if they descend from one sequence in a common ancestor. Homology is a conclusion, not a measurement. Similarity is the evidence we use for it (lesson 8, section 06, shows how similarity is scored and why a high score is hard to get by chance). Homologous genes are of two kinds, and the difference decides what a tree of them means. Koonin (2005) puts it in one sentence: orthologs and paralogs are "two fundamentally different types of homologous genes that evolved, respectively, by vertical descent from a single ancestral gene and by duplication".

  • Orthologs: copies in different species that separated because the species separated. They are "the same gene" in two animals.
  • Paralogs: copies that separated because the gene was duplicated within a genome. They can sit in one animal, or in two, if the duplication was older than the species split.

A gene tree of orthologs follows the species. A gene tree that mixes in a paralog follows a gene duplication, not a species split, and it can look wrong for the species tree although the programs worked correctly. Click any two genes below.

For SINEs the same question is asked about insertion sites: whether two copies sit at the same place in two genomes (lesson 7, section 05). Section 10 of this lesson returns to gene trees that disagree with the species tree.

The alignment is the data

Lesson 8 built an alignment between two sequences. Tree programs need one for many sequences at once, a multiple sequence alignment, often shortened to MSA. Each column says: these letters descend from one letter in the ancestor. A dash says that a letter was inserted in the other sequences or deleted from this one. Wong and colleagues (2008) describe the usual practice bluntly: "the alignment is treated as an observation, and all of the subsequent inferences depend on the alignment being correct". Their yeast data showed that different alignment methods can lead to different conclusions. An alignment is a hypothesis you test, not a measurement you read.

What goes into a column depends on the data type. For DNA each column is one base position, and the states are A, C, G, T (plus the dash). For a SINE insertion the character can be a whole insertion that is present or absent (section 05). For amino acids there are 20 states. This lesson uses DNA.

Most columns carry no information about the tree. A column where all sequences have the same letter is constant. A column is parsimony-informative if at least two different letters each occur in at least two sequences, because only then can it prefer one grouping of the sequences over another (section 05 shows how). A letter that occurs in only one sequence says something about that sequence alone.

Move a gap, trim a column

Choose which gap to move and slide it, then click column numbers to remove columns (trim them). Watch the table of pairwise differences. For two sequences, a difference is a column where both have a letter and the letters differ. This page ignores columns where either sequence has a dash, which is a choice of this toy: programs handle gaps in different ways. The p-distance is the number of differences divided by the number of columns compared (section 03 explains what it misses).

Simple explanation with examples: why a misplaced gap makes sequences look different

Take two spellings of one word: COLOUR and COLOR. Line them up letter by letter and you get COLOUR over COLOR-: the U meets O, the R meets U, and three of six columns "differ". Put the dash under the U instead, COLOUR over COLO-R, and nothing differs at all. The words did not change. Only the claim about which letter is the same letter changed.

A tree program sees only the columns. If a gap is in the wrong place, it counts invented differences, and an invented difference looks exactly like a real one. This is why the alignment is treated as part of the result, and why you should look at it.

In a run of identical letters, as in the three C above, the dash can be placed at any of the three spots and the columns agree equally. That is a different problem: a gap whose place cannot be known. It is harmless for distances, but it shows that some columns are less certain than others.

Making the alignment: MAFFT

MAFFT is a widely used program for multiple alignment of nucleotide or amino acid sequences. Its manual says that --auto "automatically selects an appropriate strategy from L-INS-i, FFT-NS-i and FFT-NS-2, according to data size", where FFT-NS-2 is the fast progressive method. The manual calls L-INS-i "probably most accurate" and recommends it for fewer than 200 sequences. That strategy (--localpair with --maxiterate 1000) computes all pairwise alignments with the Smith-Waterman method (lesson 8, section 06) and then refines the result by repeated cycles. Gap opening and extension scores play the same role as in that section. The manual page is dated 2007 and says it does not cover all features of the latest version, so check mafft --help for the version you install.

# let MAFFT choose the strategy from the data size
mafft --auto genes.fa > genes.aln.fa
# fewer than 200 sequences, most accurate strategy of the manual (L-INS-i)
mafft --localpair --maxiterate 1000 genes.fa > genes.aln.fa

Look at the result before you use it, in a viewer that shows columns. Regions where the sequences have no clear similarity, long gaps, and a sequence of very different length are the places to check.

Some columns are badly aligned, or have changed so often that their letters carry no signal. The ClipKIT authors write that such "highly divergent sites ... can stem from erroneous inference of homology and saturation of substitutions". Trimming programs remove them before the tree is built. One gene or many genes joined together

A single gene gives one alignment, and its tree is the history of that gene. It can be short and carry little signal. The usual remedy is to align many genes one by one and join the alignments end to end into one long table, a concatenated alignment (also called a supermatrix). The program then looks for the one tree that fits all columns. That assumes that all the genes follow the same tree, and they do not always do so: paralogs, as above, and chance differences in how gene copies were inherited (section 10) both break it. The other route is to build a tree for each gene and combine the gene trees afterwards. Both routes start from the careful alignments of this section.

Before you build a tree. Keep orthologs only, align them, look at the alignment, decide what to trim and write it down, and know whether you join genes into one table or keep them apart. The next section turns the columns into distances.

03Distances and substitution models

Once sequences are aligned (section 02), the first thing we want is a number: how far apart are two species? The obvious answer, the share of letters that differ, is always too small, and the error grows with time. This section shows why, how a substitution model repairs it, and what each added piece of the model (unequal change rates, unequal base frequencies, rate differences between sites) buys.

The plain distance: p

Take two aligned sequences and count the columns where the letters differ. Divide by the number of columns compared. This is the p-distance. Columns with a gap are left out of the count (a gap is not a letter; see alignment scoring).

A A C G T T G C A A G C T T A C G G A T C B A C G T C G C A A G C T T G C G G A T A ^ ^ ^ 3 differences in 20 columns: p = 3/20 = 0.15

A change from one letter to another is a substitution. The p-distance counts the differences we can see. It does not count the substitutions that happened, and the two are not the same.

Multiple hits and saturation

A site can change more than once. Along one branch, an A may become G and later G becomes A again: two substitutions, no visible difference. Or A becomes G and then T: two substitutions, one visible difference. These hidden changes are called multiple hits. The older the split, the more of them there are, so p falls further and further behind the true amount of change.

There is also a ceiling. Once every site has been hit many times, the two sequences have forgotten their common ancestor. Each pair of letters is then unrelated, and two unrelated letters agree one time in four if the four bases are equally common. So p cannot go above 3/4 = 0.75, however long ago the species split. This flattening is saturation. Two saturated sequences look equally different whether they split 20 million or 200 million years ago.

The remedy is to correct the distance with a model. The simplest is JC69 (Jukes and Cantor, 1969): every site changes at the same rate, and a change goes to each of the three other bases equally often. Write d for the true distance: the average number of substitutions per site on the whole path between the two sequences (both branches added together, counting every hit, visible or hidden). Under JC69 the expected p is

p = 3/4 × (1 − e−4d/3)    so    d = −3/4 × ln(1 − 4p/3)

For small d the curve follows p = d. As d grows it bends and flattens at 0.75. The second form is the correction: give it a p measured from data and it returns an estimate of d. It cannot be used when p reaches 0.75, because the logarithm no longer exists. In practice the estimate becomes very uncertain well before that, since a tiny change in p then means a huge change in d.

Things to try. Leave the settings at "none" and slide d up: the dots follow the blue curve and the corrected distance stays near the truth until p nears 0.75. Shorten the sequences: the dots scatter, and the corrected value scatters even more, because a short sequence gives a noisy p. Then choose strong rate differences or invariable sites: p flattens earlier, and the JC69 correction, which assumes equal sites, now falls short of the true d. That shortfall is the reason for the extra model pieces below.

Simple explanation with examples: why p stops at 0.75

Imagine a column that starts as A in the ancestor. Each lineage keeps changing that column from time to time. After a very long time, the letter in species 1 is just whichever of the four letters it landed on, and the same in species 2, independently. Two independent picks from four letters agree 1 time in 4, so they differ 3 times in 4.

Short time: most columns have no change, so p is about the number of changes. Medium time: some columns change twice and are partly hidden, so p lags. Long time: p is stuck at 0.75 and tells you nothing more. A correction can still recover the distance in the middle, but not at the end.

Example with numbers: p = 0.30 under JC69 gives d = −0.75 × ln(1 − 0.4) = 0.38 substitutions per site. The sequences differ at 30 % of sites but changed 38 times per 100 sites.

Not all changes are equal: K80

The four bases come in two chemical families. The purines are A and G; the pyrimidines are C and T. A change inside a family (A↔G or C↔T) is a transition; a change between families is a transversion. In real DNA transitions are usually much more common than JC69 expects. If all changes were equally likely there would be twice as many transversions (two targets per base) as transitions (one target).

Kimura (1980) built this in. He took the share of sites with a transition difference (P) and with a transversion difference (Q) and gave the distance per site as

K = −½ ln[(1 − 2P − Q) × √(1 − 2Q)]

This is the formula given in the paper's abstract. Programs now usually fit the ratio of the two kinds of change by likelihood instead, but the idea is the same. Our reading, not a statement from the paper: transitions saturate sooner than transversions, so counting them separately keeps information that a single p throws away.

HKY and GTR: unequal base frequencies and free rates

The next two steps drop two more equalities. Real genomes do not have 25 % of each base; many are rich in A+T or in G+C. And the six kinds of change (A↔C, A↔G, A↔T, C↔G, C↔T, G↔T) need not all have the same rate. HKY (Hasegawa, Kishino and Yano, 1985) keeps K80's two classes of change and lets the base frequencies differ. GTR, the "general time-reversible" model, gives each of the six kinds of change its own rate, plus free base frequencies. "Time-reversible" means that the process looks the same run forwards or backwards in time, so the tree does not need an arrow of time and can be drawn unrooted.

Sites differ in rate: gamma and invariable sites

All the models above give every site the same rate. In coding DNA that is clearly false: the third position of a codon changes far more freely than the first two, and some positions cannot change without damage to the protein. Two additions handle this; they are written after the model name.

  • +I, invariable sites. A share of the sites is set to rate zero. They stay the same for ever.
  • +G, gamma-distributed rates. Each site has its own rate, drawn from a gamma distribution (a smooth curve of positive numbers) whose single "shape" number controls how unequal the rates are. Small shape: most sites nearly frozen, a few very fast. Large shape: nearly equal rates. Yang (1994) approximated the curve with a few equal-probability categories and found that "four such categories appear to be sufficient"; four is also the IQ-TREE default (+G, or +G8 for eight).

The two can be combined (+I+G). The toy above shows the effect: a few very fast sites hit the ceiling early while the many slow sites barely change, so p rises quickly at first and then creeps. A model that gives every site the same rate misreads this as less change than really happened. ModelFinder also offers a "free-rate" family (+R) that estimates the rate categories from the data instead of forcing a gamma curve; its authors note that the gamma assumptions "may be unrealistic and bias phylogenetic estimates".

A rate model assumes the alignment columns are homologous (section 02). Gamma will not rescue a misaligned column.

How a model is chosen

More parameters always fit the data at least as well, just as a wiggly curve always fits a scatter of points better than a straight line. The question is whether the extra fit is worth the extra parameters. Model selection scores each model by its fit minus a penalty for its parameters. The common scores are AIC (the Akaike information criterion) and BIC (the Bayesian information criterion); BIC punishes parameters harder, and section 06 gives both formulas. The model with the best score is used.

You do not do this by hand. ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017), built into IQ-TREE, runs it for you: it compares 22 DNA substitution models together with several rate models (equal, +I, +G, +R) and reports the best by the criterion you choose. Its worked example uses BIC and reads a difference above 10 as "strong evidence against the model with the higher BIC score". Its authors also warn that the search can get trapped and recommend repeating the model selection.

# model selection only, no tree search (command form as in the ModelFinder paper)
iqtree -s alignment.fasta -m MF

Two cautions. First, "best model" means best among the ones tried; none of them may describe the data well. Second, richer is not always needed. Our reading: when p is small (say below 0.1) there are few hidden changes and JC69 and GTR give nearly the same distance; the choice matters most for old splits, where saturation is serious. The next section turns distances into trees (section 04), and the maximum-likelihood section later uses the same models directly.

Simple explanation with examples: the model ladder

Think of the models as a ladder, each rung giving up one simplifying assumption. JC69: "every change equally likely, every base equally common." K80: "but transitions are more likely." HKY: "and bases can be unequally common." GTR: "and every kind of change has its own rate." +I and +G: "and sites differ in rate."

Example: two sequences differing at 5 % of sites. Almost no site has been hit twice, so every rung of the ladder gives almost the same distance, about 0.05. Two sequences differing at 60 % of sites are close to the ceiling: the corrected distance depends strongly on which rung you chose. In practice you let ModelFinder choose and then report which model it chose.

04Distance trees: UPGMA and neighbour joining

Section 03 turned a pair of sequences into one number, the distance between them. This section builds a tree from a table of such numbers. Two methods do it: UPGMA, which is simple and assumes every lineage changes at the same speed, and neighbour joining, which does not need that assumption. You will run both, step by step, on the same small table, and then make one lineage evolve faster and watch one of them fail.

What a distance method does

A distance matrix is a table with one number for every pair of tips: how different those two sequences are. A distance method uses only this table. It never looks at the sequences again. It repeats one move: find the pair that should be neighbours, replace the pair by one new group, and shrink the table by one row. When one group is left, the tree is finished. This is quick, and it is easy to follow by hand. The two methods differ in one thing only: what "should be neighbours" means.

Words used below. A cluster is a tip or a group of tips treated as one item. A branch length is the amount of change along a branch, in the same units as the table. The topology is the branching pattern alone, without lengths.

UPGMA: join the closest pair, always

The name is "unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean". It is the method of Sokal and Michener (1958). The steps:

  1. Find the smallest number in the table. Join those two clusters.
  2. Put the new node halfway: its height is half the distance. Each of the two new branches reaches from the new node down to its cluster's own height (a tip has height 0).
  3. Make a smaller table. The distance from the new cluster to any other cluster is the average of all tip-to-tip distances between them. This is the formula of the average method in SciPy, which its documentation calls "the UPGMA algorithm".
  4. Repeat until one cluster is left. That cluster is the root.

The tree it draws has every tip at the same distance from the root. The word for this is ultrametric. That is a built-in assumption: UPGMA gives the correct tree only if all lineages changed at the same rate, a molecular clock (s09). Then distance grows with time since the split, so the smallest distance is the youngest split, and joining the closest pair is right. If one lineage changes faster, it is far from everyone, even from its own sister, and the closest pair in the table may not be the closest relatives.

Neighbour joining: join the pair that is close compared with everything else

Saitou and Nei (1987) proposed it. The abstract says: "The principle of this method is to find pairs of operational taxonomic units (OTUs [= neighbors]) that minimize the total branch length at each stage of clustering of OTUs starting with a starlike tree." A starlike tree has all tips joined to one central point and nothing else. Each step pulls one pair out of the star, choosing the pair that keeps the total length of the tree smallest. The formulas below are as written in the code comments of the scikit-bio implementation, which cites that paper. With n clusters left:

Row sum r
For each cluster i, add up its distances to all the other clusters: ri. A cluster on a long branch has a large r.
Q criterion
Q(i,j) = (n − 2) × d(i,j) − ri − rj. Join the pair with the smallest Q. A pair scores well if it is close to each other and each of the two is far from everything else.
Branch lengths
Li = d(i,j)/2 + (ri − rj)/(2(n − 2)), and Lj = d(i,j) − Li. The two branches are not equal: the cluster with the larger row sum gets the longer branch.
New table
d(new,k) = (d(i,k) + d(j,k) − d(i,j))/2 for every other cluster k.
The end
When three clusters are left, their three branch lengths follow from the three distances: the branch to a is (d(a,b) + d(a,c) − d(b,c))/2, and likewise for the others.

The result is an unrooted tree: it has no root and no time direction, and its tips are not all the same distance from the centre (s01). To root it you add an outgroup or use the midpoint of the longest path, as s01 explains. In the figures below an unrooted tree is drawn from an inner node, only so that it fits on the page.

Why does this work when rates differ? Take a tip on a long branch. All its distances are large, so its row sum r is large, and Q subtracts that. The long branch no longer makes the pair look unrelated. NJ compares each pair with the whole row of distances, not with the single smallest number.

The test case for NJ is the additive distance matrix: the distance between two tips equals the sum of the branch lengths on the path between them in some tree. If the table is additive, NJ gives back that tree. The scikit-bio documentation says "NJ is most accurate when distances are additive", and the check at the end of this section confirms exact recovery on 200 random additive tables. Real tables are not exactly additive, because distances are estimated from finite sequences (s03). The same documentation warns that then "negative branch lengths may be produced". In that implementation NJ takes time proportional to n3 for n tips.

Simple explanation with examples: why the closest pair can be the wrong pair

Think of a family. Anna and her brother Ben grew up in the same town. Ben still lives there, and so does their cousin Carl, in the next street. Anna emigrated and now lives 9,000 km away. Measure only how far apart people live: Ben and Carl are closest, and "join the closest pair" says Ben and Carl are the nearest relatives. But Anna and Ben are siblings. Distance travelled is not the same as kinship.

DNA does the same. If Anna's lineage changed fast, its sequence is far from everyone, including its sister lineage. UPGMA reads the table like a map of distances and trusts the smallest number, so it pairs the wrong two. Neighbour joining asks: "who is close, once I allow for who is far from everybody?" Anna is far from everybody, so her large distances are discounted, and her best partner, Ben, is found.

05Parsimony

The distance methods of the last section first squeeze the sequences into a table of distances and then build a tree from the table. Parsimony does not make a table. It looks at the sequences column by column and asks one question of every possible tree: how many changes would this tree need to explain what we see? The tree that needs the fewest is the winner. The idea is old, simple and easy to do by hand, and its failure is also instructive. This section shows how the changes are counted, which columns carry information, how the method is misled when some branches are long, and why a SINE insertion is a particularly clean kind of character.

The criterion

A character is one column of the alignment. In each taxon (a species or sequence in the tree) the character shows one state. A tree explains a character if we can write a state on every inside node of the tree so that the states change as rarely as possible along the branches. Each change is one step. The parsimony score of a tree is the number of steps summed over all characters. Among the trees that were tried, maximum parsimony picks the lowest score. "Parsimony" means thrift: the tree that needs the fewest changes is the one that assumes the least.

Notice what is missing. There is no model of how fast each base changes, and branch lengths play no part in the count. That keeps the method simple, and it is also where it goes wrong later in this section. Compare the model-based methods in section 06.

Counting the steps on one tree: Fitch's method

For one character on one tree there could be very many ways to write states on the inside nodes. Fitch (1971) gave a method that finds the smallest number of changes without trying them all. Start at the tips. Every tip has a set holding its one state. Then move toward the root, taking two sister nodes at a time:

if the two sets share a state:  parent set = the shared states  (no step)
otherwise:  parent set = both sets together  (add one step)

The number of "otherwise" cases is the score for that character. The page below applies this rule to a 5-taxon tree. Click a letter on the tree, or in the table, to change a state. Red dots mark the nodes where a step was added.

Which sites matter

Look at the table's "kind of site" column and try changing bases.

  • A constant site has the same state everywhere. It needs no change on any tree.
  • A site where every state except one is shared by only one taxon (for example A A A A T) is uninformative. That single odd taxon needs exactly one change wherever it is placed, so every tree costs the same.
  • A site is informative when at least two different states each occur in at least two taxa (G G A A A). Only such a site can prefer one tree over another.

"Informative" means only that the site could prefer some tree. A site can be informative in general and still cost the same on the three trees shown, as site 8 does here. Parsimony therefore ignores most of an alignment: only the informative sites vote. When the species are close relatives, most columns are constant, and a few informative ones decide the tree.

Counting steps for one tree is easy. Finding the best tree is not: the number of possible trees grows very fast with the number of taxa (see section 01), so programs search through trees rather than test them all. Searching is the same problem for every tree method, and section 06 comes back to it.

When parsimony is misled: long branches

Parsimony counts every change as evidence. Think about two lineages that have changed a great deal, so that their branches in the tree are long. Each has many changes of its own. Because there are only four bases, some of these changes will by chance land on the same base in both lineages. To the counting method, a base shared by two taxa looks like a base inherited from a common ancestor, and the two long branches are pulled together, even when they are not close relatives. This is long-branch attraction.

Felsenstein (1978) showed this in a four-taxon case. His abstract says that for the three parsimony and compatibility methods he studied, the conditions of failure "are essentially that parallel changes exceed informative, nonparallel changes", that this is possible "even when change is improbable a priori, provided that evolutionary rates in different lineages are sufficiently unequal", and that the methods "will fail to converge to the true phylogeny as more and more data are accumulated". (We read the abstract only; the paper itself was not available to us.) The last point matters most: more data does not rescue the method, because the extra data carry the same wrong signal.

Simple explanation with examples: why a shared base does not always mean shared ancestry

Imagine two cousins, each given a different number at birth, who both change their phone number every month. Over years each has had hundreds of numbers. Sooner or later both will have had the same number at some time, by pure chance. If you only compared lists of numbers and counted the matches, you would think they shared a household.

DNA has four letters, so a match by chance is far easier: a position that changes often will land on the same letter in two unrelated lineages one time in four. The cousins with few changes are rarely matched by chance, so for them a match is better evidence of a family link.

That is the whole idea. Fast-changing, long branches produce matches that mean nothing, and parsimony cannot tell them from the matches that do.

Simple explanation with examples: counting by hand with the rule

Take the four taxa A, B, C, D with bases G, G, A, A on the tree (A,B) (C,D).

tips: A=G B=G C=A D=A pairs: {G} {A} (A,B) agree; (C,D) agree root: {G,A} {G} and {A} share nothing: 1 step

Score: one step. On the tree (A,C) (B,D) the pairs are G with A and G with A: both pairs share nothing, so each adds a step, and then {G,A} and {G,A} share states, so the root adds none: two steps. Parsimony prefers (A,B) (C,D) because it needs one change instead of two. This site is informative.

Rare genomic changes: SINE insertions as characters

A base can change again and again. A new SINE insertion at one place in one genome is a rare event. Lesson 7, section 05 shows how the same place is found in several genomes and why "copy present" or "empty site" can be written as a character; this section does not repeat that. What matters here is how such a character is read with a parsimony count.

Each locus (one place in the genome) has two states: 1, the copy is present, and 0, the empty site. The ancestral state is 0, because the copy had to arrive at some time, and reversal is rare. If two species share a copy, the simplest explanation is one insertion in their common ancestor, so they form a group. A copy in only one species says nothing about the tree, just like an uninformative base. A locus that does not fit a tree would need a second insertion or a loss. Nikaido, Rooney and Okada (1999) used SINE and LINE insertions this way for the even-toed ungulates and whales, and, by the abstract (read; the full text was not), concluded that whales are deeply nested within the even-toed ungulates and form a group with the hippopotamuses. For a given amount of data, rare changes like this are slow to collect, since each locus must be found, checked in the other genomes and confirmed to be at the same place, so the data sets are small compared with sequence alignments.

A SINE locus needs a different count from the DNA one. The Fitch count allows a change from 1 back to 0 as easily as 0 to 1. For SINE insertions a loss is rare, so the toy table asks only whether the sharing group is a group of the tree.

Simple explanation with examples: why an insertion is better evidence than a base

If two books have the same word on page 100, it could be because the word is common. If both have the same misprint in the same place, they were probably copied from the same book. A SINE insertion is the misprint; a base is the common word.

The comparison is not perfect. A misprint can be copied from an old edition into only some of the later ones, so a gene tree may disagree with the species tree even when nothing is wrong. That is the subject of section 10.

What to take away

  • Parsimony chooses the tree that needs the fewest changes. Fitch counting finds that number for a given tree, one site at a time.
  • Only informative sites separate trees.
  • When branches differ much in length, parsimony may join the long ones, and more data makes this worse, not better.
  • Rare changes such as SINE insertions are characters with a known direction and little chance of repeating, which suits a simple counting method.

06Maximum likelihood

Parsimony counts changes. Maximum likelihood asks a different question: if this tree and this model of evolution were true, how probable would our alignment be? The tree that makes the alignment most probable wins. This section works through that calculation by hand on four sequences, then shows how programs do the same thing on thousands.

What a likelihood is

The likelihood of a tree is the probability of the data given that tree. Here the data are the aligned sequences. The "tree" means its shape (the topology) and its branch lengths, together with a model of substitution from section 03. Posada and Crandall (2021) write it as P(S | T, M): the probability of the sequences S, given tree T and model M.

Read that direction carefully. A likelihood is the chance of the data if the tree is right. It is not the chance that the tree is right. Getting from one to the other needs extra assumptions, which is what section 08 is about.

Felsenstein's 1981 method makes two simplifying assumptions, and most programs still make them: sites change independently of each other, and branches change independently of each other. Posada and Crandall call these "unrealistic", but they are what makes the calculation possible. Because sites are independent, the likelihood of the alignment is the likelihood of each column multiplied together.

The numbers become very small very quickly, so programs add up the natural logarithms of the column likelihoods instead of multiplying. This sum is the log-likelihood, written ln L. It is negative, and a value closer to zero is better. RAxML-NG's own tutorial shows a real example of −5708.92 for one alignment.

Simple explanation with examples: likelihood with a coin

You toss a coin 10 times and get 7 heads. You want to know the coin's chance of heads, h.

If h = 0.5, the probability of exactly this sequence of tosses is 0.57 × 0.53 = 0.00098. If h = 0.7, it is 0.77 × 0.33 = 0.00222. So h = 0.7 is the better explanation: it has the higher likelihood. No other value of h does better, so 0.7 is the maximum likelihood estimate.

Nothing here says "h is probably 0.7". It says only that, among the values of h, 0.7 makes what we saw most probable. Tree inference works the same way. The tree plays the part of h, and the alignment plays the part of the tosses.

The likelihood of one site

Take one column of an alignment with four sequences, P, Q, R and S. (These letters avoid confusion with the bases A, C, G, T.) Four sequences have three possible unrooted trees (section 01). Take the tree that joins P with Q and R with S. It has two internal nodes, u and v, which stand for ancestors we never saw.

If we knew the ancestors' bases, the probability of the column would be a simple product: the chance of the base at the start, times the chance of what happened along each of the five branches. We do not know them, so we must add up every possibility. Each of the two nodes could be any of four bases, which gives 4 × 4 = 16 products:

L = ∑u ∑v πv × P(v→u, m) × P(u→P's base, tP) × P(u→Q's base, tQ) × P(v→R's base, tR) × P(v→S's base, tS)

Here πv is the chance of that base at the starting node. P(x→y, t) is the chance that base x has become base y after a branch of length t. Branch lengths are in expected substitutions per site, as in section 03. Under JC69 every base has π = ¼, and the branch chances have a simple form:

same base: ¼ + ¾ e−4t/3    one particular different base: ¼ − ¼ e−4t/3

Sixteen terms is easy. The trouble is growth. Posada and Crandall give the count: for 12 sequences, a rooted tree has 11 internal nodes, so 411 = 4,194,304 products of 23 terms each, for every site.

Felsenstein pruning

Felsenstein's answer was to work from the tips towards the root, one node at a time. Posada and Crandall describe it as computing "in terms of conditional likelihoods, starting from the tips". The conditional likelihood of a node for a base is the probability of everything seen above that node, given that the node had that base. Each node holds four such numbers, one per base.

  1. Tips. A tip has 1 for the base we see and 0 for the other three.
  2. Each internal node, once its neighbours are done. For each base the node might have had: for each neighbour, sum over that neighbour's four bases the chance of the branch change times the neighbour's own number. Then multiply the neighbours' results together.
  3. Finish at the root. Weight the root's four numbers by π and add them up.

This works because a node's four numbers already summarise everything above it, so nothing above it needs to be looked at again. The work now grows in step with the number of nodes, not as a power of four. The method is known as Felsenstein pruning. It gives exactly the same answer as the full sum; it only avoids repeating work. The figure below checks this for whatever you set.

Felsenstein showed that for a reversible model the likelihood is the same wherever the root is placed; he called this the pulley principle. So the method gives an unrooted tree, and the root must come from elsewhere, such as an outgroup (section 01).

Real analyses add one more layer. With gamma rates (section 03), each site's likelihood is calculated once for each rate category, and the results are averaged using the category weights. The pruning itself does not change.

Branch lengths are estimated too

A topology alone has no likelihood. Its branch lengths must be chosen first, and the fair choice is the set that gives that topology its highest likelihood. Finding that set is branch length optimisation.

The smallest case is two sequences joined by one branch, length d. With n sites and k differences under JC69, the log-likelihood has only two kinds of column:

ln L(d) = n ln ¼ + (n − k) ln(¼ + ¾ e−4d/3) + k ln(¼ − ¼ e−4d/3)

The value of d at the top of this curve is the maximum likelihood distance. In this one case there is a formula for it, and it is the JC69 correction you have already met in section 03: d = −¾ ln(1 − 4p/3), with p = k/n. So the correction was a likelihood estimate all along.

A larger tree has no such formula, so programs climb numerically. Felsenstein's method improves one branch at a time while the others stay fixed, then goes round again until nothing improves. RAxML-NG's default for this step is a Newton-Raphson method (--blopt nr_fast), which is a way of climbing a curve using its slope and bend.

Comparing the three trees

Now everything can be put together. For an alignment of four sequences, take each of the three topologies, optimise its five branch lengths, and compare the best ln L each one can reach. This is a complete maximum likelihood analysis, because with four sequences every tree can be tried.

Two things differ from parsimony (section 05). First, every column counts. IQ-TREE's FAQ says that constant sites "are important for the correct estimation of the branch lengths", and should not be thrown away. Delete the constant columns from the example and watch the branch lengths grow. Second, the size of the answer is meaningful. Branch lengths come out of the same calculation as the score, and a change on a long branch costs less than a change on a short one.

The ln L values above are only comparable with each other because the alignment and model are the same. Our reading: never compare ln L between different alignments. Within one alignment, a lead of a few log units from a handful of sites is not yet a statement of confidence; that needs section 07.

One warning from building this figure. Even on a single fixed tree, a short alignment can have more than one peak for its branch lengths. On the Conflict example, some starting points stopped about 2 log units below the best value, because a change could be placed on either of two sister tips. The page therefore starts from five points and keeps the best. Real programs face the same risk, which is one reason for the repeated runs below.

Searching for the tree

With four sequences we simply tried all three trees. With 50 sequences that is impossible (section 01). Finding the best tree is what computer scientists call NP-hard, which means, in plain terms, that no shortcut is known that would guarantee it for large data sets. So, as Nguyen et al. (2015) put it, "one has to rely on search heuristics". A heuristic is a good method with no guarantee.

The basic search is hill climbing. Start from a quick tree, try small changes, keep any change that raises the likelihood, and stop when nothing nearby is better. Two kinds of small change are common:

  • Nearest neighbour interchange (NNI) "swaps two subtrees across an internal branch". Each internal branch offers two such swaps, so a tree of n sequences has 2(n − 3) NNI neighbours (Nguyen et al. 2015). With n = 4 that is 2: from any of the three topologies in the figure above, one NNI reaches either of the other two.
  • Subtree pruning and regrafting (SPR) cuts off a subtree and attaches it to another branch. It reaches many more trees in one move. RAxML-NG's search is built on SPR moves, and its radius limits how far away a subtree may be reinserted.

Hill climbing has a known weakness: it can stop on a lower peak, a local optimum, because every nearby tree is worse even though a better tree exists further away. Nguyen et al. note that this is worse when a move reaches few trees, which is why SPR often beats NNI in plain hill climbing.

The two main programs escape in different ways. IQ-TREE keeps a small set of good trees, randomly disturbs one of them with a few NNIs, and climbs again. It stops when the best tree has not improved after 100 such attempts. RAxML-NG runs complete SPR searches from several different starting trees and keeps the best result. Its tutorial shows why: on one data set, 15 of 20 searches ended near ln L = −9974.67, and 5 ended near −9980.60 on a different tree.

Neither result is guaranteed to be the true best tree. Nguyen et al. found that both programs "still finish sometimes in local optima", and advise repeated runs. Compare the final ln L across runs. If they differ, keep the highest and be more careful with that data set.

Choosing the model

Likelihood also tells us which model to use. Section 03 explained the idea: richer models always fit at least as well, so the fit must be charged for its parameters. These are the formulas used in the IQ-TREE source code, where k is the number of free parameters and n is the number of alignment sites:

AIC = −2 ln L + 2k    BIC = −2 ln L + k ln n

A lower score is better. In IQ-TREE's report, k counts the branches as well as the model parameters, so a tree of 10 sequences starts at 17.

ModelFinder in IQ-TREE computes these scores for many models on one quick tree, a parsimony tree, and keeps the model with the lowest BIC. RAxML-NG 2 has a similar tool called MOOSE, also using BIC by default. In the ModelFinder paper, a BIC difference above 10 is treated as strong evidence against the higher score. Because the default compares models on a single tree, the paper notes that a poor starting tree can lead to a suboptimal model. The -mtree option does a full tree search for each model, at a large cost in time.

Simple explanation with examples: what a maximum likelihood run does, in order
  1. Read the alignment and merge identical columns, since they have the same likelihood.
  2. Choose a model, by you or by BIC.
  3. Build quick starting trees.
  4. For a tree: fit the branch lengths and model values, using pruning to get ln L at each try.
  5. Rearrange the tree with NNI or SPR moves, and keep what improves ln L.
  6. Stop by the program's rule, and report the best tree found.

Step 4 sits inside step 5, so pruning runs a very large number of times. That is why likelihood is slow compared with distance methods, and why these programs spend so much effort on making it fast.

07How much to trust a branch

A tree method always returns a tree, even from poor data. It does not say which of its branches the data really support. This section is about the numbers written on branches: what the bootstrap, the ultrafast bootstrap, SH-aLRT and the transfer bootstrap each count, and what none of them tells you. Read it after maximum likelihood: the faster methods below are built on likelihood trees.

The question, and why we cannot ask it directly

Suppose the tree says that species D and E (letters only, not the species of our projects in earlier lessons) are each other's closest relatives. The honest question is: if I could collect a fresh, equally large set of data from the same animals, would I get D and E together again? I cannot collect fresh data. So the bootstrap does the next best thing: it treats the data I have as a stand-in for all the data I could have collected, and draws new data sets from it.

The bootstrap, step by step (Felsenstein 1985)

The data are an alignment: one row per species, one column per position. Felsenstein argued that the proper way to resample is to keep all the species and to draw the columns with replacement, assuming the columns evolved independently. In his words, the bootstrap "involves resampling points from one's own data, with replacement, to create a series of bootstrap samples of the same size as the original data."

  1. Draw as many columns as the alignment has, one at a time, each time from all original columns. With replacement means a column can come up twice or more, and some never come up.
  2. Build a tree from the new alignment, with exactly the same method you used for the real one.
  3. Repeat hundreds or thousands of times. Each new alignment, with its tree, is called a replicate.
  4. Count. For every branch of your real tree, write down the percentage of replicates whose tree contains the same branch. That percentage is the bootstrap support. To compare branches between trees we describe each branch by the split it makes: the two groups of species it separates.

Why about 63 % of the columns appear

An alignment has n columns. In one draw a given column is not picked with probability 1 − 1/n. It is missed in all n draws with probability (1 − 1/n)n, which for large n approaches 1/e ≈ 0.368. So the expected share of original columns that appear at least once is 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632. For 30 columns the exact value is 1 − (29/30)30 = 0.638. The readout under the toy shows your own average next to this figure. This is ordinary probability, not a claim from a paper. It explains why a replicate is a thinner, noisier copy of the data, and why a branch supported by only one or two columns is easily lost.

What the number means, and what it does not

The suggested reading. Felsenstein proposed that "if a group shows up 95% of the time or more, the evidence for it is taken to be statistically significant." That is the origin of the popular 95 % rule.

A later check. Hillis and Bull (1993) tested bootstrap values on simulated data with a known true tree. In their abstract (we read the abstract only), bootstrap proportions are "biased but highly conservative" estimates of how likely a clade is to be real: under their conditions (equal rates of change, symmetric trees, few changes on each internal branch) a value of 70 % or more usually corresponded to a probability of 95 % or more that the clade is real. They also warn that under very high rates of change, or very unequal rates between lineages, values above 50 % can overestimate accuracy.

So the number is best read as a measure of repeatability: how stable the answer is when the columns are reshuffled and reweighted. It is not a probability that the branch is true, because:

  • It only sees random sampling error. If the method or the model is wrong in a consistent way, every replicate is wrong in the same way and the support can be high for a false branch. The long-branch attraction of the parsimony section is the standard example. A bootstrap cannot fix a biased method.
  • It assumes independent columns. Neighbouring sites in a gene, or the three sites of a codon, are not independent. The bootstrap treats them as if they were (Felsenstein states this as his assumption).
  • It depends on the alignment. A branch can be stable over columns and still come from a poor alignment.
  • It judges one branch at a time. A tree whose every branch has 70 % support is not 70 % right as a whole.

Ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot)

The ordinary bootstrap needs a full tree search on every replicate, which becomes slow for large data. Minh, Nguyen and von Haeseler (2013) built UFBoot to compute the support of groups in likelihood trees much faster. By their abstract it combines the "resampling estimated log-likelihood method" with a "simple but effective collection scheme of candidate trees", and adds a stopping rule that decides when the support values have converged. They report a median speed-up of 3.1 to 10.2 times over the RAxML rapid bootstrap on real data. In their simulations the values "appear to be relatively unbiased compared with the conservative standard bootstrap", which they say "provides a more direct interpretation of the bootstrap support".

That last point is the catch. A UFBoot percentage and a standard bootstrap percentage are not the same kind of number. The IQ-TREE FAQ says so directly: UFBoot has "a different meaning than the normal (more conservative) bootstrap supports", and "you should not compare BS% with UFBoot% directly".

Threshold, as the program's authors give it. The IQ-TREE FAQ: "For UFBoot, you should only start to rely on a branch if its support is ≥ 95%". The UFBoot2 paper (Hoang et al. 2018) gives the same figure for data with "no or mild" model violations. Under severe violations UFBoot2 "overestimated the branch supports". For that case the paper recommends an extra nearest-neighbour-interchange step on each replicate, which IQ-TREE switches on with -bnni. That is the authors' recommendation, not a guarantee: it reduces the risk of over-estimating, it does not repair a wrong model.

SH-aLRT

SH-aLRT (Guindon et al. 2010) is not a count of resampled trees. It is a test, based on likelihood ratios, of how well the data support one internal branch; the abstract calls it "a nonparametric, Shimodaira-Hasegawa-like procedure" that extends the earlier approximate likelihood-ratio test. The calculation itself is worked through, from the IQ-TREE source code, in section 12. IQ-TREE prints it next to UFBoot as "SH-aLRT support (%) / ultrafast bootstrap support (%)", for example 92.5/98. Its FAQ gives a joint rule: "One would be more confident if a clade has its SH-aLRT ≥ 80% and UFboot ≥ 95%."

Comparing whole trees: KH, SH and AU

Support values judge one branch. Sometimes the question is about a whole tree: a published tree, or a group you expected, is not in your best tree. Is it significantly worse, or just slightly worse? Three tests answer this from the per-column likelihoods. The IQ-TREE manual sums up their history: the KH test "was designed to test 2 trees and thus has no correction for multiple testing"; the SH test "fixes this problem", but "becomes too conservative (i.e., rejecting fewer trees than expected) when testing many trees"; and the AU test (Shimodaira 2002) addresses that. The abstract of that paper warns that "comparing many trees at the same time ... often leads to overconfidence in the wrong trees". A tree with p below 0.05 is rejected, and IQ-TREE marks it with a minus sign. The file TREES_FILE holds the trees to compare, one per line. The manual recommends at least 1,000 draws, here called RELL replicates. You can run all three tests on a small alignment in section 12, under "Comparing whole trees".

iqtree3 -s ALN_FILE -z TREES_FILE -n 0 -zb 10000 -au

Transfer bootstrap (TBE)

The classical (Felsenstein) bootstrap proportion, FBP, counts a replicate only if it contains the identical split. Lemoine et al. (2018) point out that for large trees this is harsh: "A replicated branch must match a reference branch exactly", and a difference of just one taxon is enough to make a nearly identical branch count as absent. As a result the FBP "tends to yield very low supports, especially on deep branches".

Their alternative, the transfer bootstrap expectation (TBE), measures how many taxa you would have to move ("transfer") from one side of the branch to the other to make a branch of the replicate tree identical to the reference branch. The formula in the paper is TBE(b) = 1 − (average transfer index) / (p − 1), where p is the number of taxa on the smaller side of branch b. It runs from 0 to 1, and the authors write that "TBE supports are easily interpreted as fractions of unstable taxa".

Worked example of the formula (ours, not from the paper) Reference branch: {1,2,3,4,5} | the other taxa, so p = 5. In every replicate taxon 5 wanders off, but 1-4 stay together. FBP: the split is never identical -> 0 % Transfer index: moving 1 taxon repairs it -> 1 TBE = 1 - 1/(5-1) = 0.75 -> 75 %

What the authors say about reading it. It "should not be interpreted as the confidence level of some statistical test" and "cannot be interpreted as the probability for the branch b to belong to the true phylogeny". On a cut-off: "our results suggest that 70% is a reasonable threshold to start with", but "it is better for users to interpret the TBE values depending on the data". TBE is a different quantity from FBP and from UFBoot, so a TBE of 80 % and an FBP of 80 % do not mean the same.

Simple explanation with examples: why shuffle the columns?

Imagine the alignment as a deck of 30 cards, one per column, and the tree as the story the whole deck tells. To test how much the story depends on particular cards, draw 30 cards from the deck, putting each back after you look at it, and read the story again. If the story barely changes whichever cards you happen to draw, many cards say the same thing: the branch is stable. If a branch appears only when one particular card is drawn, it rested on that card.

Example: if 10 columns support AB | CDEF and none support a rival, almost every draw of 30 contains at least one of the 10 columns, so the split comes back almost every time. If only 2 columns support a split, a draw can easily miss both, and the split vanishes from many replicates.

Simple explanation with examples: 95 % of what?

"This branch has 95 % support" is easy to read as "there is a 95 % chance this branch is real". It does not say that. It says: in 95 of 100 resampled data sets, the same method found this branch. If the method is biased towards a wrong answer, it will find the wrong branch in 95 of 100 resampled data sets too. Think of a bathroom scale that reads 2 kg too heavy: weighing yourself 100 times gives the same number 100 times, the repeatability is perfect, and the number is wrong.

08Bayesian inference

Maximum likelihood (s06) looks for the tree that makes the data most probable, and the bootstrap (s07) tests how stable that tree is. Bayesian inference asks a different question: given the data, a model and what we assumed beforehand, how probable is each tree? This section builds the answer from zero: first a coin, then one distance, then three trees.

Three words: prior, likelihood, posterior

The Bayesian method uses probability to describe everything we do not know, including the values in the model (Nascimento et al. 2017). Three quantities do all the work:

Prior
What you believe about the unknown value before seeing the data, written as probabilities.
Likelihood
The probability of the data if a given value were true. This is the same quantity as in s06.
Posterior
What you should believe after seeing the data. It combines the prior and the likelihood.

The rule that joins them is Bayes's theorem:

posterior of a value = prior × likelihood ÷ (sum of prior × likelihood over all possible values)

The sum on the bottom is called the marginal likelihood. It is one number, the same for every value, and its only job here is to make the posterior probabilities add up to 1. So the posterior is proportional to prior × likelihood.

A coin, worked by hand

A coin is one of three kinds: it lands heads 30 %, 50 % or 70 % of the time. We toss it 10 times and get 7 heads. Each row below takes the likelihood of "7 heads in 10", multiplies it by the prior, and divides by the sum of those products. The table does this for two priors. The first has no preference. The second is sceptical: it gives 80 % to the fair coin, since most coins are fair.

Simple explanation with examples: the same logic in the field

You hear a call at night that could be one of two bat species. The call fits species X slightly better. But species Y is a hundred times more common at this site. Your final belief is the fit of the call (likelihood) weighed by how common each species is (prior). One unclear call leaves you with Y. Twenty clear calls that fit X would change your mind.

Nothing here is special to bats or coins. A prior is written down, the data are weighed, and the result is a new statement of belief that can be checked and argued with.

Why a chain is needed

For the coin the bottom sum had three terms. For one distance between two sequences we can still do it: try thousands of evenly spaced values on a grid and add them up. For a tree we cannot. The sum runs over every possible tree (s01 shows how fast their number grows) and, for each tree, over all branch lengths and model values together. It "may be too expensive to compute" (Nascimento et al. 2017).

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) avoids the sum. Instead of calculating the posterior, it draws a sample from it, and the sample shows us its shape. "Monte Carlo" means the method uses random numbers. "Markov chain" means each step depends only on where the chain is now, not on where it has been. The usual recipe is the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm:

  1. Start at any value.
  2. Propose a new value near the current one. Here we use a sliding window: a random value from a window of fixed width centred on the current value.
  3. Compare. Calculate R = (prior × likelihood at the new value) ÷ (prior × likelihood at the current value). The bottom sum of Bayes's theorem cancels in this ratio, so it is never needed.
  4. Decide. If R is 1 or more, move. If R is below 1, move with probability R. If the move fails, stay, and count the current value again.
  5. Repeat many times, and record the value every so often.

The chain visits each value in proportion to its posterior probability (Nascimento et al. 2017), so a histogram of the visited values is an estimate of the posterior. A sliding window is symmetrical, so step 3 needs no correction.

Simple explanation with examples: a walker on a hill in fog

Think of the posterior as a hill. The walker sees only the ground under their feet and the ground one step away. A step uphill is always taken. A step downhill is taken only sometimes, and a steep one rarely. So the walker wanders over the hill, but stays longest on the high ground.

Now count how many times the walker stood at each spot. That count is a map of the height of the hill, drawn without ever seeing it from outside. The downhill steps matter: a walker who only climbed would stop at the top, which gives one best value (like maximum likelihood) and no picture of the uncertainty.

Burn-in, mixing and convergence

Two different things can go wrong, and the walker shows both (Nascimento et al. 2017):

Convergence
How quickly a chain gets from its starting point to the high part of the posterior. After that it is said to be stationary: it samples the posterior and no longer drifts.
Burn-in
The early samples, from before the chain became stationary. They are thrown away.
Mixing
How efficiently the chain moves around the posterior once it is there.

The step size controls mixing. If the steps are too large, most proposals land in the tails and are rejected. If they are too small, almost all are accepted but the chain hardly goes anywhere. For a move that changes a number, Nascimento et al. (2017) put the best acceptance "at about 30-40%", and the RevBayes guide gives common recommendations of 0.2 to 0.4. Most current programs tune their step sizes automatically, so this is rarely your job (Nascimento et al. 2017).

From one number to a tree

In a tree analysis the chain's position is a whole tree: its topology, its branch lengths and the values of the substitution model. Moves that change a number work as above. Moves that change the topology use the branch swaps of the tree search in s06: NNI, SPR or TBR (Nascimento et al. 2017).

The output is a long list of sampled trees. To read it, you count. The posterior probability of a tree is the share of samples that are that tree, and the posterior probability of a clade is the share of samples that contain it.

Posterior probability against bootstrap

The toy shows a pattern that is well known from real comparisons: posterior probabilities tend to be higher than bootstrap values for the same branch. They are not two versions of one number.

Posterior probability
The probability that the clade is correct, given the data, the model and the prior (Nascimento et al. 2017). It is only as good as those assumptions.
Bootstrap
How often the clade comes back when the sites are resampled (s07). It is a measure of stability, not a probability that the clade is true.

Simulation studies give a consistent picture. In Erixon et al. (2003), posterior probabilities were "significantly higher" than bootstrap frequencies for true clades, "but also ... erroneous conclusions will be made more often", and those errors were "strongly accentuated" when the model was too simple. Alfaro et al. (2003) found the two often strongly correlated, but substantially different on short branches, and found that posterior probabilities gave high support to correct groups with fewer characters. Douady et al. (2003) concluded that the two are "surely not interchangeable and cannot be directly compared". A posterior of 0.95 and a bootstrap of 95 % therefore do not mean the same thing. Report which one you used.

There is also a known hazard on very short branches. When the true tree is a star, posterior probabilities for the three resolved trees do not settle at 1/3 even with unlimited data; they vary between data sets (Yang 2007). This star-tree paradox reflects "high and presumably spurious posterior probabilities", and they are "very sensitive to the prior". A high posterior on a very short branch deserves a second look.

Simple explanation with examples: two different questions

Take a clade with bootstrap 70 % and posterior 0.98. The bootstrap says: shake the data, and it falls apart in 3 of 10 trials. The posterior says: if this model and prior are right, this clade is very likely true. Both can hold at once. The word "if" carries the weight. With a poor model, the posterior can be confidently wrong.

Choosing and checking priors

The prior is part of your analysis, whether you chose it or not. "Default priors in many Bayesian software packages may not be appropriate" and "specification of the prior is the biologist's responsibility" (Nascimento et al. 2017). The same paper warns that independent exponential or uniform priors on branch lengths can produce very long trees from highly similar sequences. Three checks are cheap:

  1. Run without data. Some programs can sample from the prior alone. Compare the result with the prior you meant to set. This is the "No data" button above: it should return the prior, and it does.
  2. Compare prior and posterior. If they look alike, the data say little about that value. If they barely overlap, the prior and the data may conflict.
  3. Change the prior and run again. If the conclusion changes, say so. But never tune the prior to match the posterior: a prior states what you knew before the analysis.

09Dating trees

The trees of s06 and s08 have branch lengths in substitutions per site. A zoologist usually wants millions of years: when did these two species split? Sequences alone cannot answer that. This section shows why, what a clock model adds, how an outside date (a calibration) turns the tree into a dated tree, and how much that one choice controls every age in it.

Why rates and times cannot be separated

A branch length is a product:

branch length = rate × time

The branch length is in substitutions per site, the rate in substitutions per site per million years, and the time in million years (My). A branch of 0.01 can be a rate of 0.001 acting for 10 My, or a rate of 0.002 acting for 5 My. Both give the same expected amount of change, so no amount of sequence can tell them apart. Drummond et al. (2006) say it directly: "the individual contributions of rate and time to molecular evolution cannot be separated". Even under a perfect clock, "the divergence times and the overall substitution rate can only be separately estimated if there is a source of external calibration information". Heath et al. (2014) put it more shortly: "molecular sequence data are not informative on absolute time".

That outside information is a calibration. Drummond et al. (2006) list three kinds:

  1. An age for an internal node, usually from a fossil. This is the usual case for animals.
  2. Known ages of the sequences at the tips. This works when samples were taken at clearly different times, as with fast RNA viruses.
  3. A rate taken from an earlier, independent study. It can be fixed or given a range. This is the same kind of step as using a mutation rate to turn a PSMC curve into years (lesson 4, s04).

With no calibration at all, you can still get relative ages: this node is three times older than that one (Heath, FBD tutorial). If the rate is simply fixed at 1, time is measured in substitutions per site (Drummond et al. 2006).

Simple explanation with examples: a trail with no clock

You follow a wolf's trail for 30 km. Did it walk for 3 hours at 10 km/h, or for 6 hours at 5 km/h? The trail looks the same. The length of the trail is the branch length, and speed and time are the rate and the age.

Now one camera trap on the trail gives a time stamp: the wolf passed the 10 km mark 2 hours after it set out. That gives a speed of 5 km/h. If the wolf kept the same speed, you can also time every other point on the trail. The time stamp is the calibration, and "kept the same speed" is the clock model.

Invented data for four invented species, P, Q, R and S, under a strict clock. The tool reads the distances below and takes each node's depth as half the average distance between its two groups. Worked by hand:

Depth is half the distance because a distance between two species counts the changes on both lines of descent, and each line has run for the whole time since the split.

Clock models: strict and relaxed

The tool used a strict clock: one rate on every branch, which is why one calibration fixed everything. It is the same assumption that UPGMA makes (s04). Dating began this way, with a constant clock whose rate was set from the fossil record (Ronquist et al. 2012, citing Zuckerkandl and Pauling 1962). But rates differ between lineages, and "it is now standard practice to accommodate such rate variation using relaxed-clock models" (Ronquist et al. 2012).

A small example of the damage. In the toy, suppose the true age of P+Q is 10 My and the rate is 0.001, but the line to P ran twice as fast. Then the branch to P is 0.02, the branch to Q is 0.01, and their distance is 0.03. Half of that is 0.015, and a strict clock with the same rate reads 15 My instead of 10.

A relaxed clock lets the rate differ between branches. To separate rate and time on each branch, the method then needs a model of how rates vary (Heath, FBD tutorial):

Clock modelWhat it assumes
StrictOne rate for the whole tree.
Local clocksA separate rate for each group of branches that you name yourself.
AutocorrelatedA branch's rate is drawn from a distribution whose mean depends on the rate of its parent branch, so relatives have similar rates.
UncorrelatedEach branch's rate is drawn independently from one shared distribution, such as a lognormal (UCLN) or an exponential (UCED). A branch's rate says nothing about its neighbour's.

Definitions from Drummond et al. (2006).

In their simulations, Drummond et al. (2006) found that when the data did not follow a clock, a strict-clock analysis "did extremely poorly": the true rates fell inside its 95 % HPD only 3 to 11 % of the time. The uncorrelated models "also perform well when the data are clocklike". The price of relaxing is precision: on their real data sets, the strict clock always gave the most precise tree estimates. These figures come from one study, not a universal rule.

A calibration is a prior, not a date

A fossil does not date a split. It shows that the lineage already existed when the animal died. If the fossil truly belongs to the group, it gives "a reliable minimum age bound", while "maximum bounds are far more difficult to come by" (Heath, FBD tutorial). The fossil probably sits on a side branch of unknown length, not on the node itself (Ronquist et al. 2012). So point calibrations taken from the oldest fossils "are likely to be substantial underestimates" (Drummond et al. 2006).

Bayesian dating therefore states a prior on the node's age, called a calibration density. The common shapes (Heath, FBD tutorial; Barba-Montoya et al. 2017):

Uniform
Equal probability between a minimum and a maximum. In BEAST2 and MrBayes these are hard bounds: ages outside have zero probability. Soft bounds leave a small probability outside.
Exponential, offset
Starts at the fossil age, with most weight just above it. Useful "if the fossil age is very close to the age of its ancestral node".
Lognormal, offset
Starts at the fossil age, with "the highest probability on ages somewhat older than the fossil", and a long tail.
Normal
Symmetrical around a mean. "Not always appropriate" for fossils, but useful for a geological date or for an age taken from an earlier study.

Offset means the curve starts at the fossil age, so it describes the time that passed between the split and the fossil.

Try itChange the calibration prior and watch the ages move

The toy tree from the first tool, with a strict clock and sequences so long that the branch lengths are known exactly. All the uncertainty left is the calibration. The three curves have the same mean gap: the uniform runs from the fossil to fossil + 2 × gap, and the lognormal has σ = 0.6 (our choice). Real analyses also have uncertain rates and limited sequence, so they do not give simple copies like this.

The toy has perfect sequence data and the ages are still uncertain. That is the general point: fossil calibrations are "expected to have a major impact on divergence time estimates even if a huge amount of molecular data is available" (Barba-Montoya et al. 2017). A narrow result is no proof either: conflict between calibrations and branch lengths can give ages that are "grossly incorrect, yet have a narrow posterior distribution" (Rannala 2016).

The prior you type is not the prior you get. Calibration densities "do not represent the marginal prior distribution of the calibration node" (Heled and Drummond 2012). The marginal prior is the prior of that one node once all the other priors have acted on it. In BEAST2 and MrBayes the density you specify is multiplied with the tree prior, and ancestors must also stay older than descendants, so "the effective priors on the calibration node ages after the truncation can be very different from the user-specified calibration densities" (Barba-Montoya et al. 2017). Always look at the prior the program really uses: run it once without the sequence data.

The fossil itself needs a justification. Parham et al. (2012) give a checklist of five points for any fossil calibration:

  1. Museum numbers of the specimens.
  2. A diagnosis based on derived characters, or an up-to-date phylogenetic analysis that includes the specimens.
  3. A statement on how morphological and molecular data sets were reconciled.
  4. The locality and the stratigraphic level.
  5. A published radioisotopic age or numeric timescale, and how the number was chosen.

For a minimum, they say to use the youngest possible age of the fossil, not the midpoint of the interval.

Fossils at the nodes, or fossils at the tips

Node dating is everything above: turn a fossil into a prior on a node. Ronquist et al. (2012) list its weaknesses. The calibration must be tied to a node, although no node is known with certainty. Only the oldest fossil of a group is used, so "one thus often ends up discarding most of the information preserved in the fossil record". And the step from fossil to curve is hard to justify: Parham et al. (2012) note that "there is presently no practical way to estimate curve parameters".

Tip dating puts the fossils into the tree as tips with their own ages. There are two forms:

Total-evidence dating
Fossils and living species are analysed together, with morphological characters for the fossils and sequences for the living species. The morphology places each fossil and informs the length of its side branch, so no node has to be fixed (Ronquist et al. 2012). In their study of Hymenoptera, the ages were less sensitive to the priors and tended to be more precise than with node dating.
Fossilized birth-death (FBD) model
A tree prior that treats living species and fossils as products of one process, with rates of speciation, extinction and fossil recovery and a sampling probability for living species. Under it "arbitrary calibration densities are not necessary", and all reliable fossils can be used (Heath et al. 2014). A fossil may also be a direct ancestor of a later sample (a sampled ancestor). The model works without morphological characters too, but you then need some prior knowledge of relationships (Heath, FBD tutorial).

Tip dating has its own traps. Ronquist et al. (2016) found that under some conditions total-evidence ages are "pushed back to very old strata" and very imprecise, which they call deep root attraction. It occurs "when there are model errors or conflicts in the data". And FBD is meant for fossil occurrences: it is "not suitable for dating trees using biogeographical information or secondary calibration dates" (Heath, FBD tutorial). A secondary calibration is an age estimated in an earlier study.

BEAST2 as used for dating

BEAST2 (Bouckaert et al. 2019) estimates the tree, the rates and the ages together by MCMC (s08). Because the tree itself can change during the run, you do not calibrate a fixed node: you give a prior to the most recent common ancestor of a named set of species (Drummond et al. 2006). It is the same program that draws skyline plots (lesson 5, s07). The usual path, from the two tutorials listed below:

  1. BEAUti, the setup program, reads the alignment and writes an XML file for BEAST. Set the substitution model (s03).
  2. Clock Model. The default is Strict Clock. The FBD tutorial uses the Optimized Relaxed Clock from the ORC package, an uncorrelated lognormal clock.
  3. Tree prior. Calibrated Yule in the node-dating tutorial, Fossilized Birth Death Model (SA package) in the FBD tutorial. This choice matters: "because the rate and time are confounded in the branch-length parameter, the prior describing the branching times can have a strong effect on divergence time estimation" (Heath, FBD tutorial).
  4. Calibration. For node dating, add a taxon set, tick Monophyletic (the tutorial requires it with Calibrated Yule), and choose a distribution for the group's common ancestor. The tutorial's human and chimpanzee example is a lognormal with M = 1.78 and S = 0.085: centred near 6 My, with a central 95 % range of 5 to 7 My.
  5. Run with Sample From Prior first. This box on the MCMC panel makes BEAST ignore the sequence likelihood. Fossil occurrence times still count. Compare the resulting ages with the curves you meant to give.
  6. Run with the sequences, then check the run in Tracer: look at the ESS and the 95 % HPD of each age.
  7. TreeAnnotator summarises the sampled trees, for example as a maximum clade credibility tree with median node heights. FigTree draws it.

The FBD tutorial is written for BEAST v2.7.x (latest release when this was written: v2.7.8). The node-dating tutorial was written for version 2.2, so its menus may differ from current BEAUti.

Faster methods for a tree you already have

Bayesian programs struggle with trees of thousands of tips (To et al. 2016). Two other approaches take a finished tree with branch lengths and fit ages to it:

Least-squares dating (LSD)
To et al. (2016) model each branch length as rate × time plus noise, and find the rate and ages that fit best. Input is a tree and the sampling dates of the tips, so it is built for samples taken through time, such as viruses. On simulated data its accuracy was similar to the most sophisticated methods at much less computing time, and the model is robust to uncorrelated departures from a clock.
Penalised likelihood
Sanderson (2002) gives every lineage its own rate, then adds a "roughness penalty that discourages rates from varying too much across a phylogeny". Cross-validation chooses how strong the penalty is. We read only the abstract of this paper.

10Gene trees and species trees

So far each method gave one tree from one alignment. But a tree built from one gene is the history of that gene. The species have their own history, and the two can honestly differ, even when every step of the analysis was right. This section explains why that happens, how often to expect it, and what to do when you have many genes that disagree.

Two different things called a tree

Species tree
The order in which populations split into separate species. There is one such history for a group of species.
Gene tree
The history of the copies of one stretch of DNA, traced back until they meet in common ancestors. There are as many gene trees as there are independent stretches of DNA.
Locus
One stretch of DNA, for example a gene (plural: loci). In this section "gene" and "locus" mean the same thing, as they do in the ASTRAL guide (Mirarab 2019): a stretch with no recombination inside it. Recombination cuts a genome into many such stretches, each with its own history (lesson 4, section 01).
Discordance
Disagreement: a gene tree has a different branching order from the species tree, or from another gene tree.

Discordance is not rare. In the gorilla genome paper, Scally et al. (2012) report that "in 30% of the genome, gorilla is closer to human or chimpanzee than the latter are to each other". The species tree says human and chimpanzee are each other's closest relatives, yet nearly a third of the genome tells a different story. The evolutionary history of a gene can differ from that of its species for several reasons: "incomplete lineage sorting (ILS), duplication and loss (DupLoss for short), gene transfer, and hybridization" (Zhang et al. 2020). We take them in turn.

Incomplete lineage sorting: the coalescent inside a species tree

Think of the species tree as a set of tubes, and of gene lines as threads running inside the tubes. Lesson 4, section 01 showed the rule that moves the threads: follow gene copies back in time, and two of them meet in a common ancestor by chance. In a population of N animals, which holds 2N gene copies, two lines wait 2N generations on average. That meeting is called coalescence.

Take three species, with the species tree ((A,B),C): A and B are sisters. Sample one gene copy from each. The lines from A and B cannot meet while they are in separate species. They can first meet in the common ancestor of A and B, which lives only for the length of the internal branch. There are two cases.

  1. A and B meet inside the internal branch. The gene tree is ((A,B),C), the same as the species tree.
  2. A and B do not meet in time. All three lines reach the ancestor of all three species. There, any two can meet first, and each pair has the same chance, one in three. So one time in three the gene tree still matches, by luck. Two times in three it disagrees.

The second case is incomplete lineage sorting (ILS): the gene lines did not sort into the species in time. The variation was already there in the ancestor, and it outlived a speciation.

The length of the internal branch only matters compared with the wait for two lines to meet. So we measure it in coalescent units: one unit is 2N generations, where N is the size of the population on that branch. For a branch of t generations:

T = t / (2N)
share of genes that match the species tree = 1 − (2/3) e−T
share of each of the two other gene trees = (1/3) e−T

Here e−T is the chance that A and B do not meet in T units. Degnan and Rosenberg (2006) give this result for the three-species tree. Their N counts gene copies, so their one unit is N generations; ours counts animals, so it is 2N.

Try this. (1) Set the branch to 0: all three gene trees are equally common, and the data cannot tell which is right. (2) Compare 10,000 generations with 10,000 animals against 20,000 generations with 20,000 animals: T and the shares stay the same, because only the ratio counts. (3) Keep the branch fixed and raise the population size: discordance grows. A short speciation interval and a large ancestral population do the same thing. (4) At any setting, the two wrong trees stay about equally common, and each is about as common as the orange part. That symmetry is the fingerprint of ILS.

Control. The simulation above does not use the formula. We ran its code for 2 million genes at each of eight settings, and separately ran a plain population model in which every line draws a random parent each generation. All matched the formula within chance (details in this section's notes).

Back to the apes. If a share of 30 % disagreeing genes came from ILS alone, the formula would give T of about 0.8 (our calculation, from 2/3 e−T = 0.3). Scally et al. (2012) also found "no significant difference" between the number of bases sorting as ((H,G),C) and as ((C,G),H), with H for human, C for chimpanzee and G for gorilla. That is the symmetry you just saw. They also found that the share varies along the genome and is lower around coding genes, so selection plays a part that this simple model leaves out.

Simple explanation with examples: why a gene can be closer to the "wrong" species

Imagine an ancestral species with two forms of a gene, form 1 and form 2. It splits into C and the ancestor of A and B. Both daughters inherit both forms. Soon the ancestor of A and B also splits, before either form has been lost. Now drift removes forms at random: A keeps form 1, B keeps form 2, C keeps form 1. At this gene A and C look like sisters, because their copies come from the same form. Nothing went wrong: the gene correctly records its own history.

A long internal branch gives drift time to remove one of the forms before the next split. A large population slows drift down. That is why T is a ratio of the two.

Worked numbers: a branch of 40,000 generations in a population of 10,000 animals gives T = 2, and about 91 % of genes match. A branch of 4,000 generations in the same population gives T = 0.2, and only about 45 % match, so more than half of the genes disagree with the species tree, although the species tree is still the most common single answer.

Gene flow and paralogy: two causes that break the symmetry

Gene flow between species, through hybrids that back-cross into a parent species, is called introgression. It moves real pieces of genome between branches, so those pieces have a different history. Unlike ILS it has a direction: "one discordant tree topology can become more common than the other", and this asymmetry "is one of the clearest signals of introgression" (Hibbins and Hahn 2022). This holds for gene flow between species that are not sisters. If your counts show ((B,C),A) clearly more often than ((A,C),B), ILS in one well-mixed ancestral population does not explain them.

Paralogy is a different kind of problem. Orthologs trace back to a speciation, and paralogs to a duplication (lesson 3, section 07). If you compare paralogs by mistake, the tree dates a duplication, not a speciation. This is why the characters must be checked first (section 02). Different losses in different species can leave each species with a single copy, so the gene looks safe although it is not. The diagram shows this case. ASTRAL-Pro (Zhang et al. 2020) takes gene family trees with several copies per species, so paralogs need not be thrown away.

Gene tree errors can look like all of these. A short alignment gives a poorly estimated tree, and that discordance is noise, not history. Keep this in mind for the rest of the section.

ABBA-BABA: testing for gene flow from the columns

The asymmetry above can be tested without building a single gene tree. The D statistic, also called the ABBA-BABA test, counts columns of an alignment (Green et al. 2010; Durand et al. 2011). Take four genomes: two sister species P1 and P2, a third species P3, and an outgroup O. The base in the outgroup is called A, for ancestral, and a changed base B, for derived. Write a column in the order P1, P2, P3, O, and three patterns matter:

BBAA
P1 and P2 share the change. This fits the species tree.
ABBA
P2 and P3 share the change, and P1 does not. Without repeat changes this needs a gene tree that does not match the species tree, with the change on a branch shared by P2 and P3 (Durand et al. 2011).
BABA
P1 and P3 share the change, and P2 does not. This needs the other discordant gene tree.

Under ILS alone the two discordant gene trees are equally common, as you saw in the figure above, so ABBA and BABA columns should be about equally common too. Gene flow between P3 and one of the sisters tips the balance:

D = (ABBA − BABA) / (ABBA + BABA)

D is 0 if the two are equal. In this order, D below 0 points to P3 exchanging genes with P1, and D above 0 to P3 exchanging genes with P2 (Durand et al. 2011). The sign says who was involved, not who gave and who received: "it can only identify which taxa are involved, and not the donor and recipient populations" (Hibbins and Hahn 2022). Beware of another trap: Patterson et al. (2012) define D with BABA first, so the sign can flip between papers unless the order of species is matched.

How far from 0 is far enough? Neighbouring columns share a history, so they are not independent, and "a simple binomial test to assess whether D significantly differs from zero is not appropriate" (Durand et al. 2011). The usual answer is the block jackknife. Cut the genome into blocks longer than the stretches that share a history, remove one block at a time, and recompute D. The spread of these values gives a standard error, and Z = D / standard error says how many standard errors D lies from 0. Zheng and Janke (2018) counted Z above 3 as significant, and Dsuite's authors remind users to correct for testing many trios.

Try this. (1) Choose No gene flow and repeat 200 genomes: the jackknife Z stays within ±3 almost every time, but the plain binomial test raises a false alarm about one time in ten. (2) Choose P3 into P2, then P3 into P1: D changes sign. At 10 % a genome still misses the signal about three times in ten. (3) Choose P1 into P2: genes moved, and D stays at 0, because "gene flow between P1 and P2 ... is not expected to produce a deviation" (Durand et al. 2011, shortened). (4) Choose No gene flow and tick the box: D is far from 0 with a large Z, and there was no gene flow at all.

Control. With few mutations the simulated D agrees with the formula of Durand et al. (2011): 0.190 against 0.187 at 10 % gene flow. Standard errors equal those of the package scikit-allel. In 400 genomes without gene flow, |Z| passed 3 in 0.3 % with the jackknife and 11 % with the binomial test. Details are in this section's notes.

The last case needs explaining, because a test can be right about the columns and wrong about the cause. D assumes that each site changed once and that rates are equal. When rates differ, "homoplasies are more likely to occur in the sister species with the higher rate", and D can falsely support gene flow (Koppetsch et al. 2024). In that study the test was significant "for all datasets simulated without introgression (m=0) whenever rate variation was present". The risk is higher in older groups. In our toy a faster P2 makes D negative, and the effect shrinks if the outgroup is brought close, so we read it as the fast species picking up chance matches with the outgroup. Other limits:

  • Ancestral structure. If the ancestral population was not randomly mating, one discordant tree can be more common without any gene flow. "D statistics do not allow us to distinguish between the models of admixture and ancestral subdivision" (Durand et al. 2011).
  • Unsampled species. Genes from a species you did not sample can also produce a signal (Durand et al. 2011).
  • Population size. The test is less sensitive when populations are large compared with the time since the split (Zheng and Janke 2018).
  • D is not an amount. It rises with gene flow, but "it is not a good estimator" of the share of the genome that moved (Hibbins and Hahn 2022). The f4-ratio is used for that. D also fails in small windows, where it "is highly stochastic", and fd was proposed for scanning a genome (Martin et al. 2015).

Dsuite runs this on all trios of species in a VCF file (Malinsky et al. 2021). Its default is 20 jackknife blocks. The sets file has two columns, sample and species, with the word Outgroup in place of a species for the outgroup samples.

# D, Z, p-value and f4-ratio for all trios
Dsuite Dtrios -t TREE_FILE.nwk INPUT_FILE.vcf SETS.txt
# assign the signal to branches of the tree; the second file is the _tree.txt output of Dtrios
Dsuite Fbranch TREE_FILE.nwk FVALS_tree.txt
Simple explanation with examples: why ABBA and BABA should balance

Think of P1 and P2 as twins, and of P3 as a cousin. A family quirk that the cousin shares with only one twin must come from old variation in the family, and chance gives it to either twin equally often. So the cousin should match each twin in the same number of quirks.

Now suppose the cousin matches the second twin in 1,120 quirks and the first in 880. D = (1,120 − 880) / 2,000 = 0.12. Something joined the cousin and the second twin after the twins separated, or something else broke the balance, for example if one twin simply collects quirks faster.

The same question with SINE insertions: the KKSC test

Insertions of retroposons can answer the question that ABBA-BABA asks of bases. A shared insertion is a rare event, so it is a clean character (section 05; lesson 7, section 05). Take three lineages and count the insertions shared by each pair. That gives three numbers, for example 8:2:0. Kuritzin et al. (2016) read them with two questions, each an exact binomial test:

  1. Is the largest count more than a third? With no resolved tree, each pair is expected to get one third of the insertions. If the largest count is well above that, the data support a tree.
  2. Are the two smaller counts equal? Under ILS the two wrong pairs should be about equal, the symmetry you saw above. If they differ clearly, ILS alone does not explain them, and the authors point to ancestral hybridization.

Try this. (1) Type 3, 0, 0, then 2, 0, 0: the authors give three conflict-free markers as the smallest number for significant support (when the markers were found from at least two of the lineages). (2) Start from 8:2:0 and move the 2 up: the tree weakens. (3) Compare 25:22:21 with 101:11:0: the first is what a near-simultaneous split looks like, the second is lopsided. (4) Scale 8:2:0 to 80:20:0: the same proportions, but now the two smaller counts differ clearly.

Control. The toy reproduces the values printed in the paper: 0.0034 for 8:2:0; 0.293 and 0.5811 for 9:8:5; 0.111 for 3:1:0; p below 3.3 × 10−21 for 192:74:60.

The test rests on assumptions: the insertions are truly at the same place, presence is the derived state, and parallel insertions and exact deletions are rare. Its model has three lineages, so a larger tree is taken three at a time.

Along the chromosome: topology weighting

D gives one number for a whole genome, and it works with four genomes. Topology weighting (Martin and Van Belleghem 2017) asks where along a chromosome each history holds, with several samples per species. Build one tree for each window of the chromosome. Take one sample from each of four species and see which of the three possible arrangements they show. Do this for every combination of samples: the weight of an arrangement is "the fraction of all unique subtrees (in which each taxon is represented by a single tip) that match the particular taxon topology". With three samples from each of four species, that is 81 subtrees per window. The three weights add up to 1.

Read the three rows. Top: the species tree holds almost everywhere. Middle: a short branch gives much ILS, so the wrong arrangements are common, but spread along the whole chromosome and about equal to each other. Bottom: one arrangement takes over in a single block. Real window trees are estimated, so window size matters: the authors found 50 SNPs "a good compromise between signal and resolution". Too few sites give poorly resolved trees and intermediate weights. The method "simply describes the signal in the data, and does not explicitly test for introgression". A block like the one above could also have other causes, so it needs other evidence. A related idea uses branch lengths: QuIBL separates ILS from introgression by "the distribution of internal branch lengths among windows" (Edelman et al. 2019).

The anomaly zone: when the most common gene tree is wrong

With three species the matching tree is always the most common: the formula never drops below 1/3, and each of the others never rises above it. A natural idea is then to take a vote: build many gene trees and pick the one that turns up most often. With four or more species that vote can fail.

Degnan and Rosenberg (2006) call a gene tree anomalous if it is more likely than the gene tree that matches the species tree. The anomaly zone is the set of branch lengths where such a tree exists. It exists for asymmetric species trees of four species, and for every species tree of five or more. It needs short branches, "especially if it is deep in the tree".

Here is a toy case. Take the four-species tree (((A,B),C),D) with both internal branches 0.1 coalescent units long. We simulated 8 million genes from it:

Why does a wrong tree win? In the wrong tree the pairs A,B and C,D can meet in either order, so there are two paths to it. The species tree has one strict order of meetings, so only one path. Adding genes makes this worse, not better: the vote settles on the wrong tree more and more firmly. The authors call the vote "statistically inconsistent" here. That means it does not converge on the truth as data grow.

There is a way out. Forget the root and look only at quartets: unrooted trees of four species. Four species have just three of these, for example AB|CD, which says that A and B are on one side of the tree and C and D on the other. Under this model, "quartet trees do not have anomaly zones" (Sayyari and Mirarab 2016). In the same simulation:

ASTRAL

ASTRAL is a summary method used in many studies. Its documentation describes it as "a tool for estimating an unrooted species tree given a set of unrooted gene trees". It "finds the species tree that has the maximum number of shared induced quartet trees with the set of gene trees". In plain words: for every set of four species, each gene tree shows one quartet. ASTRAL looks for the species tree that agrees with as many of these as possible, choosing among branches that it collects from the data. Because the most likely quartet is the true one, this is statistically consistent under the multispecies coalescent, even in the anomaly zone, if gene trees are correct.

Real ASTRAL has far too many trees to score all of them, so it searches among branches found in the gene trees.

Branch lengths
ASTRAL-III gives internal branches in coalescent units, the same T as in our figure. Its guide calls them "a direct measure of the amount of discordance in the gene trees" and warns that they are "prone to underestimation because of statistical noise in gene tree estimation". ASTRAL-IV gives lengths in substitutions per site for all branches instead.
Support
The default is the local posterior probability (Sayyari and Mirarab 2016). It assumes that gene trees differ "only due to ILS", so gene flow and paralogy are outside its model.
Polytomy test
If a branch has length zero, the three arrangements around it are equally common. Sayyari and Mirarab (2018) test this with a chi-square on the three counts: a p-value below 0.05 says the branch is unlikely to be a polytomy. It assumes error-free gene trees. In ASTRAL-III it is -t 10, and the toy above shows it for each branch.
Rooting
Input trees are treated as unrooted and "the output of ASTRAL should also be treated as an unrooted tree" (ASTRAL guide). ASTRAL-IV has the option --root YOUR_OUTGROUP to root its output.
Low-support branches
Zhang et al. (2018) found that contracting gene tree branches with very low support, for example below 10 %, improved accuracy, while "overly aggressive filtering is harmful".
Which version
The ASTRAL-III page encourages using the newer code in the ASTER package. ASTRAL-IV lacks some ASTRAL-III features; its guide suggests passing its tree to ASTRAL-III with -q for these. Paralogs: astral-pro3 in the same package.

Gene trees are not the only way in. SVDquartets (Chifman and Kubatko 2014) infers quartets straight from the sites under the coalescent model, without building gene trees.

Concordance factors: how much of the genome agrees with a branch

Bootstrap support answers "how sure are we of this branch, given the data?" (section 07). With thousands of loci that answer is almost always 100 %. Concordance factors answer another question: how much of the data actually shows this branch?

gCF, gene concordance factor
The "percentage of 'decisive' gene trees containing that branch" (Minh et al. 2020). A gene tree is decisive for a branch if it has the species needed to show that branch.
sCF, site concordance factor
The "percentage of decisive sites supporting a branch". Alignment columns are compared on quartets of species, one at a time.
gDF1, gDF2, gDFP; sDF1, sDF2
The matching discordance factors. The numbers 1 and 2 are the two other arrangements around a branch, like our two wrong trees. gDFP collects the decisive gene trees that show none of the three arrangements (the P is for paraphyly).
# 1. a reference tree, here from the concatenated alignment
iqtree3 -s ALN_FILE -p PARTITION_FILE --prefix concat -B 1000 -T AUTO
# 2. gene concordance factors, using the locus trees made above
iqtree3 -t concat.treefile --gcf loci.treefile --prefix concord
# 3. site concordance factors, likelihood version, 100 quartets per branch
iqtree3 -te concat.treefile -s ALN_FILE --scfl 100 --prefix concord2

IQ-TREE writes the values onto the tree (concord.cf.tree) and into a table (concord.cf.stat). Its documentation recommends --scfl (from version 2.2.2) over the older parsimony-based --scf, and at least 100 quartets per branch.

How to read them, following Lanfear and Hahn (2024):

  1. A concordance factor is not support. It is a "biological parameter": an estimate of "the proportion of the genome for which a given clade is true". A true branch can have a low one. Minh et al. (2020) show a tree in which all but one bootstrap value are 100 %, yet the lowest gCF is 4.5 % and the lowest sCF 33.2 %.
  2. The figure above predicts them. Under ILS alone, the expected concordance is our formula: near 1 for long branches, near 1/3 for very short ones, and about 75 % at T = 1.
  3. Compare the discordance factors. Under ILS alone, the two alternatives should be about equal. If one is clearly larger, ILS alone does not explain it, and gene flow is one possible reason.
  4. Mind the floors. sCF has a floor of about 33 %: a value near it means the sites hardly prefer this branch over the two others. gCF has no such floor, and estimation error in short loci pulls it down, because noisy gene trees fall outside all three arrangements.

Networks

A tree cannot draw gene flow, because every branch has only one parent. A phylogenetic network adds extra edges for hybridization or gene flow, and each carries an inheritance probability: the share of the genes that a hybrid population took from that parent. One published method, SNaQ (Solís-Lemus and Ané 2016), takes a table of quartet concordance factors from many loci and models ILS and hybrid edges together. Networks are harder to infer than trees: those authors note that an earlier full likelihood method was practical only for "up to about 10 species and 4 hybridizations", and they built SNaQ to go further. If your discordance factors are clearly unequal, a network is the next model to try.

Common mistakes

Reading the tree as a ladder
Omland, Cook and Crisp call it the "primitive lineage fallacy": assuming that a species-poor lineage that looks "early branching" or "basal" is ancestral source: Omland 2008, abstract only. Present-day species on a tree are all the same age. The order of tips on the page means nothing, because any node can be rotated (try it in the drawing tool below).
Calling 100 % "proof"
IQ-TREE's FAQ warns that for phylogenomic data UFBoot and even the standard bootstrap "will tend to be 100%", and recommends concordance factors for any phylogenomic analysis source: IQ-TREE FAQ. The gene concordance factor is the percentage of decisive gene trees that contain the branch, and the site concordance factor the percentage of decisive sites that support it source: IQ-TREE concordance factors. A branch can be strongly supported by the pooled data and still be contradicted by many genes.
Using the wrong support scale
Do not set UFBoot beside the standard bootstrap as if they were the same measure source: IQ-TREE FAQ. The FAQ says to start relying on a branch at UFBoot of at least 95 %, and to be more confident when SH-aLRT is at least 80 % as well, but it adds that these recommendations only apply to single gene trees source: IQ-TREE FAQ.
Trusting a Bayesian run that has not converged
The MrBayes manual calls an average standard deviation of split frequencies below 0.01 "very good indication of convergence", and values between 0.01 and 0.05 possibly adequate for some purposes. It says an effective sample size (ESS) below 100 suggests poor mixing, and that PSRF should be close to 1.0 source: MrBayes manual.
One gene taken for the species tree
A single locus is the history of that piece of DNA (s10). Say "gene tree" in the text unless you have dealt with disagreement between loci. advice
Branch lengths read as time
On a tree from sequences, a branch length is an amount of change, not years, unless you did a dating analysis (s09). advice
No root, or an unstated root
Most programs give an unrooted tree, and the drawing program chooses some root to show it. A rooted-looking figure must say how it was rooted. The Newick description itself says that for an unrooted tree the convention is to root it arbitrarily and report the result source: Newick standard.

File formats

A tree file is plain text. Two formats cover nearly every program.

Newick
Interior nodes are written as matched parentheses. Inside them are the descendants, separated by commas. The tree ends with a semicolon. A branch length comes after a node, after a colon. A name may be any printable characters except blanks, colons, semicolons, parentheses and square brackets source: Newick standard. Trees "can be multifurcating at any level" (a node may have more than two children).
Nexus
A file made of separate blocks. Each block holds one kind of data, such as taxa, characters or trees, so that one file can carry the alignment and the trees together source: Maddison et al. 1997, abstract only. Inside a trees block the trees are usually written in Newick.
# Newick: the first two examples are on the Newick standard page

Simple explanation with examples: what a rotation is

A tree is like a hanging mobile. Each node is a joint where two or more arms hang. Spin any joint and the arms swap places, but nothing is cut or tied differently. ((A,B),(C,D)); and ((D,C),(B,A)); are the same tree, drawn twice. In both, A and B are sisters, and C and D are sisters.

So do not say "A is close to C" because their tips are drawn next to each other. Find the node where the paths from A and from C meet. The nearer that node is to the tips, the closer the relatives.
