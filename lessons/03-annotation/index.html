Lesson 3 · from assembly to biology

Repeats, Genes and ROH

An assembled genome is a few billion letters without labels. This lesson turns it into something a biologist can use. First we find the repeats: what kinds of repeated DNA a mammal genome holds, how to build a library of your species' own repeat families, and how to mark every copy so that later programs are not fooled by them. Then we find the genes, using RNA and proteins as evidence, gene-prediction pipelines, and genes carried over from a well-annotated relative. We give the genes names and test whether the gene set is believable. Last, we use the same reads and assembly for a question that needs no genes: how heterozygous the animal is and where its genome carries long identical stretches inherited twice from one ancestor.

The running example is one of our own rodent genomes, called species A here because the work is not yet published: a good long-read assembly, no RNA-seq, and a gene set projected from human with TOGA2. It shows both what these methods give you and how base errors in the assembly can turn into false biology. Population history (PSMC and relatives), DNA methylation and an in-depth treatment of SINEs and satellites are left for later lessons. Hover over a dotted term to see what it means.

01From assembly to annotation: what and in which order

An assembly is a few billion letters with no labels. Annotation writes labels onto those letters: here is a repeat, here is a gene, its exons start and stop at these positions, and this gene probably makes a protein of this kind. This section explains the words, the order of the work, the one file convention every later tool depends on (soft-masking), what data to collect before you start, and how to choose a gene annotation route.

Two kinds of annotation

Structural
Where things are, as coordinates on the assembly. Three layers:
  • Repeats: transposable elements, satellites and simple repeats (sections 02 to 04).
  • Protein-coding genes: for each gene, its exons and introns, the CDS and the UTRs, and alternative transcripts (sections 05 to 07).
  • Non-coding RNA genes: genes whose product is the RNA itself, such as tRNAs and rRNAs. NCBI's pipeline EGAPx, for example, predicts rRNAs by default and tRNAs, snoRNAs and snRNAs when you switch that on. This lesson does not cover non-coding RNA genes further.
Functional
What a gene probably does: a name (for example from its ortholog in a well-studied species), the protein domains it carries, and functional terms. Section 08.

The order of the work

  1. Clean the assembly and fix the names

    Remove contamination (lesson 2, FCS-GX) and the copies of mitochondrial DNA (lesson 2, mito). Give the sequences short, simple names now (see Sequence names below). Every later file refers to these names, so changing them later means converting every file.

    EGAPx README: screen for contamination before running it · BRAKER README: "Use simple scaffold names"

  2. Repeats: build a library, then mask

    Find the repeat families of your species (03), then mark every copy in the genome (04). The result is a soft-masked genome: repeats in lowercase. Repeats come first because gene finders mistake transposable elements for genes (next part).

  3. Collect gene evidence

    RNA from the animal, proteins of relatives, an annotated relative genome. Align them to the masked genome (05).

  4. Predict or project genes

    A gene-prediction pipeline (06), a projection from a relative's annotation (07), or both.

  5. Name, check, submit

    Give genes names and domains, measure completeness, prepare files for a database (08).

  6. Side branch: heterozygosity and runs of homozygosity

    Uses the reads and the assembly, not the gene annotation, but uses the repeat mask to choose which regions to trust (09).

Simple explanation with examples: annotation as labelling a map

Think of the assembly as a satellite photo of a country with no labels. Annotation is drawing on it. First you shade the areas you know are not interesting for your question: forests that repeat the same pattern again and again (repeats). Then you draw the roads (genes) and mark where each road starts, where it is interrupted by bridges (introns) and where it ends. Last, you write names on the roads: "this one goes to the harbour" (function).

Why shade the forests first? Because from above, a long straight logging track inside a forest looks just like a road. A transposable element such as a LINE really does carry protein-coding reading frames; a gene finder that sees it unmasked will report it as a gene, and there are tens of thousands of copies.

Example from one of our rodent genomes (species A, not yet published): its largest single repeat family is a LINE-1 family with a full-length (several kb) consensus and tens of thousands of copies. Unmasked, every intact copy is a candidate "gene".

Soft-masking and hard-masking

Masking marks repeat bases in the FASTA file itself. There are two ways:

Soft-masking
Repeat bases are written in lowercase (acgt), everything else in capitals. No information is lost; a program can choose to treat lowercase as "repeat" or ignore the case. RepeatMasker writes this with -xsmall.
Hard-masking
Repeat bases are replaced by N ("unknown base"). The sequence of the repeat is gone from that file. This is RepeatMasker's default output; -x uses X instead of N.

For annotation, soft-masking is the standard. The tools say so themselves (table below). Keep one soft-masked FASTA as your master copy; you can always make a hard-masked or unmasked copy from it, but not the other way round.

Why repeats are masked before genes

Gene finders look for long open reading frames with good splice sites. Many transposable elements have exactly that, because they carry genes for their own copying enzymes. GALBA's README describes the result: if transposable elements "have not been masked appropriately, AUGUSTUS tends to predict those elements as protein coding genes. This can lead to a huge number genes." BRAKER's README gives the same reason for masking: it "will avoid the prediction of false positive gene structures in repetitive and low complexity regions." A gene set with far more genes than its relatives, where many proteins are near-copies of each other, is the typical sign. Section 08 shows how to check.

Soft-masking does not forbid genes in repeats; it only makes them less likely. Real genes can contain repeat fragments in their introns or UTRs, and a gene finder on a soft-masked genome can still place an exon there when RNA or protein evidence supports it.

What evidence to gather, before you start

  1. RNA-seq from the same species, several tissues

    Short-read RNA-seq. A gene appears only in tissues where it is switched on, so RNA from one organ shows only that organ's genes; brain, testis, liver, kidney and an embryo or whole juvenile together cover much more. For gene prediction the reads are aligned to the genome; BRAKER needs each intron "covered by many alignments", so a few well-covered libraries are better than one shallow one. Used by BRAKER3, EGAPx (05, 06).

  2. Long-read RNA (Iso-Seq, Nanopore cDNA)

    Each read is a whole transcript, so it shows which exons go together. BRAKER has a separate long-read variant that must not be mixed with short reads in one run; EGAPx accepts long reads alone or together with short reads. Its value depends on depth: BRAKER warns that accuracy "heavily depends on the depth of your isoseq data".

  3. Proteins of relatives

    Two different kinds. (a) A large protein database with many representatives of each protein family, such as the OrthoDB clade files the BRAKER team provides (Vertebrata.fa.gz at bioinf.uni-greifswald.de/bioinf/partitioned_odb12/). BRAKER requires this kind: "many representatives of each protein family must be present". (b) The proteomes of one or a few close relatives, for example from UniProt reference proteomes or NCBI RefSeq. GALBA is built for this kind.

  4. An annotated relative genome

    A related species with a good genome and gene annotation (for mammals, for example human or mouse). Its genes can be projected onto your genome through a whole-genome alignment (TOGA2, 07). This gives orthology and, uniquely, a call for each reference gene: intact, lost or missing.

The tissue list is this course's advice, not a tool requirement. Plan RNA sampling together with the DNA sampling, so that the RNA comes from the same population or, better, the same animal.

    Annotation files: GFF3, GTF and BED

    Gene and repeat annotations are plain tab-separated text files: one line per feature (a gene, a transcript, an exon). Three formats dominate, and the trap is that they do not count positions the same way.

    GFF3
    9 columns: sequence name, source, feature type, start, end, score, strand, phase, attributes. Positions are 1-based and inclusive: the first base of a contig is 1, and start=1, end=100 is 100 bases. Lines are linked by ID= and Parent= in column 9. The format for submission and for most pipelines' final output (Helixer, EGAPx, BRAKER with --gff3).
    GTF
    The same 9 columns and the same 1-based counting (it is GFF version 2), but column 9 says gene_id "…"; transcript_id "…";. BRAKER writes GTF unless you add --gff3.
    BED
    UCSC's format: name, start, end, and optionally up to 9 more columns. Positions are 0-based, end not included: the first 100 bases are start=0, end=100. BED12 holds a whole transcript on one line. TOGA2 writes its gene models as BED12; repeat tracks are often BED.

    Sequence names

    Contig names travel into every file: the BAM of RNA-seq reads, the repeat table, the GFF3, the .2bit, the chains. Problems the tools' own documents warn about:

    02Repeats I: what is repeated in a genome

    In a typical placental mammal, transposable elements alone make up close to half of the assembly. Before you can find these repeats (section 03) or mask them (section 04), you need their names: what kinds exist, how they are grouped into families, and how the difference between a copy and its family's "master" sequence tells you its age.

    Two ways to be repeated

    Interspersed repeats
    Copies scattered all over the genome, each sitting in its own place with its own unique neighbours. Almost all of them are transposable elements (TEs): DNA segments that can make new copies of themselves at new places in the genome. A copy that stopped moving long ago is still called a TE.
    Tandem repeats
    Copies lying side by side, head to tail, in one block (an array): CACACACACA, or a unit of a few hundred bp repeated many times. Microsatellites, minisatellites, satellite DNA and ribosomal-RNA gene arrays are tandem repeats.
    Segmental duplications
    Long stretches (genes included) copied to another place. Usually defined as copies longer than 1 kb that are more than 90 % identical. They are not made by a TE's own machinery and are not found with repeat libraries.

    Transposable elements: two classes, a few orders

    The scheme used by almost everyone was proposed by Wicker et al. (2007). It sorts TEs first by how they move (the class), then by structure and enzymes into orders, superfamilies and families. Its orders are LTR, DIRS, PLE, LINE and SINE in class I, and TIR, Crypton, Helitron and Maverick in class II. Dfam and RepeatMasker use their own, more detailed tree that maps onto it.

    • Class I, retrotransposons ("copy and paste"). The element is first read into an RNA copy. A reverse transcriptase then writes that RNA back into DNA at a new place. The old copy stays, so every round adds copies.
    • Class II, DNA transposons. No RNA step. Most of them encode a transposase that cuts the element out and pastes it elsewhere ("cut and paste"). Helitrons copy themselves by a rolling-circle mechanism; Mavericks carry a virus-like DNA polymerase.

    What makes a SINE

    SINEs matter in this course because our lab works on them and because generic tools often miss or mislabel them (section 03). SINEBase (Vassetzky & Kramerov 2013) describes them as short non-autonomous retroposons, usually under 700 bp, that are transcribed by the cell's RNA polymerase III (Pol III) from a promoter inside the element itself. They are built from modules:

    • Head. Derived from a small RNA made by Pol III: a tRNA (the canonical case in Dfam), 7SL RNA (primate Alu, rodent B1) or 5S rRNA. Dfam's class tree is built on this: SINE;tRNA_Promoter, SINE;7SL-RNA_Promoter, SINE;5S-RNA_Promoter, SINE;U-RNA_Promoter. Rodent B4 is filed under a combined "tRNA_and_7SL_RNA" head.
    • Body. Either of unknown origin or taken from the partner LINE's 3′ end, which lets the SINE RNA be recognised by that LINE's enzyme. Some bodies contain a "core" domain shared by distant SINE families.
    • Tail. Simple, often degenerate, repeats of variable length; the tail can differ between copies of one family.
    • Dimeric SINEs join two monomers (single SINE units): the primate Alu is a dimer of 7SL-derived monomers. Simple SINEs have a head and a tail only.

    SINEBase's working definition, useful when you judge a candidate: (1) shorter than 1 kb and not tandem; (2) at least 100 copies per genome; (3) at least 60 % identity with a tRNA, 5S rRNA or 7SL RNA over at least 60 nt. The A and B boxes are evidence, not proof: pattern searches for them give many false positives and misses. A SINE is also not an RNA pseudogene (a reverse-transcribed copy of the cellular RNA itself): pseudogenes are fewer and lack the extra body.

    Tandem repeats, rDNA and duplications

    Microsatellites
    Very short units repeated in short runs: (CA)n, (TTAGGG)n. RepeatMasker reports them as Simple_repeat (Dfam: "short pattern tandem repeats found in short runs"). RepeatMasker's help file says it scans only di- to pentameric and some hexameric units and ignores runs shorter than 20 bp; in the 4.2.6 code its internal TRF passes accept units of up to 10 bp (section 04). Their length often differs between the two chromosome copies, and a variant caller may then report a run of false SNPs (section 04).
    Minisatellites
    Units longer than microsatellite units. Authors draw the size boundaries between micro-, mini- and satellites differently, so check the definition used by each tool or paper.
    Satellite DNA
    Dfam: "medium to long pattern tandem repeats found in long runs". Dfam's subclasses show where they sit: centromeric, subtelomeric, acrocentric short arms, Y- and W-chromosomal (RepeatMasker labels such as Satellite/centromeric). Like rDNA and segmental duplications below, long near-identical arrays are among the regions assemblies most often collapse.
    rDNA arrays
    The ribosomal RNA genes sit in tandem arrays of identical units. In the human T2T-CHM13 genome these lie on the short arms of the acrocentric chromosomes; with a ~45-kb unit, the first T2T assembly still missed about 10 Mb (about 200 units) of rDNA, which had to be assembled separately. Expect these arrays to be collapsed or missing in your assembly too.
    Segmental duplications
    > 1 kb, > 90 % identity. In humans they cover 7.0 % of the complete T2T-CHM13 genome (218 Mb), up from 5.4 % estimated on GRCh38; much of the gain was sequence that older assemblies had collapsed. Duplicated genes inside them confuse gene annotation (section 07).
    Low complexity
    Stretches of biased composition (for example AT-rich) without a clear unit. RepeatMasker label Low_complexity.
    The classes blur. A satellite can be built from a piece of a SINE: SINE-derived satellites with 67-290 bp monomers are known from scaled reptiles (Vassetzky, Kosushkin & Ryskov 2023), and in the bat Rhinolophus sinicus we found a 140-bp, 58-copy satellite derived from an r2 SINE. A SINE library will "mask" such an array as SINE and a tandem-repeat finder will call it a satellite: both are right.

    Families, subfamilies and the consensus

    Family
    All copies descended from one ancestral active element. The practical rule used by most programs, from Wicker et al. (2007): two elements belong to one family if they share at least 80 % identity over at least 80 % of their length, in a region of at least 80 bp (the "80-80-80 rule"). Some groups use other thresholds.
    Subfamily
    Within a family, the copies made by one of several successive "master" source genes. They share a set of diagnostic substitutions and small indels that the master carried when it made them. Average identity alone separates subfamilies badly, because most differences between two copies are private mutations each acquired after it was inserted.
    Consensus
    Align many copies of a family and take the majority base at each column. Because each copy decays independently, the majority recovers, approximately, the sequence of the active element that made them. A repeat library is a file of such consensus sequences (or profile HMMs built from the alignments), one per family or subfamily, each with a name and a class label.

    How old is a copy? Divergence from the consensus

    Most TE insertions have no effect on fitness, so a copy collects mutations at the neutral rate. Its difference from the consensus (which stands for the source element) therefore grows with time since insertion. Counting raw mismatches underestimates old copies, because a site can mutate twice and only one change is visible. The Kimura two-parameter (K2P) distance corrects for this, treating transitions and transversions separately. RepeatMasker computes it per alignment (SearchResult.pm, sub calcKimuraDivergence):

    p = transitions / L    q = transversions / L    K = −½ · ln[ (1 − 2p − q) · √(1 − 2q) ] × 100 %

    Here L counts the aligned positions where both the copy and the consensus have a clear base (A, C, G or T); positions opposite a gap are not counted. One more correction is specific to mammals and other animals with methylated DNA: a CpG site in the consensus mutates by transition much faster than other sites. With the CpG modification (on by default in RepeatMasker), at each CpG of the consensus:

    • two transitions (CG → TA) count as one transition;
    • a single transition (CG → TG or CA) counts as 1/10 of a transition;
    • transversions count normally.

    The source comment notes that CpG transitions are about 15 times faster than other transitions in human, and less in rodents. Three different "divergence" numbers come out of one RepeatMasker run, so always check which one a table shows:

    To turn divergence into time you need a neutral substitution rate, and rates differ between lineages and along the genome. The Zoonomia survey used one mammal-wide rate, 2.2 × 10−9 per site per year, under which 4 % K2P divergence is about 20 million years; the authors treat such dates as rough and use them only to call copies under 4 % "young". Copies of one family made in a short burst have similar ages, so they form a peak in a histogram of divergence: a repeat landscape.

    Simple explanation with examples: photocopies of photocopies

    Think of a TE family as a page that a photocopier keeps copying and sticking into random places of a long book. Every page in the book then slowly fades and collects smudges, each in its own random spots.

    To read the original page, line up many copies and, letter by letter, take what most of them say: that is the consensus. A copy with few smudges compared with the consensus was stuck in recently; a heavily smudged one is old. If the copier ran hard for a short while long ago (a burst), you find many copies with the same amount of smudging: a peak in the histogram.

    Sometimes the copier itself got a smudge and kept copying with it. All later copies share that smudge: they form a subfamily. That shared smudge is "diagnostic"; the private smudges each page collected afterwards are not.

    How much of a mammal genome is repeat?

    Numbers depend on the library and the masking program as much as on the animal, so compare only results made the same way. With one curated mammal library and RepeatMasker 4.1.0, the Zoonomia survey found TE contents of 27.6-74.5 % (mean 45.6 %, mean assembly 2.67 Gb). Rodents spread widely: the hazel dormouse reached 65.8 %, the Brazilian guinea pig 28.1 %. In the same study, SINEs and LTR retrotransposons were more prevalent in Euarchontoglires (rodents, primates and their relatives), and LINEs dominated most other lineages. Genome size rose with TE content, driven mostly by young LINEs.

    In our projects

    For the assembly of our species A (a rodent, about 3 Gb; not yet published, so no name or exact numbers here), a RepeatMasker run with a RepeatModeler2 de novo library (made by the Hiller lab; exact versions not recorded) soft-masked about half of the genome. Summed by RepeatMasker class, Unknown was by far the largest class (more than a third of the genome, hundreds of families); LINEs (all L1) came next, roughly a tenth of the genome; LTR elements (ERVK, ERV1, ERVL, ERVL-MaLR), simple repeats and DNA transposons (mostly hAT-Charlie and TcMar-Tigger) each covered only a few per cent or less; and SINE: no family at all. The largest single family is a young L1: a full-length consensus of several kb, tens of thousands of copies covering about 5 % of the genome, with a low mean divergence in the table (the exact divergence measure of that column is not recorded).

    A rodent with no SINEs would be odd, and it is not true: over a hundred of the "Unknown" families match known rodent SINE consensus sequences (B1- and B4-like and others) and together cover more than a tenth of the genome. The de novo library found them but could not name them. Section 03 shows why classification is the weak step of automatic libraries.

    Repeat libraries: Dfam and RepBase

    A masker needs a library of consensus sequences or profile HMMs. Two public collections exist, and you will usually add your own de novo library (section 03).

    Dfam
    Open database (licence CC0, public domain) run by the Dfam consortium; RepeatMasker reads it through FamDB, which its README calls optional but highly recommended. Release 4.0 (May 2026) holds 7.6 million families across 6,549 taxa. Curated families have DF accessions and were checked by people; for Mammalia these are 8,864 retrotransposon, 1,744 DNA-transposon and 177 other families. Uncurated families have DR accessions: de novo libraries submitted as they came, such as 1,704 RepeatModeler libraries from EBI in this release; for Mammalia 200,058, 40,316 and 64,079. Uncurated libraries can contain gene families, processed pseudogenes and tandem artefacts: the Zoonomia curators removed 152 pseudogene models and more than 210 tandem-artefact models from their own de novo set before submitting it.
    FamDB
    Dfam's download format (HDF5 files), read by RepeatMasker and by famdb.py. Since FamDB 3.0 (May 2026) it is split into components, each partitioned along the taxonomy: cc curated consensus, ch curated HMMs, uc uncurated consensus, uh uncurated HMMs, plus one root file that is always required. For Dfam 4.0 the root file is 61 MB and the whole curated-consensus component a single 28 MB file (gzip sizes), while the uncurated HMMs come in 110 partition files of 1-4 GB each. Download only what your taxon needs; famdb.py check tells you which partitions a species query uses.
    RepBase
    The older collection of the Genetic Information Research Institute (GIRI). Since 12 April 2019 access has needed a paid subscription, and Dfam's 4.0 release notes still call it "a closed database". On 25 June 2026 GIRI announced it will release RepBase under CC0 and partner with Dfam. Check the current state on girinst.org before you plan around it.

    Pitfalls

    • "Unknown" is not "not a TE". In de novo libraries a large Unknown share often hides whole classes (our SINEs above). Report it, and try to classify it, rather than leave it out of class totals.
    • Know which divergence you plot. Raw .out divergence, K2P, and K2P with the CpG rule give different numbers; landscapes and ages need the .align-based values.
    • Divergence is not a date. Converting it uses an assumed neutral rate that varies between lineages; give ages as rough ranges.
    • Collapsed arrays look small. Satellites, rDNA and young segmental duplications are under-assembled; their share in the assembly is a lower bound.
    • Compare like with like. Repeat percentages from different libraries, tools or assembly qualities are not comparable across species.
    Later deep-dive: SINEs in our lab. SINEs are the lab's own research topic, and they get a lesson of their own. In short: our SINEderella pipeline searches a genome with a SINE consensus bank, refines consensus sequences and boundaries, and assigns every copy to a subfamily by its diagnostic pattern of small indels and substitutions; it also has a de novo stage that finds SINEs without a library. With a lab SINE bank for the right rodent group it found on the order of a million SINE loci in species A and in its published close relative, far more than the generic library labelled as SINE (none). Our boundary and subfamily results so far come mostly from one SINE type and should not be generalised to all SINEs.

    03Repeats II: building a repeat library de novo

    A masking program such as RepeatMasker (section 04) only finds repeats it has a description of. That description is a repeat library: a FASTA file with one consensus sequence per repeat family. This section is about making that library from your own assembly, de novo (from scratch, without a reference), and about checking it.

    Why your species needs its own library

    • Lineage-specific families. Transposable elements (TEs) keep arising and dying out. Many families exist only in one genus or family of animals. A public library built from other species (Dfam or RepBase) cannot contain a family nobody has described.
    • Known families, wrong consensus. Even when a related family is in Dfam, its consensus was made from another species. Your copies differ from it more than they differ from their own consensus, so matches are shorter, weaker or missed.
    • Classification needs relatives. A de novo tool can tell you "this is repeated" without telling you what it is. Families that match nothing known are labelled Unknown. In a new lineage this can be a large share.
    In our projects

    The assembly of our rodent species A (not yet published, so no name or exact numbers here) was masked with a de novo library (family names like rnd-1_family-5, the naming RepeatModeler uses; the exact RepeatModeler and RepeatMasker versions of that run are not recorded in our folder). About half of the genome is soft-masked. Of the masked bases, the largest class by far is Unknown: hundreds of families, more than a third of the genome. Not one family was labelled SINE. Yet when the family consensuses were compared with a bank of known SINEs (rodent SINEs from SINEBase plus the lab's own SINE bank), over a hundred of the "Unknown" families matched a SINE, and together they cover more than a tenth of the genome. Example: one of the most abundant "Unknown" families, a consensus of about 200 bp with hundreds of thousands of copies, matches a known rodent SINE at about 90 % identity over nearly its whole length. The library found the SINEs; it just did not name them. How the "SINE-like" threshold was set in that comparison is not recorded, so treat that share as approximate.

    What every de novo tool does

    1. Find sequence that occurs many times
      Either by sequence (count repeated words, or compare pieces of the genome all against all) or by structure (look for the shape of one TE type, for example two long terminal repeats a few kb apart).
    2. Group copies into families
      Copies that align well to each other are one family.
    3. Align the copies and call a consensus
      The consensus stands for the whole family in the library.
    4. Extend to full length
      The first consensus is often only a piece of the element. Copies are extended into their neighbouring sequence until they stop agreeing: that is the end of the element (the toy below).
    5. Classify
      Compare with known TE proteins and consensus sequences; give a label such as LINE/L1 or Unknown.
    6. Filter
      Remove simple repeats, duplicates of other families and pieces of real genes.

    The four tools below differ mainly in how they do steps 1 and 4.

    RepeatModeler2 (version 2.0.9)

    The standard tool, from the RepeatMasker authors (Hubley and Smit, with the LTR extension by Flynn; paper: Flynn et al. 2020, PNAS). It combines three repeat finders:

    1. BuildDatabase
      Turns the assembly into a BLAST database. Sequence names are replaced by numbers; the .translation file keeps the link back.
    2. Round 1: RepeatScout on a 40 Mb sample
      Finds the young, abundant families from a small random sample. Simple repeats are filtered out.
    3. Rounds 2-5: RECON on growing samples
      Samples of 10, 30, 90 and 270 Mb (defaults since version 2.0.4; -quick uses the older, smaller samples). Before each round, the sample is masked with the families already found, so the round looks for new ones. A family needs at least 15 copies in the sample; the code comments say this also keeps out segmental duplications, which have few copies.
    4. Refiner
      Builds a seed alignment and consensus for each family. Since 2.0.7 it also tries to extend each family with RepeatAfterMe. Large satellites and families contained in other families are then filtered out.
    5. Optional LTR module (-LTRStruct)
      Runs LTRharvest and LTR_retriever and merges their families in (redundancy removed with CD-HIT). The paper reports the largest gain for LTR retrotransposons.
    6. RepeatClassifier
      Labels each family, in three steps described below.
    # 1. database (use a local disk, not a network drive: the README says I/O is heavy)
BuildDatabase -name spA genome.fa

# 2. de novo run with the LTR module; keep the log, it holds the random seed
#    (random seed: the start number for the random sampling; the same seed gives the same samples)
#    nohup ... & : keep running in the background after you log out
#    BLAST_USAGE_REPORT=false stops RMBlast reporting usage over the network (README caveat)
export BLAST_USAGE_REPORT=false
nohup RepeatModeler -database spA -threads 20 -LTRStruct > run.out 2>&1 &

# 3. if it stops: continue from the last finished round
RepeatModeler -database spA -threads 20 -LTRStruct -recoverDir RM_12345.MonOct271200002026
    spA-families.fa
    The library: one consensus per family, named rnd-<round>_family-<n>#Class/Family (LTR-module families start ltr-1_family-). This is what you give RepeatMasker with -lib.
    spA-families.stk
    Stockholm seed alignments, the copies behind each consensus. Needed for curation and for submitting to Dfam.
    spA-rmod.log
    Summary log with the random seed. With -srand <seed> you can reproduce the same samples.
    RM_<PID>.<date>/
    Working folder with one subfolder per round. Keep it until the run is finished: -recoverDir needs it.

    Time and memory. The README recommends one machine with more than 32 GB RAM and many cores (their own: 28 cores, 128 GB) and says a well-assembled mammalian genome can take more than 1-2 days with 20 threads. RepeatModeler runs on one machine only; do not split a genome into batches and do not merge several runs into one library: the result is "largely redundant" (README). To sample more of the genome, raise -genomeSampleSizeMax instead.

    How RepeatClassifier names a family, and why SINEs stay "Unknown"

    1. Simple repeats
      The consensus is masked for simple repeats; one with fewer than 40 unmasked bases is treated as a simple or tandem repeat.
    2. Proteins
      The consensus is translated and compared with a library of TE proteins (rmblastx, cutoff p < 0.001). A hit to an L1 reverse transcriptase gives LINE/L1, a transposase gives a DNA transposon class, and so on.
    3. Known consensuses
      The consensus is compared with all TE consensuses in the installed Dfam (rmblastn, minimum score 250). The best match gives the label.

    A SINE encodes no protein, so step 2 can never label it. It is named only if step 3 finds a known SINE that is similar enough and that is in the Dfam partitions installed on your machine. A short, lineage-specific SINE often has neither, and comes out as Unknown. This fits what we saw in species A (box above). Unknown means "repeated, but not matched to anything known". It does not mean "not a TE".

    EDTA (version 2.3.3)

    The Extensive de novo TE Annotator (Ou et al. 2019, Genome Biology) runs one specialised finder per TE type and then filters the candidates hard. It was designed and benchmarked on rice with a curated rice library, and most of its users work on plants. The --species option knows only Rice, Maize and others; the default mutation rate for dating LTR elements (--u 1.3e-8) is also from rice.

    • LTR: LTRharvest and LTR_FINDER (parallel versions) with LTR_retriever.
    • SINE: AnnoSINE_v2, then TEsorter to drop misclassified candidates and a tandem-repeat clean-up.
    • LINE: RepeatModeler2 (or your own classified RepeatModeler library via --rmlib).
    • TIR: TIR-Learner. Helitron: HelitronScanner.
    • Genes out: with --cds (coding sequences of your species or a close relative, no introns, no UTRs), library entries matching genes are removed.

    HiTE (version 3.3.3)

    HiTE (Hu et al. 2024, Nature Communications) looks for full-length TEs: it finds repeated regions, extends them and adjusts their boundaries by comparing the copies (the authors call it "dynamic boundary adjustment"), then runs separate modules for LTR, TIR, Helitron and non-LTR elements (SINEs and LINEs; LINE candidates are checked for a complete LINE protein domain). Trap: --plant is 1 by default; set --plant 0 for an animal. The README recommends 40 CPU cores and 128 GB RAM. For many genomes of one species or group, panHiTE does the same across a population.

    Earl Grey (version 7.3.1)

    Earl Grey (Baril, Galbraith and Hayward 2024, Molecular Biology and Evolution) is a complete pipeline around RepeatModeler2. Its own contribution is an automatic curation step, BEAT ("BLAST, Extract, Align, Trim"), done by its TEstrainer scripts: every consensus is extended and trimmed the way a person would do it by hand (the toy below shows the idea). Then it masks the genome, joins fragments of the same element, and draws plots.

    1. Optional pre-mask with a known library (-r a Dfam group name, or -l your own FASTA). The README advises against it unless a good library exists for your species or a very close one.
  1. RepeatModeler2 (Earl Grey calls it without -LTRStruct).
  2. BEAT / TEstrainer, up to 10 rounds (-i): BLAST the consensus to the genome, take the 20 best copies (-n) with 1,000 bp of flank on each side (-f), align them with MAFFT, cut off the flank where copies stop aligning, call a new consensus (needs at least 3 copies, -a). A family goes into another round only if its consensus grew substantially (in the current code: by more than half the flank length).   3. RepeatMasker with the new library (-s, sensitive mode).
  4. Defragmentation: LTR_FINDER finds full-length LTR elements, and RepeatCraft merges neighbouring pieces of one element.
  5. Divergence and plots: Kimura divergence per copy, repeat landscapes, a pie chart, summary tables.

    What de novo libraries get wrong

    Short, young SINEs
    They are found, because they have many near-identical copies. But they come out as Unknown (no protein, see above), their ends can be fuzzy (A-rich tails of different lengths; parts shared with other SINEs), and several related subfamilies may be merged into one consensus or one family split into many.
    Satellites
    Long tandem arrays look like a repeat with many copies. RepeatModeler filters "large satellites" from its families, and TEstrainer moves them to a separate list. Tandem repeats are better found with tandem-repeat tools (section 04).
    Gene families
    Multi-copy genes (for example olfactory receptors, immune genes) are repeated too, and can slip into the library. Masking them would hide real genes from gene prediction (section 06). Remove them: EDTA --cds, or compare the library with a TE-free protein set (blastx) and drop gene hits; ProtExcluder from the MAKER-P toolkit (Campbell et al. 2014) is a widely used script for this.
    Segmental duplications
    Long duplicated blocks with only a few copies. RepeatModeler's minimum of 15 copies keeps most of them out.
    Truncated consensuses
    A 6-kb LINE may be represented only by its 3′ end, because most copies are 5′-truncated. Extension (RepeatAfterMe, BEAT) or manual curation fixes this.

    Manual curation in four steps

    The standard method is described in "A beginner's guide to manual curation of transposable elements" (Goubert et al. 2022, Mobile DNA). For one family:

    1. BLAST the consensus against the genome; keep the best copies (tens, not thousands).
    2. Extract and extend: take each copy plus extra sequence on both sides (for example 1,000 bp).
    3. Align the extended copies (MAFFT) and look at the alignment.
    4. Trim: the TE ends where the copies stop agreeing with each other, because outside it each copy sits in a different, unique place in the genome. Cut there and call a new consensus. If the consensus grew to the edge of the extension, extend again.

    TE-Aid draws four plots per consensus that help decide: genome hits along the consensus, how deep the consensus is covered, a self dot-plot (which shows terminal repeats), and open reading frames with TE protein hits. It also prints how many copies are "full length" (hit at least 90 % of the consensus by default, -f 0.9).

    Extend, align, find the boundary

    Twelve copies of one toy TE (70 bp), each sitting in its own unique genome sequence. A de novo tool gave you only a seed: bases 21-50 of the element (framed). Move the two sliders to take more sequence on each side. The bars under the alignment show, for each column, the share of copies that carry the most common base. Inside the TE the copies agree; in the flanks they do not. Find where the bars drop, then press "Trim" to let the toy rule find it. Then raise the family age and trim again: with old, diverged copies the agreement inside the element falls too, and the rule usually stops inside the element, giving a consensus that is too short.

    Toy rules (what the figure computes)
    • One random 70-bp element. Each of 12 copies gets its own random 45-bp flanks on both sides, and each TE base is changed with the chosen divergence. No insertions or deletions, so the copies are already aligned column by column (real copies need MAFFT).
    • Column agreement = copies carrying the most common base ÷ copies that have a base there. A column with at least 60 % agreement is shown in capitals in the consensus; below 60 % the consensus base is shown as lower case, and as n when two bases tie (the way TEstrainer's consensus function writes ties).
    • "Trim" walks outwards from the seed and stops at the first place where three columns in a row fall below 60 %. In random flanks one column now and then reaches 60 % by chance; requiring three in a row avoids stopping there. This rule is a teaching simplification, not the exact rule of any tool.
    • With truncated copies on, four copies have unique sequence instead of the TE left of a random break point. They lower agreement near the left end; the real start is still where agreement falls for all copies.
    Simple explanation with examples: why "where copies stop agreeing" is the end of a TE

    Think of a poster glued on many different walls in a city. Photograph each poster with a wide frame, so you also get some wall around it. Lay the photographs on top of each other, lined up on the poster. Inside the poster every photo shows the same picture (some faded, some torn: that is divergence). Just outside its edge, one photo shows brick, another glass, another a tree. The edge of the poster is the place where the photos stop looking alike.

    A TE copy is the poster, the genome around it is the wall. A de novo tool may have photographed only the middle of the poster (the seed). Extending means widening the frame; trimming means cutting the frame back to the poster's edge.

    Two things make it harder. Old posters are faded (high divergence), so the inside also looks less alike and the edge is less sharp. And torn posters (5′-truncated copies) end early on one side: the edge must be read from the copies that are complete.

    After the tool: what to check

    • Look at the biggest families first. Sort the RepeatMasker summary by bases covered; curate the top families (and every family you will discuss in a paper) with TE-Aid or an alignment.
    • Read the Unknown pile. Compare it with a SINE database such as SINEBase and with Dfam; look for a poly(A) tail (a run of A at the 3′ end, typical of SINEs and LINEs) or another simple tail, terminal repeats or protein domains.
    • Check for genes (EDTA --cds or blastx against TE-free proteins) before the library is used to mask for gene prediction.
    • Record versions of the tool and of Dfam (Earl Grey's README warns that Dfam 4.0, with new families, may change results compared with Dfam 3.9).

    For SINE families the lab uses its own pipeline, SINEderella (Toki-bio).     04Repeats III: masking and repeat landscapes

    Section 03 gave you a library: one consensus per repeat family. Now every copy of every family has to be found in the assembly, written into a table and marked in lowercase. The same search also tells you how old each copy is, which gives the repeat landscape of the genome.     RepeatMasker: how it works

    RepeatMasker (Smit, Hubley & Green; current version 4.2.6, October 2026) compares the genome with every consensus in a library and keeps the best matches. It is the standard for interspersed repeats. It ships without a repeat library: you give it one.

    1. Choose the library
      Either -species <clade>: RepeatMasker extracts from Dfam all families known from that clade and its ancestors (for example -species rodentia). Or -lib my_library.fa: your own FASTA of consensus sequences, with names written >family#class/subclass so the classes can be counted. Only curated Dfam families are used unless you add -uncurated.
    2. Align the consensus sequences to the genome
      The alignment program is called the search engine. RMBlast is the default. nhmmer (-engine hmmer) uses Dfam's profile HMMs instead and is more sensitive but much slower. Each match gets a Smith-Waterman score; matches below the cut-off (225 for most repeats, -cutoff with your own library) are dropped. There is no divergence cut-off: a match can be less than 60 % identical and still be kept.
    3. Peel off the young layer first
      With -species and a mammal clade the search runs in stages. Young, full-length insertions are cut out of the sequence first, so that the older element they landed in can be aligned in one piece. Many related consensus sequences match the same place; the best score wins the name.
    4. Find simple repeats and low-complexity DNA
      RepeatMasker runs TRF (Tandem Repeats Finder, described below) itself, in two passes, for units of up to 10 bp (source code of 4.2.6). It also marks low-complexity stretches. Satellites with longer units are found only if they are in the library.
    5. Clean up the annotation
      The script ProcessRepeats joins fragments of one insertion that were split by later insertions or by deletions, gives them one shared ID, and writes the output files.

    Which library for a non-model mammal?

    The RepeatMasker help is frank: most species are not yet covered by the repeat databases, and many entries are far from complete. A de novo library (03) has the families of your species but often with weak class labels. The help file shows how to put both into one FASTA: export the Dfam families of your clade with famdb.py and append your own consensus sequences, then run with -lib.

    Run 8 search jobs at once. Each RMBlast job uses 4 cores, so -pa 8 needs about 32 cores (nhmmer: 2 per job). Long contigs are cut into 60-kb pieces with 2-kb overlaps ($fragmentSize and $overlapLen in the 4.2.6 script), so even one big contig runs in parallel.
    -xsmall
    Write repeats in lowercase, the rest in capitals (soft-masking, see 01). Without it repeats are replaced by N (hard-masking); -x uses X.
    -a
    Also write every alignment to a .align file. Needed for repeat landscapes. A large file; compress it with gzip (the landscape script reads .align.gz).
    -s / -q / -qq
    Slow: finds 0-5 % more repeat DNA, 2.5 times slower. Quick: misses 5-10 %, 3-4 times faster. Rush: misses 10-25 % (figures from the help file, measured on mammals). The settings change the seed word length (how long an exact word match must be before an alignment is tried); they have less effect on nhmmer.
    -nolow
    Do not mask simple repeats and low-complexity DNA. The help file suggests it when the masked sequence will be used to look for exons.
    -noint
    Only simple repeats and low-complexity DNA. Useful to see microsatellites that the full run hides inside the poly-A tails of SINEs and LINEs.
    -gff, -dir
    Extra GFF file; output folder (default: next to the input file).

    Time. The help file benchmarks 1 Mb of human sequence in 15 seconds on 16 cores with RMBlast and Dfam 3.9, and says run time grows in proportion to length. That gives, as a rough extrapolation, about 3,000 × 15 s ≈ 12-13 hours for a 3-Gb mammal genome on 16 cores; nhmmer took ten times longer in the same benchmark (2 min 30 s per Mb).

    Simple explanation with examples: masking is like highlighting a book

    Imagine a book in which the same 300-word advertisement has been printed 100,000 times, each copy with a few typos added by later reprints. You want to read the story. You take a list of known advertisements (the library), find every printed copy, even damaged ones, and highlight it in grey (lowercase). The story stays readable; the adverts are marked, not cut out.

    story text ... buy now cheap watchs best prise ... more story library: buy now cheap watches best price differences: ^ ^ 2 typos in 32 letters ≈ 6 % divergence

    Copies with few typos were printed recently; copies with many typos are old. Counting how many copies have 1, 2, 3 … % typos tells you when the advertising campaigns happened. That count is the repeat landscape.

    Hard-masking would black out the adverts completely (N). The gene finders of section 06 want the grey highlight, because some real sentences (exons) overlap an advert.

    Common pitfalls with RepeatMasker

    • Gene families in the library. A de novo library can contain consensus sequences built from multi-copy genes (for example olfactory receptors or zinc-finger genes). RepeatMasker then masks those genes as "repeats", and gene prediction later misses them. Clean the library against proteins first (03; EDTA, for example, takes a CDS file "to purge gene sequences in the TE library"). Even with Dfam, the help file warns that real genes derived from transposable elements can be partly masked.
    • Simple repeats hide inside elements. Microsatellites that grew out of SINE or LINE poly-A tails are reported as part of the element. Run -noint separately if you need every microsatellite.
    • Only short units count as "Simple_repeat". The internal TRF passes look for units up to 10 bp. A 180-bp satellite is masked only if your library contains it; otherwise use a tandem-repeat finder (below).
    • Masked bases and class totals differ. The help file says the "bases masked" line of .tbl and the sum of the classes need not agree exactly (masking uses the raw matches; very short fragments are masked but not annotated).
    • Old versions had silent bugs. Before 4.2.6, a long file path (about 260 characters) made TRF crash inside RepeatMasker and simple repeats were silently missing. From 4.1.1 to 4.2.2 the staged search was not applied to rodents (lower sensitivity). Write the version into your methods.

    Repeat landscapes: how old are the copies?

    When a transposable element inserts, the new copy is identical to its source. From then on it collects random mutations at the neutral rate. Its divergence from the family consensus therefore grows with age. A histogram of how many bases of the genome sit at 0-1 %, 1-2 %, 2-3 % … divergence, stacked by repeat class, is a repeat landscape. Peaks at low divergence are recent bursts of activity; broad humps at 20-30 % are ancient families.

    Two corrections make the raw percentage a better clock:

    Kimura 2-parameter
    A position can mutate twice (A→G→A), and the second hit hides the first. The Kimura 2-parameter formula estimates the true number of changes from the observed transitions (p) and transversions (q): K = −½ ln[(1 − 2p − q)·√(1 − 2q)]. Gap positions are not counted.
    CpG rule
    In the dinucleotide CG (CpG), methylated C mutates to T very fast, so CpG-rich young elements look older than they are. RepeatMasker's rule, at each CpG site of the consensus: two transitions count as one; a single transition counts as 1/10; transversions count normally. The help says this applies to organisms with CpG methylation (mammals do).
    # 1. RepeatMasker must have been run with -a (alignments)
# 2. per-family and per-class divergence summary (reads the Kimura line under each alignment)
calcDivergenceFromAlign.pl -s genome.divsum genome.fa.align.gz
# 3. landscape as an HTML page; -g = genome size in bp (or -twoBit genome.2bit, which excludes Ns)
createRepeatLandscape.pl -div genome.divsum -g 2992238030 -t "my genome" > genome_landscape.html

    Both scripts are in the util/ folder of RepeatMasker. The .divsum file has two parts: the bp-weighted mean Kimura divergence of each family, and a table of bp per class in 1-% bins from 0 to 70 %. Simple repeats and low-complexity DNA get no divergence. To turn divergence into years you need a neutral substitution rate per year for your lineage, which is a separate assumption, so many papers show divergence only.

    Fast masking without a library: WindowMasker and Red

    Both programs count k-mers (lesson 1) in the assembly itself. A stretch made of words that occur thousands of times is called repetitive. They are fast and need no curated library, but they give no family names, no classes and no divergence, so no landscape.

    WindowMasker (NCBI)

    Part of the NCBI C++ toolkit; the bioconda blast package installs it. It works in two stages. Stage 1 (-mk_counts) counts every word of length unit in the genome and on its reverse strand. The unit length is chosen from the genome size; the code picks 15 for a 2-3 Gb genome. Words rarer than the 90th percentile are not stored, and thresholds are set at the 99th, 99.5th and 99.8th percentiles of the counts. Stage 2 (-ustat) slides a window of five overlapping units along each sequence, scores it as the average count of its units, and masks windows whose score is above a threshold set at the 99.5th percentile of word counts; gaps between masked windows are filled if every base lies in a window above the 99th-percentile value. -dust true adds the DUST low-complexity filter.

    # stage 1: word counts, once per genome
windowmasker -mk_counts -in genome.fa -out genome.wm_counts
# stage 2: mask; fasta output = lowercase repeats
windowmasker -ustat genome.wm_counts -in genome.fa -dust true -outfmt fasta -out genome.wm.fa
# or intervals: "START - END", 0-based, per sequence
windowmasker -ustat genome.wm_counts -in genome.fa -dust true -outfmt interval -out genome.wm.txt

    WindowMasker ignores the case of the input: all input is treated as unmasked. NCBI's eukaryotic annotation pipeline masks human and mouse with RepeatMasker and Dfam, and all other species with WindowMasker, so if NCBI annotated your animal, that RefSeq annotation was made on a WindowMasker-masked genome.

    Red (REpeat Detector)

    Red (Girgis 2015; version 2.0, code last changed 2018) also counts k-mers and compares each count with what a background model built from shorter words predicts, but then trains itself: it labels likely repeat and non-repeat regions from these scores and trains a hidden Markov model on them, then scans the whole genome with that model. This is the "machine learning" in its description; no external training data are needed. Output is soft-masked.

    # Red reads every .fa file in a directory
mkdir red_in red_msk red_rpt && ln -s "$PWD/genome.fa" red_in/genome.fa
Red -gnm red_in -msk red_msk -rpt red_rpt -frm 2 -cor 16
# red_msk/genome.msk = soft-masked FASTA; red_rpt/genome.rpt = repeat intervals (-frm 2: name, start, end; 0-based, end exclusive)

    When to use which. RepeatMasker with a good library when you want to know what the repeats are, how much of each, and how old (what genome papers report). WindowMasker or Red when you only need a mask quickly, for example before gene prediction of a draft, before a BLAST search, or as a cross-check: bases masked by Red but not by RepeatMasker can point to families missing from your library.

    Tandem repeats: TRF

    Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) finds arrays of head-to-tail copies without being told the unit. It spots pairs of identical short words that sit the same distance d apart again and again, tests statistically whether that looks like a tandem repeat of period d, then aligns the stretch to a consensus unit and scores it. It finds units of 1 to 2000 bp. Seven numbers follow the file name:

    trf genome.fa 2 7 7 80 10 50 500 -d -h          # the README's "recommended parameters"
trf genome.fa 2 5 7 80 10 50 2000 -d -h         # the README's Quick Start line: milder mismatch penalty, units up to 2000 bp
trf genome.fa 2 7 7 80 10 50 2000 -d -h -l 6    # chromosome-scale satellite arrays: allow arrays up to 6 Mb
    ValueNameMeaning
    2MatchPoints for each base that agrees with the consensus unit.
    7MismatchPenalty for a disagreeing base. 3 is permissive, 7 strict.
    7DeltaPenalty for an insertion or deletion.
    80PMExpected % identity between neighbouring copies (80 or 75; the detection statistics exist only for these).
    10PIExpected % indels between copies (10 or 20). 75/20 gives similar results but can take ten times longer.
    50MinscoreReport arrays with an alignment score of at least 50, e.g. 25 perfectly matching bases (5 copies of a 5-mer).
    500MaxPeriodLargest unit to report (1-2000).
    -d, -hoptions-d writes a plain-text .dat file (one line per array: start, end, period, copies, consensus size, % matches, % indels, score, A C G T %, entropy, consensus unit, sequence). -h stops the HTML pages (thousands for a genome) and switches -d on. -m writes a masked FASTA (N).
    -l 6max lengthLongest array expected, in millions of bp; default 2. TRF's own help says the human genome hg38 needs -l 6; more needs more memory.

    TRF reports one array at up to three period sizes (a (CA)n array can also appear as period 4 and 6). Remove these overlaps before you count satellite content.

    Other tandem-repeat tools

    ULTRA
    Olson & Wheeler 2024, current release v1.2.0 on GitHub (TravisWheelerLab/ULTRA), 1.2.1 on bioconda. Uses a hidden Markov model; the RepeatMasker help file names it as another option next to TRF. It reports where the unit changes inside an array (subrepeats), can estimate its own false-discovery rate by shuffling (--fdr) and tune itself to an unusual base composition (--tune). Default maximum period 100; for satellites set -p 500 or more. Example from its README: ultra --read_all -p 500 -t 8 -i 3 -d 3 -o out.tsv genome.fa; --mask masked.fa writes lowercase masking.
    TRASH
    Tandem Repeat Annotation and Structural Hierarchy (Wlodzimierz et al. 2023), built for centromeric satellites. It scores windows by their share of repeated k-mers, finds the period, maps every monomer, and can find higher-order repeats. Output: monomer and array tables (CSV) and GFF. The authors now point to TRASH_2 (GitHub vlothec/TRASH_2, rewritten, "early-development", needs R, MAFFT and nhmmer: TRASH.R -f genome.fa -o outdir -p 16; units 7-1000 bp by default).
    TideCluster
    Novák and colleagues (GitHub kavonrtep/TideCluster, version 1.22.3, October 2026). Finds arrays with TideHunter (a tandem-repeat finder first written for noisy long reads), groups arrays from all over the genome into families (default: at least 75 % identity over 80 % of the shorter array), optionally names them with RepeatMasker and a satellite library, and builds family consensus units with TAREAN. Reports per-array monomer size and higher-order structure. Default minimum array length 5 kb (-m). Example: TideCluster.py run_all -c 32 -f genome.fa -pr mygenome; --long searches units up to 25 kb.

    Rule of thumb: TRF or ULTRA for microsatellites and a first genome-wide table; TideCluster when you want satellite families across the genome; TRASH for monomers and higher-order structure of centromeric arrays. A satellite can be built from a piece of a SINE, so the same stretch may be called "SINE" by RepeatMasker and "satellite" by a tandem finder; both are right.

    In our projects: three lessons about tandem repeats and masks. (1) For the satellite study (CLsat workflow) TRF ran on whole genomes with 2 5 7 80 10 50 2000, keeping units of 80-300 bp with at least 10 copies as satellite candidates. (2) In the published genome of a close relative of our species A, most of the handful of most heterozygous 100-bp windows lay in (TA)n, (GT)n or (CT)n microsatellites: the reads showed two array lengths, and the variant caller wrote that length difference as a run of false "SNVs" (single-base variants) (a small fraction of a per cent of the heterozygous calls sat in such evenly spaced runs, more in that genome than in species A). Mask or filter tandem repeats before counting heterozygosity (09). (3) When we tested masks before demographic analysis, a RepeatMasker mask cut a population-size peak of that genome roughly in half, but a random mask of the same size also lowered it clearly. Much of the effect was simply less data. Always compare a mask with a size-matched random mask.

    05Evidence for genes: transcripts and proteins

    A gene predictor guesses where genes are from the DNA alone. It guesses much better when you also show it evidence: pieces of messenger RNA from the animal itself (RNA-seq, long-read transcripts) and proteins of related species laid onto the new genome. This section explains what a gene looks like in the genome, how each kind of evidence is produced and aligned, and which files the gene predictors of section 06 expect.

    What a gene looks like in the genome

    In a mammal, a protein-coding gene is not one continuous stretch of code. It is cut into pieces that are joined only in the RNA copy.

    Exon
    A piece of the gene that stays in the mature messenger RNA (mRNA, the RNA copy that leaves the nucleus and is translated into protein).
    Intron
    A piece between two exons that is copied into RNA and then cut out. Mammal introns are often thousands of bases long, many times longer than the exons.
    Splicing, splice sites
    Cutting out the introns and joining the exons is called splicing. Almost every intron begins with the two bases GT (the donor site) and ends with AG (the acceptor site). Rarer types are GC-AG and AT-AC. The point where two exons meet in the mRNA is a splice junction.
    CDS
    Coding sequence: the part of the mRNA that is translated, from the start codon (ATG, read as methionine) to the stop codon (TAA, TAG or TGA). It is read three bases (one codon) at a time, so its length is a multiple of 3.
    UTR
    Untranslated region: the parts of the mRNA before the start codon (5′ UTR) and after the stop codon (3′ UTR). They are in exons but do not code for protein. The first exon is often all UTR.
    Isoform
    One gene can be spliced in several ways, for example with or without one exon, or with a different last exon. Each version is an isoform (or transcript). Different tissues often make different isoforms.
    Simple explanation with examples: a recipe with adverts in the middle

    Think of a gene as a recipe printed in a magazine, interrupted by adverts. The recipe parts are the exons; the adverts are the introns. Before cooking, the cell cuts out the adverts and glues the recipe parts together (splicing). Every advert starts with the same two letters, GT, and ends with AG, so the scissors know where to cut.

    DNA ATGGCTCAGgtaagt...advert...ttttcagGTTGAACTGgtgag...advert...cctagCCTTAA mRNA ATGGCTCAGGTTGAACTGCCTTAA protein M A Q V E L P stop

    The title and the notes at the end of the recipe are not cooking steps: those are the UTRs. The cooking steps from "ATG" to the stop word are the CDS. A shortened version of the recipe, with one paragraph left out, is another isoform: liver might use the full recipe, testis the short one.

    Why this matters for annotation: in the genome you only see the magazine with the adverts. An RNA read that runs across a glued joint ("split read") proves exactly where an advert was cut out. A protein of a related species tells you which recipe parts should be there, but not the title page (UTR) and not which version each tissue uses.

    Three kinds of evidence

    1. Short-read RNA-seq
      mRNA is turned into cDNA, broken into fragments and sequenced as 100-150 bp Illumina reads. Many reads, cheap, but each read sees only a small part of one transcript. Reads that cross an exon-exon junction show the exact intron positions.
      tools: HISAT2 or STAR (mapping), StringTie (assembly)
    2. Long-read transcripts
      PacBio Iso-Seq or nanopore cDNA sequencing reads a whole mRNA molecule in one read, from 5′ end to poly(A) tail. One read shows a complete isoform, so no assembly is needed, but there are fewer reads per gene.
      tools: lima, isoseq, pbmm2 (PacBio); pychopper, minimap2 (nanopore)
    3. Proteins of other species
      Known protein sequences (from a close relative, or from a large database such as OrthoDB) are aligned to the genome across introns. They show coding exons and their borders, need no lab work, but say nothing about UTRs or tissue-specific isoforms, and miss genes your species has and the relatives do not.
      tools: miniprot; ProtHint inside BRAKER
    4. A related annotated genome
      Whole gene models of a relative projected onto your genome through a genome alignment. This is the topic of section 07 (Liftoff, TOGA2).

    Planning RNA-seq

    • Tissues. A gene leaves RNA evidence only where it is switched on. The human tissue atlas (Uhlén et al. 2015, Science 347:1260419) needed transcriptomes of 32 tissues and organs to detect more than 90 % of the protein-coding genes. For annotation, pool or sequence several different tissues of the same species rather than many replicates of one.
    • Preservation. RNA degrades fast. The ERGA pilot guidelines (v2) state that tissue in RNAlater is sufficient for RNA-seq, and the listed sequencing centres ask for total RNA with a RIN above 8 for Iso-Seq (most list 300-500 ng as the minimum).
    • Depth. There is no single agreed number of reads for annotation. Depth decides whether weakly expressed genes and rare isoforms get any split reads at all; the toy below shows this. More tissues usually help more than more reads from one tissue.
    • Stranded or unstranded. A stranded library keeps the information which DNA strand the RNA was copied from; an unstranded one loses it. Strand matters where genes overlap on opposite strands and for UTRs. The BRAKER README says strandedness is not very important for gene prediction without UTRs, because spliced reads are "artificially stranded" by their GT-AG motif, but stranded data help UTR prediction. If nobody tells you which kind you have, RSeQC infer_experiment.py measures it from mapped reads (it needs a gene model file, for a new species for example protein-based models of conserved genes).

    Mapping short reads: HISAT2 or STAR

    An RNA read that crosses a splice junction lies in two places on the genome, often thousands of bases apart. A normal DNA aligner (BWA, or minimap2 in genome mode) cannot place it. A spliced aligner splits the read and writes the gap as an intron. In the SAM file this is the CIGAR operator N: the HISAT2 manual's example read 90M200N10M means 90 bases match, then a 200-bp intron is skipped, then 10 more bases match. Such a read is a split read (or spliced read).

    Mask repeats first (section 04) and use the soft-masked genome; HISAT2 ignores lowercase masking, as the BRAKER README notes.

    # HISAT2 2.2.3 (2.2.1 is the version listed by BRAKER 3.0.8)
hisat2-build -p 16 genome.fa genome_idx            # index; about 8 GB RAM for a mammal-size genome without --ss/--exon
hisat2 -p 16 --dta -x genome_idx \
       -1 liver_R1.fq.gz -2 liver_R2.fq.gz \
       --novel-splicesite-outfile liver.junctions.txt \
  | samtools sort -@ 8 -o liver.bam
samtools index liver.bam
# stranded library: add --rna-strandness RF or FR (paired), R or F (single-end); ask the provider or measure it
    • --dta ("downstream transcriptome assembly"): HISAT2 asks for longer anchors before it accepts a new splice site, which helps StringTie. BRAKER's GeneMark-ETP runs StringTie on your BAM, so BRAKER requires --dta for HISAT2 alignments: spliced reads must carry the XS strand tag.
    • --rna-strandness: unstranded is the default. F/R for single-end, FR/RF for paired reads; the manual equates RF with TopHat's fr-firststrand.
    • Default maximum intron length 500,000 bp (--max-intronlen).
    # STAR 2.7.11b: build the index (mammal genomes: at least 16 GB RAM, ideally 32 GB, says the README)
STAR --runMode genomeGenerate --runThreadN 16 \
     --genomeDir star_idx --genomeFastaFiles genome.fa
# map one tissue, per-sample 2-pass mode
STAR --runThreadN 16 --genomeDir star_idx \
     --readFilesIn liver_R1.fq.gz liver_R2.fq.gz --readFilesCommand zcat \
     --twopassMode Basic --outSAMstrandField intronMotif \
     --outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate --outFileNamePrefix liver.
    • 2-pass mode. In pass 1 STAR finds junctions; it then inserts them into the index and maps all reads again. The manual says this "does not significantly increase the number of detected novel junctions, but allows to detect more spliced reads mapping to novel junctions", which is what a new genome without annotation needs. With several tissues you can instead collect every sample's SJ.out.tab and give them all to pass 2 with --sjdbFileChrStartEnd.
    • --outSAMstrandField intronMotif adds the XS strand tag (and drops reads with inconsistent or non-canonical introns). BRAKER asks for this option when the BAM comes from STAR.
    • SJ.out.tab lists every junction with its read counts; it is the quickest way to see how much splicing evidence you have.
    • For an assembly with more than 5,000 contigs the manual recommends lowering --genomeChrBinNbits to min(18, log2(max(genome length / number of contigs, read length))). For example, a 2.5 Gb assembly in 25,000 contigs gives log2(100,000) ≈ 16.6, so 16.

    Which one? Both write sorted BAM files that BRAKER accepts. HISAT2 needs less memory (the index fits on a desktop); STAR is the aligner NCBI's EGAPx pipeline uses for short RNA-seq reads. BRAKER itself runs HISAT2 when you give it FASTQ files instead of BAM.

    Long reads: whole transcripts in one read

    PacBio's current bulk Iso-Seq workflow (isoseq.how; isoseq 4.3.0/26.2.0, pbmm2 26.2) starts from HiFi reads, which already have predicted accuracy of at least Q20:

    • isoseq cluster2 replaces isoseq cluster (to be deprecated). It outputs isoforms supported by at least 2 FLNC reads; add --singletons to keep the rest.
    • pbmm2 is PacBio's wrapper around minimap2 that reads and writes PacBio BAM and comes with parameter presets; ISOSEQ is the preset for transcripts (its README lists -G maximum intron length and -C non-canonical splice cost as the extra settings you may change).

    Nanopore cDNA. Reads from cDNA kits come in both orientations and include primers. Pychopper (v2.7.10) finds the primers, keeps full-length reads and orients them (-k picks the kit's primers; PCS109 is the default, check that your kit is in its list). Then align with minimap2's spliced mode:

    Long reads in BRAKER. The standard BRAKER3 expects short reads. EGAPx (section 06) takes short and long RNA reads together.

    From alignments to transcripts and ORFs

    Mapped reads are not yet transcripts. A transcript assembler joins overlapping reads and their junctions into full isoform models.

    # StringTie 3.0.3 (the "StringTie2" algorithm, extended)
stringtie -p 8 -o liver.gtf liver.bam                    # short reads
stringtie -p 8 -L -o isoseq.gtf isoseq.bam               # long reads
stringtie -p 8 --mix -o mix.gtf liver.bam isoseq.bam     # both: short BAM first, long BAM second
stringtie --merge -o merged.gtf liver.gtf brain.gtf testis.gtf   # one non-redundant set across tissues

    Defaults worth knowing: minimum transcript length 200 bp (-m); minimum junction coverage 1 read (-j); isoforms below 1 % of the gene's most abundant isoform dropped (-f 0.01); --rf/--fr declare a stranded library. The README recommends at least 8 GB of RAM, 16 GB for large data sets.

    A transcript is not yet a protein. TransDecoder finds the most likely ORF in each transcript and writes it back to genome coordinates:

    # TransDecoder v6.0.0: full wrapper, genome + GTF mode
TransDecoder --genome genome.fa --gtf merged.gtf
# main output: merged.fasta.transdecoder.genome.gff3 (plus .pep, .cds, .bed on the transcripts)

    By default it reports ORFs of at least 100 amino acids (-m; the wiki warns that lowering it raises false positives sharply). It scores coding potential with a model it trains on your own data, so it needs a whole transcriptome (hundreds of candidates at least), not a handful of transcripts. Version 6.0.0 introduced this TransDecoder wrapper; older tutorials call TransDecoder.LongOrfs and TransDecoder.Predict separately.

    Pitfalls

    • Wrong genome version. RNA-seq mapped to an earlier assembly cannot be reused after scaffolding or polishing; contig names and coordinates change. Map again.
    • Missing XS tag. HISAT2 without --dta or STAR without --outSAMstrandField intronMotif: BRAKER's StringTie step needs the tag on spliced reads.
    • Contamination and organelles. RNA-seq contains many mitochondrial and ribosomal RNA reads; they map to NUMTs and repeats and create false "genes". The STAR manual suggests removing junctions in the mitochondrial genome and non-canonical junctions with few reads.
    • Expressed repeats. Active transposable elements are transcribed and spliced too. Masking repeats before prediction (section 04) keeps them from becoming gene models.
    • Absence of evidence. No reads on a gene means it is not expressed in the tissues you sampled, not that it is absent.

    06Predicting genes: BRAKER, GALBA, Helixer, EGAPx

    A gene predictor reads the repeat-masked genome and writes down where each protein-coding gene lies: where it starts, where every exon begins and ends, and where it stops. It combines three kinds of information: signals in the DNA itself, evidence from RNA and proteins (section 05), and parameters learned for your species. This section explains how that combination works, which pipeline to choose for which data, what the pipelines need and produce, and how to check the result.

    Words used in this section

    Gene model
    One predicted gene written as coordinates: a chain of exons joined by introns, with a start codon in the first coding exon and a stop codon in the last. A gene can have several transcripts (alternative splice forms); each is a separate chain.
    CDS
    Coding sequence: the part of the exons that is translated into protein, from the start codon (usually ATG) to the stop codon (TAA, TAG or TGA). Exon parts before and after it are the UTRs (untranslated regions).
    Splice sites
    The two ends of an intron. Almost all introns begin with GT (the donor site) and end with AG (the acceptor site). A rare class begins with GC.
    Ab initio prediction
    Latin for "from the beginning": predicting genes from the genome sequence alone, using only what a model has learned about how genes of that kind of organism look (codon usage, splice-site patterns, typical exon and intron lengths).
    Evidence, hints
    Extra information from outside the genome sequence: introns seen in spliced RNA-seq reads, proteins of other species aligned to the genome, repeat masking. AUGUSTUS calls each such piece a hint ("this interval is probably an intron", "this interval is probably coding").
    Training
    Fitting a predictor's parameters to one species. A predictor trained on human splice sites and codon usage works less well on a fish or an insect, so pipelines first build a set of reliable genes from the evidence and train on them.
    GTF / GFF3
    Two tab-separated text formats with one line per feature (gene, mRNA, exon, CDS) and 9 columns: sequence name, source, feature type, start, end, score, strand, phase, attributes. GTF links lines by gene_id/transcript_id; GFF3 by ID/Parent. Most pipelines below write one or both. Both count from 1; the toy in section 01 compares them with BED.

    How a classic predictor decides: AUGUSTUS

    Most pipelines in this section end with AUGUSTUS (current release 3.5.0) or a program built the same way. AUGUSTUS is a generalized hidden Markov model (the title of its 2006 hints paper). In plain words: it scores every possible way of cutting the DNA into intergenic region, exons and introns, and reports the most probable one. The scores come from a parameter set chosen with --species. AUGUSTUS ships 167 parameter sets (folders in config/species/), among them human, chicken, zebrafish and fly. For a new species, pipelines such as BRAKER and GALBA train a new set.

    Evidence enters as a hints file (16 hint types, for example intron, CDSpart, exonpart, start, stop). The AUGUSTUS manual describes the rule: a gene structure that agrees with a hint gets its probability multiplied by a bonus; each feature that has no supporting hint gets multiplied by a malus (penalty) below 1. Bonus and malus are set per hint type and per evidence source in an extrinsic configuration file. In the shipped example extrinsic.MPE.cfg, an RNA-derived intron hint (source E) has a bonus of 10,000 and a protein-derived CDSpart hint (source P) a bonus of 100,000. So evidence does not force a gene: it tilts the scores, and a large enough bonus wins against the sequence signals.

    Repeats enter through soft-masking. With --softmasking=1 AUGUSTUS treats lower-case bases as evidence against exons (a nonexonpart hint with a default bonus of 1.15 per base, which grows with the length of the masked stretch). The manual says this is slightly more accurate than hard masking with N and, on human, more than twice as fast. Two smaller rules worth knowing: AUGUSTUS predicts a GC donor site only where a hint supports it, and it reads every letter other than a, c, g, t (either case) as an unknown base.

    Score of a gene model = sum of the exons' sequence scores, minus 1 per intron, minus 2 per gene (a cost that stops the toy from calling genes everywhere). A gene is predicted only if its total is above 0. RNA-seq on: +weight for each intron that matches an RNA-seq intron exactly. Protein on: +weight for each exon that fully contains a protein hit. Masking on: −4 × (masked fraction of the exon). Frames, UTRs and the second strand are ignored. In the real programs the same idea runs over millions of candidates with probabilities instead of points.

    Simple explanation with examples: reading a recipe with the spaces removed

    Imagine a long text with all spaces and punctuation removed, in which a few recipes are hidden among pages of random letters. You know some rules: a recipe starts with "TAKE" and ends with "SERVE", and inside it the words look like cooking words. Reading only with these rules is ab initio prediction. You will find most recipes, but you will sometimes cut a sentence at the wrong place, skip a short step, or mistake a copied advertisement that also says "TAKE ... SERVE" for a recipe.

    Now a friend who has cooked some of the dishes marks with a pencil where steps really break (that is RNA-seq: it shows where introns were cut out in living cells), and you have a cookbook of a neighbouring country with similar dishes (that is protein evidence). You give the pencil marks some weight. A light weight lets the marks settle close calls; a heavy weight makes you follow them even where the text disagrees. The copied advertisements are the transposable elements: they really contain "TAKE ... SERVE", and the neighbouring cookbook may even contain the same advertisement, so heavy trust in the cookbook brings them back. That is why repeats are masked before prediction and repeat-derived genes are filtered afterwards.

    Which pipeline for which data

    1. RNA-seq of your species and a protein database
      BRAKER3 mode (called ETP). The developers call it the most accurate mode for short-read RNA-seq. Long-read RNA (Iso-Seq) plus proteins is a separate mode in BRAKER4.
    2. RNA-seq only
      BRAKER1 mode (ET). Each intron must be covered by many reads; the BRAKER guide warns that it does not work with an assembled transcriptome mapped to the genome.
    3. No RNA, but proteins
      BRAKER2 mode (EP) with a large protein database such as an OrthoDB partition (many relatives per gene family), or GALBA with the proteins of one or a few close relatives. GALBA's own guide says: if you have no RNA-seq and the genome is large, use GALBA; otherwise try BRAKER first.
    4. Nothing but the genome
      Helixer (a trained neural network, no training on your species needed). BRAKER can also run genome-only (ES mode), but its developers warn that this mode has the lowest accuracy and recommend protein mode instead.
    5. You want NCBI's own method and a GenBank submission
      EGAPx, the public version of NCBI's annotation pipeline. It picks proteins for your taxon automatically; RNA-seq is "highly recommended".
    6. A well-annotated close relative exists
      Projection of its genes onto your genome (Liftoff, TOGA2) is a different route; see section 07. It is often combined with the predictors here.

    BRAKER: three modes, one logic

    BRAKER (Greifswald and Georgia Tech) never predicts genes by itself. In BRAKER3 a third program, TSEBRA, merges the AUGUSTUS and GeneMark-ETP gene sets and keeps transcripts with strong support from introns and start/stop evidence.

    Helixer: a trained network, no evidence

    Helixer (Weber and Usadel labs; version 0.3.7, July 2026) is ab initio in a new way. A deep neural network, trained by its authors on annotated genomes of one lineage, gives every base a probability of being intergenic, UTR, coding or intron. A hidden Markov model step (HelixerPost) turns these probabilities into gene models. There is no training on your species and no evidence input. Four models exist: vertebrate, invertebrate, land_plant and fungi (the vertebrate model file is vertebrate_v0.3_m_0080.h5, released 2022).

    • Hardware. An NVIDIA GPU is "necessary for acceptable performance" on real genomes; the models run on GPUs with 8-11 GB of memory. A web tool (plabipd.de) takes genomes up to 1 GB per file, with every sequence at least 25 kb.

    EGAPx: NCBI's pipeline to run yourself

    EGAPx (version 1.0.1, September 2026) is the public version of the NCBI Eukaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline that builds RefSeq annotations. You give a YAML file with the genome, the taxid and RNA-seq.     In our projects

    For our rodent species A (not yet published, so no name or exact numbers here) we had no RNA-seq, so none of the RNA-based modes was possible; gene models came from projecting human genes with TOGA2 (section 07). What we learned there applies to every predictor in this section. Our assembly is built from long reads that miscount long runs of one letter (homopolymers). Inside the projected exons, the reads contradicted the assembly at hundreds of short insertions or deletions; nearly all of them shift the reading frame and the great majority sit in homopolymers. For 6-bp homopolymers in exons, most of the reads agreed with the assembled length in only a minority of tracts, and in even fewer for C/G runs than for A/T runs. Such read-contradicted frameshifts were almost absent from the "fully intact" TOGA2 genes but hit about one in ten of the "uncertain loss" genes.

    A false frameshift breaks a gene model whichever program builds it: TOGA2 calls the gene lost, while a predictor has to end the gene early, jump frames with an extra intron, or split it (EGAPx would count it as a "major correction"). We did not run BRAKER or EGAPx on this assembly, so we have no measured numbers for them. The lesson we draw: polish before you predict (lesson 2, section 06), and when a long-read annotation shows many broken genes, suspect the bases before the biology.

    07Annotation by projection: Liftoff and TOGA2

    If a related species already has a good gene annotation, you can carry its genes over to your assembly. Each gene of the annotated genome is located in your genome, and its exons are aligned there. This is called projection. It works without RNA-seq, and it also tells you which genes look broken in your genome. That second answer is the one that needs the most care.

    Words used in this section

    Reference
    The annotated genome you copy genes from (for us: human, or our own species A). Both tools call it the "reference".
    Query / target
    Your new, unannotated assembly. TOGA2 says "query", Liftoff says "target".
    Projection
    One reference transcript placed at one locus of the query. In TOGA2 a projection is one transcript plus one alignment chain, named transcript#GENE#chainID. One gene can have several projections (several loci, or one gene split over several contigs).
    Ortholog, paralog
    The same gene in two species, inherited from their common ancestor, versus an extra copy made by duplication (see lesson 2, BUSCO). Projection tools must decide which copy in the query is the true ortholog.
    Processed pseudogene
    A copy made when a spliced mRNA was reverse-transcribed and inserted back into the genome. It has the exons joined, without introns, and usually no promoter. It can look like an intact gene in the wrong place. If its reading frame is intact, TOGA2 calls it a retrogene candidate.
    Reading frame
    The grouping of coding bases into codons (three bases each). Inserting or deleting a number of bases that is not a multiple of 3 shifts the frame: a frameshift. Every codon after it is read wrongly and a stop codon soon appears.

    Step 1: a whole-genome alignment, as chains

    TOGA2 needs to know which part of the reference lines up with which part of the query, over the whole genome. This information is stored as chains. The UCSC Genome Browser team, who defined the format, explain it like this:

    • A gapless block is a base-for-base alignment of a piece of the reference with a piece of the query, of equal length (mismatches allowed).
    • A chain is a series of gapless blocks in the same order and on the same strand in both genomes, with gaps between them. In a single-sided gap sequence is skipped in only one genome (an insertion in one, or a deletion in the other). In a double-sided gap sequence is skipped in both (the two pieces differ too much to align).
    • A net (made by the UCSC program chainNet) is a hierarchy of chains: the best chain on top, lower-scoring chains filling its gaps. It keeps one alignment per reference region and removes pile-ups. Nets are used for liftOver of coordinates. TOGA2 takes the chain file itself as input: it wants all candidate chains, because choosing between them is part of its job.

    How the Hiller lab makes chains. Their pipeline make_lastz_chains (v3.1.7, August 2026) runs LASTZ on pieces of both genomes, builds chains with axtChain, fills chain gaps with a more sensitive second alignment (RepeatFiller), and removes doubtful local alignments (chainCleaner). The final file is 07_final/<ref>.<query>.allfilled.chain.gz.

    Liftoff: same or closely related species

    Liftoff (Shumate & Salzberg 2020; release 1.6.3, PyPI 1.6.3.2) maps a GFF or GTF annotation between assemblies of the same species or of closely related species. It does not need chains. It cuts each gene's sequence out of the reference and aligns it to the target with minimap2. Then, for each gene, it chooses the exon alignments that give the highest identity while keeping the transcript structure. When two genes land on overlapping loci, it decides which one is mis-mapped and tries to place that one elsewhere.

    # argument order: TARGET first, then REFERENCE
liftoff -g ref.gff3 -o target.gff3 -u unmapped_features.txt -p 16 \
        -copies -sc 0.95 target.fa ref.fa
#   -a 0.5 / -s 0.5 (defaults): a gene counts as mapped if its exons/CDS align with
#                coverage >= 50 % and identity >= 50 %; weaker hits stay in the GFF with
#                partial_mapping=True / low_identity=True unless -exclude_partial is set
#   -copies      look for extra gene copies not annotated in the reference
#   -sc 0.95     identity needed in exons to call an extra copy (default 1.0; must be > -s)
#   -chroms chroms.txt   lift chromosome by chromosome (lines "refchr,targetchr"); advised for
#                        chromosome-level assemblies of the same species
#   -flank 0.1   also align 10 % of flanking sequence; can help when gene structure differs
#   -polish      re-align exons to repair lost start/stop codons and in-frame stops
#                (writes target.gff3 and target.gff3_polished)

    Outputs: the target annotation in the same format as the input, and unmapped_features.txt. Each gene gets sequence_ID (exon identity to the reference), coverage and valid_ORFs. Each transcript gets flags: valid_ORF, matches_ref_protein, missing_start_codon, missing_stop_codon, inframe_stop_codon. Extra copies carry extra_copy_number.

    Limits, from the README: Liftoff works best when gene structure (for example intron sizes) is similar; when genes differ much in size, alignments fragment and small first or last exons are often lost. For more divergent species the README suggests raising minimap2's -r and -z through -mm2_options="-r 2k -z 5000". Liftoff reports whether the reading frame is valid, but it does not classify orthologs against paralogs or call gene losses. Use it to move an annotation between two assemblies of one species (an old and a new assembly, or two haplotypes), or between very close species.

    TOGA2: orthologs and gene losses from chains

    TOGA2 (Hiller lab; v2.0.10, September 2026) is the successor of TOGA (Kirilenko et al. 2023 Science). TOGA itself is no longer maintained. TOGA2 does three jobs in one run: it annotates genes in the query, decides which locus is the ortholog of each reference gene, and classifies each gene as intact, lost or missing.

    1. Inputs
      Reference and query genomes as .2bit files, the chain file, the reference annotation as BED12, and three "semi-mandatory" inputs: an isoform table (gene → transcripts), a U12 intron file, and SpliceAI predictions for the query. Since v2.0.7 you must either give each of these or set its placeholder flag (--no_isoform_file, --no_u12_file, --no_spliceai). Without an isoform file there is no gene-level result.
    2. Which chain is the ortholog?
      For every transcript–chain pair TOGA2 measures features such as: the share of the transcript's coding bases the chain aligns (gl_exo, loc_exo), the share of intron bases it aligns (intr_perc), how much of 10 kb on each side of the gene it aligns (flank_cov), and how many other reference genes the same chain covers (synt, for synteny). An orthologous locus keeps its introns and neighbours aligned; a paralog or a processed pseudogene usually does not. Two XGBoost models (single-exon and multi-exon genes) turn the features into an orthology probability; 0.5 or more counts as orthologous. Processed pseudogenes are recognised by a rule: exons aligned but introns not. A chain that spans the gene without aligning any coding base is a spanning chain.
    3. Realign each exon
      Within each locus, CESAR2 aligns every reference exon to the query, keeping the reading frame and the splice sites in view. Each exon is then Present, Missing (outside the chain, or in an assembly gap; 90 % or more N) or Deleted (neither).
    4. List the mutations
      Frameshifting insertions and deletions, premature stop codons, broken splice sites (not GT/GC…AG), and deleted or missing exons. Some are masked: recorded, but not used to call a loss (rules below).
    5. Classify
      Each projection gets a status. A transcript takes the best status among its projections, and a gene the best among its transcripts, in the order FI > I > PI > UL > L > M > (PG, PP) > N. So a gene is "lost" only if every projection is lost. Finally TOGA2 groups overlapping projections into query genes and names the orthology class: one2one, one2many, many2one, many2many, one2zero.

    Masking: which mutations do not count

    Frameshifts and stop codons near the ends of genes are common in conserved, working genes. TOGA2 therefore masks (records but ignores) these:

    • Mutations in the last 10 % of the reference coding sequence (C-terminal), always.
    • Mutations in the first 10 % (N-terminal) only if the query has another in-frame start codon (ATG) downstream of the mutation, still within the first 10 %. With --mask_n_terminal_mutations (-m10m), all mutations in the first 10 % are masked.
    • Two or more frameshifts that compensate each other (they add up to a multiple of 3 and there is no stop codon in the shifted stretch).
    • Deletion of a whole exon whose length is a multiple of 3 (frame-preserving); mutations inside Missing or Deleted exons; non-canonical splice sites of known U12 introns; missing start or stop codons.

    The unmasking rule. In the source code (processed_segment.py, v2.0.10), masks for the first and last 10 % and for compensated frameshifts are removed again when the projection has at least one other unmasked inactivating mutation. The wiki explains the reason: a gene with one frameshift in the middle plus a terminal stop has more evidence of loss than a gene with the frameshift alone.

    Simple explanation with examples: copying a recipe book from a neighbour

    Your neighbour has a recipe book with every recipe indexed. You have a shelf of the same recipes in a different order, and no index. Projection means: for each recipe in the neighbour's index, find the matching page on your shelf and copy the page numbers into a new index.

    Why every "lost" gene needs checking

    TOGA2's L means: in this assembly, no projection keeps an intact reading frame. Several things other than real gene loss give the same result:

    • Base errors in the assembly. A 1-base indel in a homopolymer (a run of one base, like GGGGG) is a frameshift. Long-read assemblies carry such errors unless polished (lesson 2, polishing). One error gives UL; two in different exons give L.
    • Assembly gaps. TOGA2 calls an exon Missing, and a gene spanned by an unaligned chain gap M rather than L, only when the query sequence there contains Ns. Gaps still matter for every follow-up test (see the flank example below).
    • The other haplotype. A primary assembly holds one copy of each region; the intact allele may be in the other haplotype.
    • Paralogs and gene families. Large families of similar genes, such as immunoglobulin and T-cell receptor segments, make the ortholog hard to pick.
    • Real divergence. An exon too different to align can be reported as deleted.

    Look at inactivating_mutations.tsv for each candidate: are the events in homopolymers? Do the reads agree with the assembly at those bases? Is there a gap nearby? Only events that survive these checks support a loss. The same base errors also break gene models from ab initio predictors (section 06), and BUSCO completeness does not reveal them (section 08).

It stays lost even if the G run is corrected. The toy's "three events" example copies this pattern.
    • Species A → a published close relative, our own run. Human-vs-relative LASTZ was abandoned: one test pair (human chr21 against a 17-Mb piece) took more than 1.6 CPU-hours, so the whole genome would take weeks. For the close pair we used minimap2 (-c --cs -x asm10) and turned its output into chains with transanno minimap2chain; that tool gives every chain the score 4900, so chains had to be rescored (chainScore -linearGap=medium, then chainSort). This is experimental: minimap2 chains are not validated for TOGA2. The reference was the intact species A transcripts from the Hiller run, so genes lost in species A could not be tested. Among the gene results, the largest group was N: reference transcripts rejected at setup (thousands had a coding length not divisible by 3), which says nothing about the relative's genome.
    • Lost is not deleted (a flank test). To ask whether an L or M gene is really gone, we use the sequence flanking the gene as anchors in the relative's genome (close species only): if both flanks are found next to each other with nothing between, the site is empty. Of several hundred L+M genes, about a third were found present, similar numbers had no anchor or only one flank, and just 2 looked empty; many lie in immunoglobulin and T-cell receptor segments, where flanks are not unique. Both "empty sites" were false: a 100-N scaffolding gap lay a few hundred bases away. HiFi reads spanning the site confirmed a homozygous deletion for one gene; two others look heterozygous (reads support both versions; a paralog is not excluded).

    Which tool when. Same species, new assembly or other haplotype: Liftoff. Close or distant species with a good reference annotation, and you also want orthologs and loss calls: TOGA2 (needs chains; for distant pairs LASTZ chains are the tested input). In both cases the projected genes are only as good as the bases of your assembly: polish first. Projected genes can also serve as evidence for gene prediction (section 05, section 06).

    08Naming genes and checking the annotation

    Gene prediction (section 06) and projection (section 07) give you coordinates and protein sequences with labels like g1234.t1. This section gives those proteins names and functions, and then asks whether the whole gene set is believable before anyone uses it or you submit it.

    Three kinds of evidence for a name

    Domains
    A protein domain is a part of a protein that folds on its own and does one job, for example binding DNA or cutting a peptide bond. The same domain turns up in many different proteins. Finding a known domain tells you what kind of protein you have ("a kinase", "a 7-transmembrane receptor"), but not exactly which one. Tool: InterProScan.
    Orthology
    Your protein is placed in a group of orthologs from many species, and the name and functions recorded for the closest members of that group are copied over. This tells you which gene it is, if the group is resolved finely enough. Tool: eggNOG-mapper.
    Best similar known protein
    Search your protein against a set of well-studied proteins and copy the name of the best hit. Simple and fast, but the best hit can be a paralog (a related gene of the same family), so the name can be wrong in a way that looks right. Tools: BLASTP or DIAMOND against Swiss-Prot.

    If your genes came from TOGA2 projection (section 07), each one already carries the human (or other reference) gene symbol of its ortholog. The tools below are still useful for the genes that projection does not cover, and as an independent check.

    The order of work

    1. One protein per gene
      Most predictors write several isoforms per gene. Keep the longest one for counting and naming, translate its CDS, and remove the final stop codon (*), which some tools reject.
      AGAT: agat_sp_keep_longest_isoform.pl, agat_sp_extract_sequences.pl
    2. Domains and GO terms
      InterProScan on all proteins.
      InterProScan 6.0.3.0
    3. Orthology-based names, GO and KEGG
      eggNOG-mapper on the same proteins.
      eggNOG-mapper 3.0.0-beta6 (eggNOG 7) or 2.1.x (eggNOG 5)
    4. Best hit in Swiss-Prot
      DIAMOND blastp; gives a second opinion on the name.
      DIAMOND 2.2.8
    5. Write names and terms into the GFF3
      Merge the results into the annotation file (GFF3); decide on one product name per gene.
      AGAT: agat_sp_manage_functional_annotation.pl
    6. Check the gene set
      BUSCO in protein mode, OMArk, AGAT statistics, gene counts against a relative, a look in a genome browser.
    7. Submit
      GFF3 + FASTA through table2asn to GenBank, with a registered locus_tag prefix.

    Step 3: eggNOG-mapper, names copied from orthologs

    eggNOG is a database of orthologous groups with functional terms. eggNOG-mapper finds the closest eggNOG protein to each of yours (the seed ortholog), takes the orthologs of that seed, and copies their gene name, GO terms, KEGG orthologs and pathways, EC numbers, Pfam domains and more. Its authors' point is that transferring from orthologs avoids taking a name from a close paralog, which a simple best hit can do.

    Where the three sources disagree, look before you choose. A common rule: take the ortholog name if eggNOG-mapper and the Swiss-Prot hit agree; if only a domain is found, name the protein by the domain ("zinc finger protein", "ABC transporter domain-containing protein"); if nothing is found, call it uncharacterized protein.

    Naming rules you will meet

    • Product names for GenBank must be concise names, not descriptions. For a protein of unknown function NCBI accepts hypothetical protein or uncharacterized protein. A CDS without a product attribute is named "hypothetical protein" automatically by table2asn.
    • LOC numbers. In NCBI Gene a gene with no official symbol gets LOC + its GeneID, for example LOC12345 = GeneID 12345. When a real symbol is assigned later, the LOC name is replaced. You do not invent LOC names yourself; they appear when NCBI annotates a genome (RefSeq) or when your submission is processed.
    • NCBI's own pipeline names predicted genes by similarity to Swiss-Prot proteins. Most of its models that need an insertion, deletion or frameshift to keep the reading frame are labelled pseudogenes; those kept as coding get the title prefix PREDICTED: LOW QUALITY PROTEIN, because the assembly under them may have base errors. That is exactly the homopolymer problem of section 06.
    • Gene symbols. Copying a human or mouse symbol (for example from TOGA2 or eggNOG-mapper) is normal for mammals. Add nothing that claims more than the evidence: a symbol from a one-to-many orthology group means "one of the copies", not "the" gene.
    Simple explanation with examples: naming strangers in an old class photo

    You find a school photo with forty unlabelled faces and want a name under each.

    • Uniform (InterProScan). One child wears a football shirt: you know he played football, not his name. A domain tells you the kind of protein, for example "has a kinase domain", but there are hundreds of kinases.
    • Family tree (eggNOG-mapper). You have the family photo albums of the town. You find the child's parents and siblings, and the family records say this was the second son, Peter. Orthology places the protein in the right family and copies the name from close relatives.
    • Look-alike (best BLAST hit). You flip through a book of famous people and pick the face that looks most alike. Often right, but cousins look alike: the best hit can be a paralog, a related gene of the same family with another job.
    • "Unknown boy, back row" (uncharacterized protein). If no clue works, say so. A wrong name is worse than no name, because others will copy it.

    Step 6: is the gene set believable?

    None of the checks below proves an annotation is right. Each one catches a different kind of mistake, and each is only meaningful next to the same number for a well-annotated relative, measured the same way.

    BUSCO in protein mode

    BUSCO was introduced on the assembly in lesson 2, section 10. In protein mode it scores your predicted proteins instead of the genome: the FASTA goes straight to HMMER, with no gene finding. Use the one-protein-per-gene file; the BUSCO 6.1.0 user guide warns that protein sets not filtered for isoforms give many false duplicates.

    busco -i proteins.faa -m proteins -l mammalia_odb12.2 -c 16 -o genome_prot \
      --download_path /data/busco_downloads --offline --opt-out-run-stats

    The most useful number is the difference between genome mode and protein mode with the same dataset. Genome mode says which marker genes are in the assembly; protein mode says which of them your annotation found. If the genome scores 98 % and the proteins 90 %, about 8 % of the markers are in the DNA but the annotation missed or broke them, and so are many other genes.

    OMArk: is every protein a real member of a known family?

    OMArk (Nevers et al. 2025, Nature Biotechnology; version 0.5.0) looks at the whole proteome, not only a few thousand marker genes. Every protein is first placed in a gene family of the OMA database by the fast k-mer tool OMAmer. OMArk then reports:

    Completeness
    Like BUSCO, but on conserved HOGs of your lineage: single, duplicated (expected or unexpected, from the known history of the family) and missing.
    Consistent
    The protein belongs to a family known in your lineage. Sub-labels: partial hit (similar to the family over only part of its length; in the code, less than 80 % overlap) and fragmented (shorter than half the median length of that family). Both point to broken gene models.
    Inconsistent
    The protein matches families known from other lineages, "seemingly at random" in the words of the OMArk summary. Look at these proteins before trusting them.
    Contaminant
    Many proteins match the same foreign species more than expected by chance, for example a bacterium or a parasite. Compare with FCS-GX on the assembly (lesson 2, section 09).
    Unknown
    No similarity to any known family: an orphan gene, or a model built on non-coding DNA, for example an open reading frame inside a transposable element or a random stretch of intergenic sequence.

    • Gene count. Mammals share most protein-coding genes. A count far above a close relative usually means split genes (one gene predicted as two or three pieces), genes on haplotigs, or genes built from transposable elements. Far below: merged neighbours, or genes missed.
    • Single-exon (mono-exonic) genes. Real ones exist: most olfactory receptor genes have their coding sequence in one exon, and retrocopies have no introns. But open reading frames of LINE and LTR retrotransposons, and spurious predictions, are also single-exon. A share clearly higher than in the relative needs explaining.
    • Transposable-element proteins.     Agreement with evidence: AED and transcript support

    When RNA-seq or protein alignments exist (section 05), ask how many gene models they support. MAKER reports for each model an annotation edit distance (AED, Eilbeck et al. 2009): 0 means the model agrees completely with the aligned evidence, 1 means no evidence supports it. A rule of thumb from the MAKER developers is that a set is good when more than about 95 % of models have AED below 0.5; it is a mailing-list heuristic, not a published standard. Outside MAKER, the same idea is the share of models whose introns are all confirmed by spliced RNA-seq reads: compare the intron coordinates of each model with the splice junctions found by the RNA-seq aligner. Without any RNA evidence, as for our species A, this check is not available, and orthology-based checks carry more weight.

    Look at it

    Numbers hide patterns that a picture shows at once. Load the genome, the gene GFF3, the repeat annotation and any RNA-seq BAM into IGV or JBrowse 2, and look at about twenty random genes plus the BUSCO genes reported as fragmented or missing. Look for exons sitting inside repeats, one gene split in two at a contig end, two neighbouring genes fused into one, introns that do not match the spliced reads, and stop codons or frameshifts in homopolymer runs.

    09Heterozygosity and runs of homozygosity from one genome

    One diploid animal carries two copies of every autosome, one from its mother and one from its father. Where the two copies differ, the animal is heterozygous. How often they differ, and where they do not differ at all for millions of bases, tells you about the population the animal came from: how large it was, and whether the parents were related. This section shows how to get both numbers from the reads and the assembly you already have, which tools to use, and how the numbers go wrong.

    What we measure

    Heterozygous site
    A position where the two copies of a chromosome carry different bases, written 0/1 in a VCF. A single-base difference is a SNV (single-nucleotide variant).
    Heterozygosity
    Heterozygous SNVs divided by the number of bases where a call could be made at all (the callable genome, explained below). Usually given per kb (1.9 per kb = 0.19 %). This is the per-site number; it is not the same as the k-mer heterozygosity of GenomeScope (lesson 1), which is estimated from reads with errors and repeats in them.
    Run of homozygosity (ROH)
    A long stretch where the two copies are (nearly) identical, because both were inherited from one ancestor without being broken by recombination. Such a stretch is autozygous or identical by descent (IBD): the same piece of one ancestor's chromosome, received twice.
    FROH
    The share of the autosomal genome in runs of homozygosity above a chosen length (McQuillan et al. 2008). With one genome it is the closest thing to an inbreeding coefficient you can measure. Always state the minimum run length and what the denominator was.
    Callable genome
    The bases where reads were good enough that a heterozygous site, if present, would have been seen: enough depth, not too much depth, reads mapped uniquely. Everything else is unknown, not homozygous.
    Simple explanation with examples: why related parents leave long identical stretches

    Think of each chromosome as a long text copied by hand from generation to generation, with a cut-and-paste between the mother's and the father's version at a few random places in every generation (recombination). Two unrelated parents pass on texts with many small differences along their whole length. If the parents are cousins, both received pieces of the same grandparent's text. Their child can get the same piece twice, once through each parent, and there the two copies match letter for letter.

    mother's copy ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGGATCCAGTTACAGGCTACCTAGT father's copy ACGTTAGCATCACCTTAGACGTTAGGATCCAGTTACAGGCTACCGAGT ^ het |------- ROH: same ancestor -------| ^ het

    The closer the shared ancestor, the fewer generations of cutting the piece went through, so the longer it is. A first-cousin mating leaves pieces of tens of megabases; an ancestor shared 500 generations ago leaves pieces of a few hundred kilobases or less. A small population, where everyone is distantly related, fills the genome with many short runs; a recent close mating adds a few very long ones.

    The workflow

    1. Map the reads
      Reads of the same animal onto its own primary assembly (or onto a close reference).
      minimap2 · bwa-mem2 · samtools
    2. Call variants
      A caller matched to the read type, with output that also tells you which homozygous positions were looked at.
      DeepVariant · Clair3 · bcftools mpileup/call
    3. Build the callable mask and filter
      Depth bounds, mapping quality, sex chromosomes, organelle contigs, simple repeats; biallelic SNVs only.
      mosdepth · bcftools · bedtools
    4. Heterozygosity in windows
      Het SNVs per callable base, in windows along each contig.
      bedtools makewindows / coverage
    5. Call ROH
      An HMM, a sliding window, or a mixture model on the window densities.
      bcftools roh · PLINK --homozyg · ROHan · window mixture
    6. Summarise and plot
      FROH by length class, genome strip, length histogram, window heterozygosity histogram.
      your own script, or the toy below

    1. Mapping: which assembly to map to

    Map to the primary (pseudo-haploid) assembly of the same individual, after haplotig purging (lesson 2, s06). Each read then has one place to go, and both alleles of a heterozygous site pile up at the same position. If both haplotypes of a region are still in the assembly as separate contigs (unpurged haplotigs), the reads split between them, each copy looks homozygous, and you get a false ROH and a mapping-quality drop at the same place. A reference from another species works too, but reads from divergent regions map poorly, and your heterozygosity is then measured only where the species are similar.

    # long reads, minimap2 2.31: map-ont (ONT), map-hifi (PacBio HiFi), lr:hq (ONT Q20+, since v2.27)

samtools index ont.bam

    3. Callable mask and filters

    The single most important step. A window with no heterozygous sites is homozygous only if it was callable; a stretch with no reads, or with reads of mapping quality 0, also has no heterozygous sites. The mask lists the bases where reads were trustworthy; every later count is divided by it.

    • Depth bounds. Keep bases with depth roughly between half and twice the autosomal median. Too low: a heterozygous site can be missed. Too high: collapsed duplications and repeats, where differences between copies look like heterozygous sites.
    • Mapping quality. Count only reads with MAPQ ≥ 10 to 20 (the mapping quality of reads in repeats is low).
    • Sex chromosomes. In the heterogametic sex the X (or Z) is single-copy and must not count as homozygous autosome. Find these contigs by half depth (lesson 2, s12) and leave them out. Do the same for mitochondrial and NUMT-like contigs.
    • Sites. Biallelic SNVs that pass the caller's filter. For a heterozygous call, the share of reads with the second allele (allele fraction) should be near 0.5; a fraction near 0.1 to 0.3 is often an error or a paralog.
    • Simple repeats. Microsatellites with two length alleles can be called as a tight cluster of "SNVs". Masking them changes little genome-wide but removes the hottest windows.
    # mosdepth 0.3.15: depth classes, MAPQ >= 10. Example for an autosomal median of 30x: callable = 15 to 54x
export MOSDEPTH_Q0=LOW MOSDEPTH_Q1=CALLABLE MOSDEPTH_Q2=HIGH
mosdepth -n -Q 10 --quantize 0:15:55: -t 4 cov ont.bam
zcat cov.quantized.bed.gz | awk '$4=="CALLABLE"' | grep -v -w -f sex_and_mt_contigs.txt > callable.bed

# biallelic PASS SNVs inside the mask (a .bed file given to -T is read as 0-based)
bcftools view -f PASS -v snps -m2 -M2 -T callable.bed -Oz -o snv.vcf.gz clair3_out/merge_output.vcf.gz

# heterozygous sites with allele fraction 0.3-0.7, as BED
bcftools query -i 'GT="het"' -f '%CHROM\t%POS[\t%AD]\n' snv.vcf.gz \
  | awk -F'\t' '{split($3,a,","); f=a[2]/(a[1]+a[2]); if(f>=0.3 && f<=0.7) print $1"\t"$2-1"\t"$2}' > het.bed

    Mosdepth's --quantize bins are half-open (15:55 means 15 to 54); the environment variables only rename the bins. Check the -v/-m2 -M2 choice against your caller: some write multiallelic sites on one line.

    4. Heterozygosity along the genome

    Cut each contig into windows, count heterozygous sites in each, and divide by the callable bases of that window, not by the window length. Windows with little callable sequence (for example under half) are dropped.

    het per kb (window) = heterozygous SNVs in window ÷ callable bases in window × 1000
    samtools faidx asm.fa && cut -f1,2 asm.fa.fai > genome.txt
bedtools makewindows -g genome.txt -w 50000 > win.bed
bedtools coverage -a win.bed -b callable.bed > win.callable.tsv   # col 5 = callable bases
bedtools coverage -a win.bed -b het.bed -counts > win.het.tsv     # col 4 = het count
paste win.callable.tsv win.het.tsv | awk '$5>=25000 {print $1,$2,$3,$11/$5*1000}' > het_per_kb.tsv

    If you assembled both haplotypes (hifiasm hap1 and hap2), you can also count differences between them without reads: minimap2 -cx asm5 --cs hap1.fa hap2.fa | sort -k6,6 -k8,8n | paftools.js call -. The defaults of paftools.js call use only alignments of at least 50 kb for calling (-L 50000) and mapping quality ≥ 5 (-q 5). It is a cross-check, not a replacement: it misses what the assembler collapsed.

    Try it

    Make an inbred toy chromosome, then find its ROH

    A 100-Mb chromosome pair of one animal. Outside autozygous segments the two copies differ at a rate you set; inside them only false heterozygous calls (sequencing and mapping errors) remain. Top controls write the true history: how many generations ago the shared ancestor lived and how many pieces of its chromosome this animal inherited twice, plus many short old segments from an ancient small population. Segment lengths are drawn from an exponential distribution with mean 100/(2g) cM (Thompson 2013), converted at 1 cM per Mb. Bottom controls are your ROH caller: window size, the heterozygosity threshold, and the minimum run length.

    What to notice. (1) A recent ancestor (g below about 10) gives a few runs of many megabases; move g to 100 and the same number of ancestors leaves only short runs, many below what a 1-Mb window can see. (2) Old short segments are found only with small windows; the minimum-run filter throws them away, so FROH "above 1 Mb" and "above 100 kb" answer different questions. (3) Raise the false-het rate and windows inside true ROH climb over the threshold: long runs break into pieces. (4) Switch the denominator to "window length" with gaps on: every gap becomes a false ROH. (5) Press "new animal" a few times with the same g: run lengths vary a lot, because the exponential distribution has a standard deviation as large as its mean.

    6. Length classes, age and FROH

    For a common ancestor g generations back, the two copies are separated by 2g meioses, and the IBD segments they share have exponentially distributed lengths with mean 100/(2g) cM (Thompson 2013: "a separation of m meioses provides segment lengths that are exponentially distributed with mean 100/m cM"). Turned around, a run of L cM points to an ancestor roughly g ≈ 100/(2L) generations back.

    g ≈ 100 / (2 × LcM) = 50 / (LMb × r) r = recombination rate in cM per Mb
    Run lengthg at r = 1 cM/Mbg at r = 0.5 cM/MbUsually read as
    ≥ 5 Mb≤ 10≤ 20Recent inbreeding: related parents a few generations back
    1 to 5 Mb10 to 5020 to 100Small recent population size
    100 kb to 1 Mb50 to 500100 to 1,000Old, long-term small population or old bottleneck

    Use these numbers only as orders of magnitude. The length of one run varies as much as its mean (Thompson 2013), so single runs cannot be dated, only the distribution. The recombination rate of most wild species is unknown, and it varies along chromosomes: a recombination cold spot or a region under selection also keeps low diversity without any inbreeding. Runs of up to 4 Mb are common even in demonstrably outbred people (McQuillan et al. 2008), so a few runs of a few Mb do not by themselves mean inbreeding.

    FROH = total length of runs ≥ Lmin ÷ callable autosomal length. Report it per length class (for example under 100 kb, 100 to 500 kb, 0.5 to 1 Mb, 1 to 5 Mb, 5 Mb or more), because methods and papers use different minimums, and compare species only at matched classes.

    Three figures that show it

    • Genome strip: window heterozygosity along each chromosome or the largest contigs, with the called runs drawn as bars underneath, the same scale for every genome (the toy above is one strip).
    • Length histogram: bases in ROH per length class on a log length axis, with the detection floor (one window) and, for a contig assembly, the contig N50 marked; a second axis can show generations for an assumed recombination rate.
    • Window heterozygosity histogram: the near-zero group of windows (the ROH) next to the main hump; this is the distribution the mixture model splits.

    Pitfalls

    • Too many hets. Errors, paralogs and collapsed duplications add heterozygous sites. Warning signs: allele fractions far from 0.5, the ratio of transitions to transversions (Ts/Tv) of het SNVs well below about 2 in mammals, clusters of hets in high-depth windows.
    • Too few hets. Low callability (gaps, repeats, MAPQ-0 regions) counted as homozygous; haplotigs left in the assembly; hard QUAL filters tuned for another depth.
    • Sex chromosomes. A male's X has no true heterozygous sites outside the PAR. Left in, it adds tens of Mb of "ROH"; used on purpose, it is a free false-positive control.
    • Contig ends. In a contig assembly a run cannot be longer than its contig, so long runs are cut into shorter ones; the share of ROH bases in runs touching a contig end shows how much.
    • Reference vs own assembly. Mapping to another species' genome loses divergent regions; mapping to your own assembly inherits its errors (indels in homopolymers are rarely heterozygous in reality).
    • A two-state model on noisy data can put most of a genome into "ROH". If FROH exceeds what the window heterozygosity histogram shows, believe the histogram.
    In our projects

    Our rodent species A (not yet published, so no name or exact numbers here): one male, long reads at a few tens of × coverage, hifiasm primary assembly; called with Clair3 1.x and an ONT R10.4.1 model. On a test set of a few contigs, het SNPs were found at roughly two per kb, and most het calls had allele fractions between 0.35 and 0.65, but Ts/Tv was clearly below the expected 2.0 to 2.2, pointing to transversion-rich false calls. Hard QUAL/AF filters changed the het density several-fold, so fixed QUAL thresholds were not used.

    The X as a control: on the half-depth X contigs, true hets should not exist. Unfiltered, the X still showed a modest het density, but after an allele-fraction filter of 0.35 to 0.65 it fell to about 1 % of the autosomal filtered rate: an upper bound for the false-het share. A few X-depth contigs behaved as diploid (hundreds of hets per Mb) and were excluded.

    Callable mask: MAPQ ≥ 10 and depth from about half to about twice the median, covering most of the genome. A two-state HMM put nearly two thirds of the genome in ROH and was discarded. The 50-kb window mixture put roughly a third of species A's genome in ROH, with many short runs but also a clear share in runs of 5 Mb and longer; a published chromosome-level genome of a close relative (HiFi) had about three times less ROH, in fewer, longer-median runs, and almost none in runs ≥ 5 Mb. In our contig assembly a noticeable share of ROH bases sat in runs touching a contig end, in the chromosome-level one almost none: a contig assembly cuts long runs. In the relative's genome, differences between the two assembled haplotypes agreed closely with read-based heterozygosity (window-by-window correlation near 1), and most of its densest 100-bp het clusters were microsatellite length alleles. GenomeScope on species A's long-read k-mers had given a different heterozygosity: k-mer heterozygosity from noisy reads is not SNV heterozygosity.
