Lesson 6 · short repeats, from one copy to a whole genome

SINEs and Satellite DNA

Lesson 3 masked repeats so that they would not get in the way of genes. This lesson makes two kinds of repeat the subject. SINEs are short elements that copy themselves through RNA and sit alone, between unique stretches of DNA, in hundreds of thousands of places; satellites are units repeated head to tail in long arrays. We follow a SINE through its life cycle, find every copy of a known family, discover new families, split them into subfamilies with consensus sequences, and use the presence or absence of a copy at the same place in two genomes as a character for phylogenetics. Then we turn to satellites: monomers, arrays and higher-order repeats, satellites built from pieces of SINEs, and why both young SINEs and satellite arrays are where genome assemblies break.

The examples come from our lab's own work: a rodent SINE family in species A and a close relative (search, de novo discovery and orthologous loci), the shrew-mole SINE Urop, and SINEs and satellites of bats and lizards, with unpublished results marked as such. The tools are the lab's public ones (sear, SubFam, SINEderella, SINE_orth_loc, CLsat_workflow) next to standard programs. Dating of insertions, transposable elements other than SINEs and population genetics of insertion polymorphisms are left for other lessons. Hover over a dotted term to see what it means.

01What a SINE is

A SINE is a short piece of DNA that cannot copy itself, yet sits in the genome in hundreds of thousands of copies. This section follows one SINE through its life: how it is read into RNA, how a partner element writes it back into a new place, what marks the insertion leaves, and why the copies of one family slowly drift apart. That life cycle is what makes SINEs useful to a zoologist.

Lesson 3, section 02 introduced the SINE as one class of transposable elements and showed its head-body-tail plan. Here we go one level deeper, because the rest of this lesson depends on it.

Definition

SINE stands for short interspersed element. SINEBase, the curated catalogue of SINE families (Vassetzky & Kramerov 2013), defines SINEs as relatively short (under 700 bp; its working criteria below allow up to 1 kb) non-autonomous retroposons: they encode no protein of their own. They are read into RNA by the cell's RNA polymerase III from a promoter inside the element, and written back into DNA by the reverse transcriptase of a partner LINE. Most SINEs are 150-300 bp long (Vassetzky et al. 2021).

SINEBase uses three working criteria to accept a sequence as a SINE:

  1. it is short (under 1 kb) and interspersed, not part of a tandem array;
  2. it has at least 100 copies in the genome (genomes poor in repeats, such as Arabidopsis thaliana, excepted);
  3. its 5′ part is at least 60 % identical over at least 60 nt to a tRNA, 5S rRNA or 7SL RNA gene.

Anatomy of a SINE copy

A SINE is built from modules. SINEBase: the 5′ head comes from a small RNA made by pol III; the body is of unknown origin or comes from the partner LINE; the 3′ tail is a run of simple, often degenerate repeats. Every genomic copy is also flanked by two short target site duplications, which are not part of the SINE but a scar of its insertion. Head
The 5′ part, descended from one of three small pol III RNAs. tRNA is the ancestor of most SINE families. 7SL RNA (the RNA of the signal recognition particle) gave rise to primate Alu and rodent B1, after a central deletion of the 7SL sequence; outside supraprimates such SINEs were known only from hagfish, until our lab found Urop in a shrew mole (Kosushkin et al. 2026, below). 5S rRNA gave rise to several SINE families of fish, lizards, rodents, bats and butterflies (Vassetzky et al. 2021).
Boxes A and B
The internal pol III promoter of a tRNA or 7SL head: two 11-bp boxes spaced by 30-40 bp (Vassetzky et al. 2021). Because the promoter lies inside the element, every copy carries its own promoter wherever it lands. A 5S rRNA gene has a different internal promoter (box A, an intermediate element and box C), so a 5S-derived head has no B box.
Body
Of unknown origin, or taken from the 3′ end of the partner LINE, so that the SINE RNA "looks like" LINE RNA to the LINE's enzyme. Some bodies share a core domain with distant SINE families (SINEBase).
3′ end and tail
Most mammalian SINEs end in an A-rich tail, like the 3′ end of L1; the L1 enzyme needs an A-rich RNA end to start copying (Vassetzky et al. 2021; Dewannieux et al. 2003 showed that the poly(A) of Alu is essential for its mobility). In "T+" SINEs (B2, Dip, Ves and nine other mammalian families) the tail is preceded by AATAAA polyadenylation signals and a short pol III terminator, TCTTT in B2 or TATTT in Dip and Ves. Elsewhere, for example in the eel, the SINE's 3′ end is a copy of its partner LINE's 3′ end, and that shared sequence is what the LINE enzyme recognises (Kajikawa & Okada 2002).
Target site duplication (TSD)
A short stretch of the host DNA that appears on both sides of the copy. It is made during insertion (next figure), so it belongs to the locus, not to the SINE family.

Life cycle: copy and paste through RNA

  1. Transcription.
    Pol III reads a genomic SINE copy into RNA, starting from the promoter in the head. In T+ SINEs it stops at the terminator; in SINEs without one (Alu, B1, ID) the A-run is read too and transcription stops further downstream at random runs of T (Vassetzky et al. 2021). Only pol III transcripts serve as templates for new copies; a SINE read as part of a gene intron by RNA polymerase II does not.
  2. Recognition by a partner LINE.
    The SINE RNA borrows the protein made by an active LINE: in mammals L1, which has an endonuclease and a reverse transcriptase. Marked Alu copies moved in human cells only when L1 was present, and 100-1,000 times more often than control messenger RNAs (Dewannieux et al. 2003).
  3. Target-primed reverse transcription (TPRT).
    The endonuclease nicks one DNA strand at the target. The free 3′ end of the nicked strand is used as the primer to reverse-transcribe the RNA, and only after that is the second strand cut (Luan et al. 1993, shown for the R2 element of the silk moth and proposed for LINEs and SINEs). In mammals the first nick falls mostly at a TTAAAA-like site; the second, on the other strand, at a variable distance, preferably within 15-16 bp (Jurka 1997).
  4. Repair: the TSD appears.
    The two nicks are staggered, so after the new DNA is joined in and the gaps are filled, the stretch between the nicks exists twice, once on each side of the new copy. TSDs of varying lengths flanked the new Alu insertions in the cell experiment (Dewannieux et al. 2003).

Families, subfamilies and why copies drift apart

All copies that share one origin and the same set of modules form a SINE family (SINEBase; the tail may differ). Within a family, not every copy is a source of new copies. The master gene model (Deininger et al. 1992) says that a family is amplified from a very small group of active "master" copies, while the great majority behave like dead pseudogenes. When a master copy itself changes and keeps making copies, all its offspring share the change: they form a subfamily. Published subfamilies are often "reliably distinguished by relatively long indels" (indels), as in B2, Dip and Ves (Vassetzky et al. 2021). Section 04 shows how we find them.

A new copy is, at birth, the same sequence as the master RNA it was copied from. After that it is just a piece of host DNA: it mutates at the neutral rate (lesson 3, s02), its TSD copies mutate independently of each other, and its poly(A) tail shortens over host generations (Vassetzky et al. 2021). Young copies are therefore nearly identical to each other and to the consensus; old ones are diverged. Genomic copies of old SINE families can differ by 35 % or more.

Simple explanation with examples: a rubber stamp that needs someone else's hand

Picture a rubber stamp (the SINE) with its own ink pad built in (the promoter in its head). It can make a print (an RNA), but it has no hand to press the print onto paper. A LINE is the hand: it makes the cut and presses the copy into the genome. The SINE borrows that hand by looking, at its tail end, like the LINE's own RNA.

Where the hand presses, it tears the paper in two slightly offset places. When the tear is mended around the new print, the few letters between the tears are written twice, once on each side: the target site duplication.

Each print then fades on its own. Prints made last year are crisp and almost the same; prints made long ago are smudged differently. Put a dozen old prints on top of each other and read the letter most of them show at each place: you get the original stamp back. That is the consensus.

Why SINEs are good markers

A SINE insertion at one particular site of the genome is a character that is either present or absent. Shedlock & Okada (2000) credit "the irreversible, independent nature of their insertion": each insertion happens on its own at its own site, so the same family inserting at exactly the same site in two lineages is very unlikely; and once in place, a copy is not removed precisely. An insertion shared by two species at the orthologous site is therefore a shared derived character. Homoplasy is rare (Kosushkin et al. 2023).

"Rare" is not "never". Ray et al. (2006) went through all published primate Alu studies and found very low levels of the three confounding events: incomplete lineage sorting, parallel insertions at the same site, and precise excision. They titled the paper "SINEs of a nearly perfect character". That is why studies use many loci and look for conflicts, not a single insertion. Section 05 shows how presence and absence are scored on whole genomes.

Pitfalls when you judge a candidate

  • Boxes A and B are evidence, not proof. SINEBase found that pol III promoter searches, even with position frequency matrices, give many false positives and misses. The test that counts is similarity to a real tRNA, 5S or 7SL gene (60 % over 60 nt).
  • A TSD is a bonus fact, not proof. TSDs fade with age (B2e above: 23 %), and other transposable elements make them too. Decide from the alignment of many copies.
  • Not detected is not absent. Standard RNA-gene finders are tuned to keep SINEs out of tRNA gene lists, so they miss decayed heads (see the bat example below). Write "no tRNA head detected at this threshold", never "not tRNA-derived".
  • An RNA pseudogene is not a SINE. A reverse-transcribed copy of a cellular tRNA or 5S rRNA is a pseudogene. SINEs usually carry a body, share substitutions with their own source copy rather than with the cellular RNA gene, and greatly outnumber RNA pseudogenes (SINEBase).
  • Results are family-specific. Lengths, box positions, TSD lengths and the share of copies with TSDs differ between families. A number measured on one family (for example Tal of moles or B2 of mice) does not describe SINEs in general.

02Finding copies of a known SINE

You have a consensus of a SINE (section 01), from a database or from your own work. Now you want every copy of it in a genome: where each copy sits, its sequence with a little of the DNA around it, and how many copies there are. This sounds like a routine search. For SINEs it is not, and the reasons decide which tool you use.

Why standard search tools miss SINE copies

SINE copies are a hard target for three reasons at once. They are short: the consensus sequences our lab searched in one rodent genome are only about 200 to 270 bp long. Short means a small maximum alignment score, so a match must be nearly perfect to look "significant". They are diverged: every copy has collected its own mutations since it was inserted, and old copies can differ from the consensus at 20-30 % of positions (see the repeat landscapes in lesson 3, section 04). And they are numerous: a successful family can have hundreds of thousands to millions of copies in one genome.

The way out is to drop the word step and compare the consensus with every position of the genome. That is the Smith-Waterman algorithm (Smith and Waterman, 1981): it finds the best local alignment between two sequences, scoring every match, mismatch and gap, without any shortcut. It is guaranteed to find the best-scoring match, and it is slow. The program ssearch36 from W. R. Pearson's FASTA package (version 36.3.8) runs it with fast processor instructions and adds statistics to every hit.

sear: the lab's search, step by step

sear2k is our lab's public Bash tool; its main script is called sear. It takes one consensus and one genome FASTA and runs ssearch36 on the genome cut into small pieces. SINEderella (section 08) runs it in its first step, once for each consensus. What follows is read from the script itself (repository commit 923ffb6, October 2026).

  1. Cut the genome into overlapping windows
    seqkit sliding -g -s 2000 -W 2500: windows of 2500 bp that start every 2000 bp, so neighbouring windows overlap by 500 bp. Any copy of up to 500 bp therefore lies whole inside at least one window (try it in the box below). The window file is about 1.25 times the size of the genome.
  2. Split the windows into 20 parts
    seqkit split2 -p 20. The parts are searched one after the other; the parallel work happens inside ssearch36, which uses all processor cores (-T).
  3. Search each part with ssearch36
    Both strands of every window are compared with the consensus. Output is a table, one line per hit: window name, percent identity, coordinates, E-value and bit score.
  4. Merge hits in a window and apply two rules
    Hits on the same strand of one window that touch or overlap are merged (bedtools merge -s). A merged hit is kept when the best identity in it is at least 65 % and its longest alignment covers at least 0.8 × the consensus length on the genome. Shorter (partial) copies are dropped, not shortened.
  5. Put the hits back on the chromosomes
    Each window's name ends in _sliding:start-end; sear adds that offset to the hit coordinates. A copy seen in two overlapping windows becomes one interval in a second merge.
  6. Write the results
    A 7-column BED file (chromosome, start, end, identity %, length, strand, bit score) and a FASTA of every copy with flanks of 50 bp on each side (--slop L,R sets other sizes). The files are named after the first three letters of the genome file and the query name, for example gen-dip.bed and gen-dip.bnk.

Three options change the search. -m (minus bank) cuts out every hit found in a part and searches what is left again, round after round, until a round finds nothing. Our reading of why this helps: ssearch36 reports further alignments in the same window only under a stricter E-value cut-off, so a second copy in a window can be left out of the first round. -b N also takes the N best hits by bit score and aligns them with MAFFT, the consensus as first row, for a quick look. Queries of 1000 bp or longer (LINEs, not SINEs) are switched automatically to minimap2 with the roles reversed: the query is indexed and the genome is streamed past it in windows.

The scores: with its DNA defaults (source code of FASTA 36.3.8j1) ssearch36 gives +5 for a match and −4 for a mismatch, and charges a gap of k bases 12 + 4k. (sear passes its own -g -3, which may change the per-base part; see above.) A perfect 250-bp copy scores 1250. Random DNA also scores something: in the toy below, shuffled 420-bp windows reach roughly 50-100 against a 150-bp consensus, and in a whole genome the best chance scores are higher, because there are far more places to look.

Statistics need care: what -z 11 is for

An E-value needs a picture of what scores pure chance produces. By default (-z 1) ssearch36 builds that picture from the scores of the whole library it searched, which works when nearly all library sequences are unrelated to the query. The FASTA guide says so directly: the statistics "assume that the library contains a large sample of unrelated sequences", and if the library contains many related sequences, the parameters should be estimated with -z 11 to -z 15. A window bank of a genome full of SINE copies is such a library: a large share of the windows hold a real copy.

With -z 11, ssearch36 also scores a randomly shuffled copy of every window (same letters, order destroyed) and fits the chance distribution to those scores. The manual page warns that this means twice as many comparisons. Because the shuffles are random, E-values change a little from run to run, so hits close to the cut-off can appear in one run and not in the next. Two consequences: report counts as "about", and do not expect two identical runs to give identical files. The lab's assignment step (section 04) repeats its searches and uses exactly this wobble to keep only assignments that survive every repeat.

A second point is the size of the search. -E 2 means "fewer than two hits this good expected by chance in this part file". For a whole genome, each of the 20 parts can therefore let through a hit or two on score alone; the identity and length rules of step 4 are what removes them (our reasoning from the options, not a measured rate).

Simple explanation with examples: finding a misprinted phrase in a long book

Imagine a 3-million-page book in which a 250-letter advertisement was printed a million times, and every reprint added typos. A seed-based search is like using the index at the back: you look up one long exact word from the advert, and only open the pages where that word occurs. If every copy of that word has a typo, you never open the page.

advert (consensus): the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog old copy: the quack brawn fax jumps ovar thi lazy dug exact 11-letter words shared: none ("jumps over " is broken by "ovar") exact 5-letter words shared: "jumps", " lazy" ...

Smith-Waterman is reading every page and comparing it letter by letter with the advert, giving points for each agreeing letter and taking points away for each typo. Slow, but nothing is skipped. Cutting the book into overlapping 2.5-page pieces (the windows) means each piece holds at most one or two copies, so each copy gets its own score.

A rough sum for real copies: if 20 % of positions are mutated at random, a given run of 28 bases survives intact with probability 0.828 ≈ 0.2 %, so a 250-bp copy with about 220 such runs has well under one intact 28-base word on average. A run of 11 bases survives with 0.811 ≈ 8.6 %: about 20 intact words per copy. (Our arithmetic; real mutations are not perfectly random.)

Copy length: full-length and partial copies

A copy in the BED file is rarely exactly as long as the consensus. Small insertions and deletions change its length; the poly(A) tail is long in young copies and shortens over generations (Vassetzky et al. 2021, IJMS); and the alignment can stop a few bases short of the true end where the copy has decayed. The flanks are there so that you can see the real ends in an alignment.

More important is the 0.8 rule. A copy that is only partly present (cut at its 5′ end when it was inserted, broken by a later insertion or a deletion, or lying at the edge of a contig) is not extracted at all if its longest alignment covers less than 80 % of the consensus. So sear's count is a count of full-length or nearly full-length copies, not of every SINE-derived fragment. That is deliberate: the downstream steps align copies and compare their flanks, and fragments would break both. You can lower the rule (the third argument, Slf) to see fragments, but then two pieces of one broken insertion can count as two copies, and pieces of other families that share a part with your SINE (a common 5′ head, for example) start to match.

Counting copies

What you count is the number of loci: intervals left after all overlapping hits have been merged. Hits from two overlapping windows become one interval inside sear. When you search with several consensus sequences of one family (a bank: the family's subfamilies, see 04), the same copy is found by several of them. SINEderella's first step therefore merges the hits of all consensus sequences into one interval per locus before it counts (strand-aware), and removes loci inside known satellite regions (06). Whenever you quote a copy number, quote the rules with it: which consensus sequences, which identity and length cut-offs, merged how. "1.2 million copies" means little without them.

Two more things limit any count. First, a copy can only be found if it is in the assembly: young, nearly identical copies are the ones assemblers collapse or leave out (section 07). Second, a search finds similarity, not proof. Whether a hit is an inserted SINE with its own unique flanks, a piece of a duplicated region, or a unit of a satellite array is decided later, from alignments (sections 04 and 05).

03Discovering new SINEs

Section 02 searched a genome with a SINE you already had. Here there is nothing to start from: no consensus exists for your species. Discovery has four jobs: find places that look like SINE copies, group them and make first consensuses, check where the head came from, and find the true ends of the element. The last two are where most mistakes are made.

What a SINE leaves in the genome

Programs that look for new SINEs use four kinds of signal (the parts are described in section 01). None of them is proof on its own.

A pol III promoter in the head
A tRNA-derived head carries the A box and B box. They are short patterns (5-11 bases) and occur by chance in any genome, so a box is "an indication, not a proof"; 5S-derived heads have no B box at all.
A tail at the 3′ end
Often a poly(A) tail or another simple repeat, sometimes after a pol III terminator. In B2, Dip and Ves SINEs young copies have long tails that shorten over generations (Vassetzky et al. 2021).
Target site duplications
TSDs on both sides of a copy. They erode with age, some SINE families do not make them, and other elements make them too. In our lab they are a bonus fact a person can look at, never a criterion.
Many copies with unique flanks
The defining property. A SINE is an interspersed repeat: the same element sits in thousands of different places, so the copies agree with each other while the DNA around each copy (its flanks) is unique. A segmental duplication or a tandem array also has many copies, but there the neighbourhood was copied too.

SINE-de-novo-genome-scan (our lab)

The lab's discovery tool (github.com/Toki-bio/SINE-de-novo-genome-scan) starts from one premise, written in its README: many SINEs share at least limited similarity with some known SINE, so even a faint match is a lead. It cuts a database of known SINE consensuses (for example from SINEBase) into short pieces and looks for each piece in the genome.

  1. Remove near-duplicates
    The SINE database is reduced at 95 % identity with vsearch (the README gives the tool and the cutoff, no script).
    SINEs.fa → SINEBase.nr95.fa
  2. Shred, region-aware (01_sanitize_shred.sh)
    Each SINE is cut into 50-bp pieces. Head and tail are diagnostic, so they are cut finer: bases 1-150 every 10 bp; the middle (151 to length−100, only for SINEs longer than 250 bp) every 25 bp; the last 99 bp (only for SINEs longer than 150 bp) every 25 bp. Each piece keeps its origin in the name, for example >SINE_ID|5p:11-60.
  3. Drop simple pieces, cluster (02_filter_lc_cluster_nr85.sh)
    Pieces with a run of one base, two bases or three bases longer than 15 bp are removed; the rest are clustered at 85 % on both strands and only the centroids are kept.
  4. Scan (sine_scan.sh)
    The genome is cut into 100-Mb chunks; every chunk is searched with all pieces using ssearch36. A hit is kept at ≥ 65 % identity when the aligned length is ≥ 90 % of the piece.
  5. Merge into candidate loci
    Each hit is widened by 50 bp on both sides (FLANK=50); widened hits on the same strand that overlap or touch become one locus, so hits up to 100 bp apart join. (The README says "within 500 bp"; the code uses the 50-bp padding.) The loci are written as BED and FASTA.
    merged_loci.bed, merged_loci.fa
  6. Hand over
    The candidates go to SubFam, which sorts them, cuts the sorted list into chunks and makes one consensus per chunk (section 04). These loci are anchors grown into candidates, not verified elements.

AnnoSINE_v2

AnnoSINE_v2 (Liao, Sun and Ou 2024, Mobile DNA; code at github.com/liaoherui/AnnoSINE_v2) extends AnnoSINE, a tool built for plant genomes (Li et al. 2022), to animals and to large genomes. It has three modes: 1 profile HMM search, 2 structure search, 3 both. The structure search uses SINE-Finder. Then each candidate passes a chain of filters:

  1. TSD search in the flanks of each candidate.
  • Copy number and boundary
    The candidate plus 100 bp on each side is searched against the genome. A position belongs to the element if at least 15 % of the hits cover it (-c 0.15). The first and last base of the widened sequence must be covered by at most one hit, which means the flanks must be unique. Candidates whose new ends move too far from the old ones (-s) are dropped.

    blastn against a built-in set of 954 tRNA, 712 5S rRNA and 63 7SL RNA sequences. A candidate with a near-perfect hit (E-value below 1e-15) is thrown out as a real RNA gene; the others are labelled by their best hit: tRNA, 5S_rRNA, 7SL_RNA or Unknown.
  • Other repeats out
    Candidates made mostly of tandem repeats (TRF) and candidates flanked by inverted repeats (IRF) are removed; CD-HIT removes redundancy; RepeatMasker then annotates the genome with the library.

    SINE_Scan

    SINE_Scan (Mao and Wang 2017, Bioinformatics; version 1.1.1 of 2016 is the last on github.com/maohlzj/SINE_Scan; Perl, BioPerl and Python 2) combines the structure, the copy number and what its authors call the hallmark of transposition. Module 1 finds candidates with an extended SINE-Finder that also knows 7SL and 5S heads. Module 2 verifies them. Module 3 classifies them and annotates the genome. Its verification is the clearest written form of the "unique flanks" idea:

    • for each candidate, the genome copies are collected (at least 5, -n), and from each copy it takes the 60-bp left flank, the first and last 25 bp of the element and the 60-bp right flank (-F 60, -E 25);
    • these pieces are aligned with MUSCLE, and a per-column alignment quality score (SAQ) is averaged over the left flank, the element ends and the right flank;
    • a candidate passes if the flanks score at most 0.6 and the ends at least 0.75 (-S, -I), or if the ends beat both flanks by at least 0.3 (-D).

    Its user guide shows example alignments of a good SINE and of typical false candidates (no drop in alignment quality at the ends; probably a MITE, a Helitron or a solo LTR) and asks the user to inspect them by eye. A head is assigned to tRNA, 7SL or 5S at ≥ 60 % similarity and ≥ 60 % overlap with the RNA (-t 0.6, -r 0.6).

    Where did the head come from: tRNA, 7SL or 5S?

    Every SINE head descends from a pol III gene: usually a tRNA, sometimes a 7SL RNA or a 5S rRNA (section 01). The field's test is similarity to the real RNA genes, not motifs: SINEBase calls a head tRNA-, 5S- or 7SL-derived at ≥ 60 % identity over ≥ 60 nt (Vassetzky and Kramerov 2013). The 7SL case is not only primate business: the shrew mole Uropsilus gracilis carries about 280,000 copies of a dimeric, 7SL-derived SINE, Urop (Kosushkin et al. 2026).

    Why gene finders are the wrong judge. Two standard tools recognise these RNAs, and both are tuned to keep SINE copies out of gene lists. tRNAscan-SE reports a tRNA only from 20 bits by default. Rfam accepts a hit only above each family's gathering threshold: 29 bits for tRNA, 38 for 5S rRNA, 70 for the 299-position metazoan 7SL (SRP) model (Rfam 15.1). A head that has decayed for millions of years, or that keeps only part of 7SL, often stays below. So "not found by Rfam" means "not detected at threshold", never "not tRNA-derived".

    What works: a score against a null. Align the head with a set of real tRNA genes (for mammals, for example, a GtRNAdb set) with ssearch36. Then shuffle the head 20 times, keeping its base composition, and search again. A head counts when its real score beats every shuffle; the best-matching tRNA gives the likely amino acid (the isotype). This is how SINEderella's head check (tools/trna_heads.sh) works, with Rfam and tRNAscan-SE as extra columns.

    # heads.fa = the consensuses (and each part of a composite SINE); tRNAs.fa = tRNA genes of a related genome
# -m 8: tabular output; -E 10: keep even weak hits (the null decides, not the E-value)
ssearch36 -3 -m 8 -E 10 heads.fa tRNAs.fa > real.m8
# 20 shuffles that keep the dinucleotide composition (-d), fixed seed for reproducibility
esl-shuffle -d -N 20 --seed 42 heads.fa > shuf.fa
ssearch36 -3 -m 8 -E 10 shuf.fa tRNAs.fa > shuf.m8
# extra columns: all Rfam families (tRNA, 5S, 7SL ...) and tRNAscan-SE with a lower cutoff
cmscan -E 1 --tblout cm.tbl Rfam.cm heads.fa
# -X 10: report from 10 bits (default 20); --detail: isotype-model scores
tRNAscan-SE -E -H --detail -X 10 -o trnascan.out -f trnascan.ss heads.fa

    Where does the SINE end? Extend until the flanks are unique

    A discovery tool rarely gets the ends right. A scan hit from a 50-bp piece stops wherever the piece stopped; a consensus made from chunk consensuses can be cut short or run into a tail of variable length. The fix is the same idea as the extend-align-trim curation of lesson 3 (lesson 3, section 03): take the copies with more sequence beyond the current end and see how far they still agree. Inside the element, copies resemble each other; past its end each copy sits in its own unique place, and similarity falls to the level of two random pieces of genome. That level must be measured, not assumed.

    SINEderella does this in step7_boundary_refine.sh:

    1. Measure the background
      300 random 70-bp windows of the genome, 2,000 random pairs, plain (ungapped) identity. It keeps the mean and the share of pairs that are elevated, more than 45 % identical.
    2. Step outward
      For each subfamily and each side, a 50-bp window is taken just past the copies' end, then 50 bp further, and so on, up to 1,000 bp. Each time 150 random pairs of copies are compared in that window.
    3. Stop at background
      The boundary is the first window where the elevated share is no more than background + 5 percentage points. Subfamilies with fewer than 7 copies are not tested.

    Why the share of elevated pairs and not the mean identity? The script's own comment explains: a mean is blind to a mixed population. If half of the copies go on (a longer subgroup) and half are already in unique flank, the mean can fall near background although a real conserved group is still there. On real data (a SINE subfamily of the hedgehog Erinaceus europaeus) the mean-based version called the boundary at 50 bp; the true boundary, found by the share test and confirmed by eye, was at 100-150 bp. A second trap is noted there too: the 100 best-scoring copies are the least diverged ones and stay similar much further out than a random sample, so SINEderella tests the two populations separately. Since October 2026 SINEderella's step 1 also moves locus ends before extraction, walking 10-bp steps outward while the copies of a group still share 10-letter words, up to 100 bp.

    Simple explanation with examples: why a SINE ends where the flanks become unique

    Imagine the same printed leaflet glued into thousands of different books, each time on a different page. If you photocopy each leaflet with some of the page around it, all copies agree inside the leaflet and disagree just outside it: one shows a recipe, another a map, another a poem. The edge of the leaflet is where the photocopies stop agreeing.

    Two things make the edge blurry. Old leaflets are faded (diverged copies), so even the inside agrees less. And some leaflets carry a stamp of different length at the bottom (the poly(A) tail), so the very last line lands at a slightly different place in each copy.

    A third case is the trap: some leaflets are a longer edition. Looking only at the average, you see half the copies agreeing and half not, and the average looks almost like "no agreement". Counting how many pairs still agree well shows that a group is still going.

    The expert still decides

    All three tools end in alignments that someone has to read. SINE_Scan's guide asks for inspection by eye; AnnoSINE_v2 can draw its alignments (-f y); our lab works from alignments in ViewAlign, and the project's rule is that a decision needs an alignment to look at. A public database is no shortcut either: we do not treat Dfam's human library as ground truth, because it contains fragments, 7SL RNA itself, mixtures of families and families with a single copy.

    In our projects: boundaries are hard

    In an internal test (lab notes, unpublished), our boundary criterion, given the expert's own start and end positions, rebuilt his consensuses exactly. Asked to find the boundaries itself, it agreed with him for 1 of 6 families. This is why SINEderella's aim asks for a workflow that produces the SINE loci and the report with alignments by itself, reproducibly, and why, for now, a new family still passes an expert's eye before it enters the bank. Two published discoveries show the end product: the Ther1-derived SINE Squam3 of scaled reptiles (Vassetzky et al. 2021) and Urop of the shrew mole (Kosushkin et al. 2026).

    A short checklist

    • Candidates come from at least one of the three approaches; record the database, version and thresholds.
    • Mask the families you already know before you look for new ones (minus bank).
    • Group with SubFam and read the alignments (section 04).
    • Test the head against tRNA, 5S and 7SL genes with a shuffle null; report "not detected", never "not derived".
    • Set the ends where copy pairs fall to a measured background; check that the flanks are unique (section 05).
    • Do not pre-filter tandem repeats away before searching: some real SINEs are next to satellites or gave rise to them (section 06).

    04Families, subfamilies and consensus sequences

    A search (section 02) gives you thousands or millions of copies of one SINE. They are not all alike. Some differences were made once, in an active "source" copy, and passed on to all its offspring; most were made later, in each copy on its own. This section is about telling those two kinds of difference apart: how copies are grouped into subfamilies, how one sequence, the consensus, is made to stand for each group, and which lab tools do it.

    Family, subfamily, consensus

    The definitions our lab works with are written in the SINEderella manual (section 6.1.6):

    Family
    A set of SINE copies made of the same parts (for example the same head, body and tail, section 01), so that they still align along their whole length.
    Subfamily
    A lineage inside a family, defined by a shared, diagnostic pattern of small indels or single-base differences that all its copies carry: in effect a synapomorphy. Not by the private mutations each copy collected after it was inserted: those are noise on top of the subfamily signal.
    Diagnostic position
    An alignment column where (almost) all copies of one group carry the same base or gap and (almost) no copy outside the group does.
    Consensus
    One sequence that stands for a group of copies: at each column of their alignment, the base most copies have. Private mutations are scattered, so they are outvoted; the shared pattern survives. A good consensus approximates the sequence of the source gene that made the copies.

    A direct consequence, stated in the same manual section: average pairwise identity is the wrong measure for finding subfamilies. Two old copies of one subfamily can differ at 20 % of positions, all private; two young copies of sister subfamilies may differ only at their two or three diagnostic positions. Identity measures the private decay, which is exactly the part the definition calls noise. The figure below lets you see this happen.

    Published SINE subfamilies show the kinds of features that diagnose them. In the rodent SINEs B2, Dip and Ves the subfamilies are "reliably distinguished by relatively long indels" (Vassetzky et al. 2021). In the shrew-mole SINE Urop, subfamilies b and c share a 24-nucleotide tandem duplication that subfamily a lacks (Kosushkin et al. 2026). The SINE Can splits into older a1 and a2 and younger b1 and b2, of which b2 (and less so b1) is still active (Kosushkin et al. 2022).

    Simple explanation with examples: scribes, slips and a family tree of manuscripts

    Think of a medieval text copied by hand. A scribe copies a manuscript and introduces a few slips. Other scribes copy his copy, so every later copy in that line carries his slips, plus slips of their own. Philologists group surviving manuscripts by the slips they share, not by how many slips each has: a manuscript full of its own errors can still belong, without doubt, to a line marked by two shared errors.

    SINE copies work the same way. A source copy in the genome mutates, keeps being copied, and all its offspring carry its changes. Those shared changes are the diagnostic positions. Each copy then decays on its own, a little more every million years. A consensus is like reconstructing the lost exemplar of one line: take many copies of that line, and at each position keep the reading most of them agree on.

    Example: 8 copies of subfamily A and 7 of subfamily B. At position 12 all A copies read G and all B copies read A; at position 57 all A copies read T and all B copies read C. Everywhere else the copies differ by scattered single changes. Positions 12 and 57 together are the diagnostic pattern: they split the copies the same way, and each split position backs up the other.

    Building a consensus

    The basic rule is a vote in each column of a multiple alignment. The details decide the result:

    • Majority or plurality. A majority call needs more than half of the sequences; a plurality call only needs the base to be the most common one, above some threshold. With old copies no base may reach half, so tools use a lower threshold.
    • Are gaps counted? If gaps stay in the denominator ("a base must be in at least 50 % of all sequences"), a column that most copies lack is not called. This trims ragged ends and rare insertions. If gaps are left out, the consensus gets longer and denser.
    • Threshold as a fraction, never a count. The same count means different things in groups of 20 and 2,000.
    • Run the final call once. Our lab notes record that re-aligning copies to a consensus and calling again, round after round, made one family's consensus creep outward from 203 to 252 to 269 bp: each round pulls in a little flank.

    SINE_consensus: a bootstrap consensus for one group

    SINE_consensus (lab tool, public) builds the consensus of one group of copies that you have already chosen. sine_consensus.sh works in rounds: it draws a random subsample of 100 copies, aligns them with MAFFT, calls a mini-consensus, adds it to the mini-consensuses of earlier rounds, aligns those and calls the consensus of the consensuses. It stops when that consensus changes at fewer than 1 % of positions between rounds (at least 5, at most 50 rounds). If the rounds keep swinging, an "anchor phase" (after round 3) mixes copies of the best consensus so far into each subsample, up to half of it, so that one unlucky sample cannot flip the result.

    The call itself, as the script implements it (version of March 2026): a column is skipped when fewer than 30 % of sequences have a base there; otherwise the most common base is called if it is in at least 50 % of all sequences, gaps included; ties are not called. The README's option table still describes an older rule (gaps left out of the denominator); the script's own help (-h) is the current one. A second script, sine_pairwise_consensus.sh, aligns every copy one by one to a reference consensus (MAFFT with a high gap-opening penalty, --op 5) and votes position by position; the README recommends it for families with thousands of copies.

# -k keeps the log and a "trace" alignment of the mini-consensus of every round
bash sine_consensus.sh -k dip_group1.fa          # writes dip_group1_consensus.fasta

# the same rule, stricter about ragged ends: base in >= 70 % of all copies

    The CpG caveat

    In vertebrates most CpG sites are methylated, and a methylated C decays to T many times faster than other sites mutate (lesson 3, section 02). A CG in the source gene becomes TG in some copies, CA in others (the same change on the other strand), and TA where both have happened. A vote is taken column by column: once more than half of the copies have lost the C, the first column is called T; once more than half have lost the G, the second is called A. In an old family the consensus loses its CpGs, and copies measured against it look less diverged than they are. SINE_consensus and SubFam use plain votes. RepeatModeler's consensus builder (MultAln.pm, "Correct for missed CpG calls") scores a CG hypothesis higher when the copies show TG or CA; its own comment notes that the setting seemed to overestimate CpGs in very old elements. What to do by hand: at every TG or CA of your consensus, look at the column pair; a mix of CG, TG and CA there points to a lost CpG. Tick "CpG-aware consensus" in the toy to see the difference.

    SubFam: thousands of copies as an alignment of consensus sequences

    Twenty copies fit on a screen; 30,000 do not, and no one can see the shared pattern in them. SubFam (lab tool, public; version 1.5.1) represents a large set of sequences as a short alignment of consensus sequences. Its README: 40 rows for 2,000 copies, each row the consensus of a group of closely related copies, "the private mutations of individual copies are averaged away, and the variants a group shares stay visible".

    1. Order
      All copies are put in an order where similar copies sit next to each other. The order comes from a tree built on k-mer counts (k = 6, weighted Jaccard distance, UPGMA), the same tree ViewAlign uses for "Reorder by similarity". No alignment is made in this step. Option -m uses MAFFT's guide tree instead (mafft --retree 0 --reorder, the method of SubFam 1.0).
    2. Chunk
      The ordered list is cut into blocks of about N = 50 neighbours (-n); nothing is dropped.
    3. Consensus per chunk
      Each chunk is aligned with MAFFT (in parallel) and gets a plurality consensus: a base is called when at least 0.36 × chunk size of the sequences support it (-p; 18 of 50, the value SINEderella has used since its first version). Bases supported by half the chunk or fewer are written in lower case.
    4. Align the consensuses
      The chunk consensuses are aligned with MAFFT L-INS-i (an accurate, slow mode) up to 300 rows, a faster setting up to 1,000, MAFFT's automatic mode beyond. Result: PREFIX.aln.fasta.

    Why chunks, not clusters at an identity threshold? Standard clustering tools (CD-HIT, VSEARCH, MMseqs2) need a cut-off such as "90 % identical". For repeat families no cut-off fits all: a young family is split into hundreds of tiny clusters at 95 % and lumped together at 80 %, and in an old family any useful cut-off leaves most copies alone. SubFam only asks which copies are each other's nearest neighbours and averages 50 of them. On the README's simulated families (8 subfamilies, 2,000 copies, decayed by 3, 8 or 15 %), SubFam recovered 8, 8 and 7 of 8 subfamilies with 40 rows; each VSEARCH threshold worked only for one age. These are simulations from the tool's own benchmark, not a test on real SINEs.

    What SubFam does not do. It does not decide what the subfamilies are. A chunk can fall inside a subfamily (redundant rows) or straddle two (mixed rows), and a subfamily needs roughly N copies to get a row of its own; use a smaller -n for rare ones. Grouping the rows is the next step (below).

    Finding the subfamilies: peeling

    The SubFam alignment of a few hundred rows is where the subfamily call is made. In the lab this has been done by eye: open the alignment, find a block of rows that share a pattern of columns no other rows have, set it aside, and repeat on the rest until only noise is left. This is called peeling. Each peeled block becomes a candidate subfamily; its copies (from chunks.tsv, or SubFam's -e option) go to SINE_consensus.

    SINEderella: the whole workflow

    SINEderella (lab tool, public) is the orchestrator: one command that runs the search, extraction, assignment and report for a genome and a bank of consensus sequences. Its stated aim (docs/AIM.md): the subject is interspersed repeats, copies with unique flanks; the output is (1) the list of SINE loci in the genome and (2) an HTML page with links to alignments, conclusions on alignment parts, copy numbers, leak and divergence from consensus, produced by the workflow itself, on every run, without manual checking. Copies whose flanks are shared with other copies (segmental duplications, units of tandem arrays) are not the subject: they are never shown in alignments, at most counted.

    1. Clean the inputs
      Genome and consensus FASTA headers and line endings are normalised; the bank is checked for reverse-complement duplicates; an assembly check runs (it never blocks).
    2. Step 1: search and extract
      Each consensus is searched with sear (section 02): a hit must cover at least 80 % of the consensus length at 65 % similarity or more (the README's table still says 0.9; the manual and code use 0.8). Hits are merged, SINE-derived satellites and tandem arrays are taken out (section 06), copies are extracted, and a random sample of up to 30,000 goes through SubFam (chunks of 50).
    3. You: review the SubFam alignment
      Not automatic. When no good subfamily set exists yet, you peel the chunk-consensus alignment, build one consensus per subfamily with SINE_consensus, and re-run with the new bank (SINEderella --add adds consensuses to an existing run). For rare subfamilies, step1c_deplete.sh runs SubFam with chunks of 20 on the copies the bank explains worst.
    4. Step 2: assignment ("asSINEment")
      Every copy is compared with every consensus by ssearch36 (Smith-Waterman, section 02), 10 times. Because ssearch36 shuffles sequences for its statistics, scores wobble slightly between runs. A copy is assigned only if the same consensus wins all 10 times, and its bitscore is at least 0.45 × the bitscore of that subfamily's N-th best copy (N = 10, or fewer for a small subfamily).
    5. Step 3: per-copy measures
      sim_ratio = the copy's score against its consensus ÷ the consensus scored against itself (1.0 = identical). LEAK = the runner-up consensus reached at least 90 % of the winner's score: the copy sits between two subfamilies. CONFLICT = search hits of different consensuses overlap at this locus. Unassigned copies get a soft label from their best search hit; nothing is discarded.
    6. Step 4: plots
      Per subfamily: a histogram of divergence (100 − % identity of each copy to the consensus it was searched with), and the base frequencies at every consensus position.
    7. Flank checks and verdicts
      For each subfamily, flank windows are widened until pairs of copies are no more alike there than random genome pairs; for each copy, its flanks are compared with those of the other copies of its family. Every copy gets a verdict: SINE (assigned, flanks tested and unique), grey (flanks could not be tested) or not SINE (not assigned, or flanks shared). "A test that could not be done is not a pass."
    8. Report and plates
      An HTML report (results/report.html) with the subfamily table, divergence and links to "plates": alignments of the top 100 and a random 100 SINE loci per subfamily, with 50 bp of left and 70 bp of right flank, viewable in ViewAlign.

    What a subfamily is in SINEderella. In practice, one consensus in the bank. Every consensus competes with every other as an equal: there is no family level yet. The manual (section 6.1.6) describes a two-stage design, families first, then subfamilies within each family, and marks it "not yet implemented". So the subfamilies a run reports are only as good as the bank you gave it, which is why the review step matters.

    SINEplot: a map of copies between subfamilies

    SINEplot (lab tool, public) draws every copy as a dot in a two-dimensional map. Input: ssearch36 scores of all copies against all subfamily consensuses (field 12, the bitscore). The subfamily centres are placed by MDS from how well the consensuses match each other; each copy is placed between the centres, pulled towards each in proportion to its score. A copy is assigned to its best subfamily; its confidence is its best score ÷ that consensus's score against itself, and its conflict ratio is second-best ÷ best score. In conflict mode the copies are marked solid (ratio below 0.50), moderate (0.50-0.85) or ambiguous (0.85 and above). It shows the same thing as SINEderella's LEAK flag, as a picture: copies floating between two centres. Despite the repository's one-line description, the plot is of scores, not a divergence histogram; the per-subfamily divergence histograms come from SINEderella's step 4.

    05Orthologous loci: presence and absence of SINEs

    A SINE copy lands at one place in one genome, in one animal. If that copy spreads through the population and is inherited, every descendant species carries it at the same place, between the same two pieces of unique DNA. Species that branched off earlier have the same place without it. So "is the copy there or not?" at one place, asked in several genomes, is a character for phylogenetics, like a feather type or a tooth cusp, only much cleaner. This section shows how the place is found in two genomes, how the lab's tool SINE_orth_loc decides "present", "absent" or "cannot tell", and when the answer misleads.

    Words used in this section

    Locus
    Here: one SINE copy together with its flanks, the unique DNA just to its left (5′) and right (3′). The flanks, not the SINE, tell you which place in the genome this is: SINE copies are similar to each other, flanks are not.
    Orthologous loci
    The same place in two genomes, inherited from their common ancestor. Found by matching the flanks (lesson 3, section 07 used the same idea for genes).
    Empty site
    The orthologous place in a genome that has no SINE there: the left and right flanks join directly. Usually only one copy of the TSD sequence is present (section 01).
    Presence/absence character
    For one orthologous locus, a table entry per species: 1 (SINE present), 0 (empty site) or "?" (no data). Many loci give a matrix that can be read like any other character matrix.
    Outgroup
    A species outside the group you study. It shows which state is the old one. For SINE insertions the old state is normally "absent".
    Polymorphic insertion
    A copy that is present in some individuals of a species and absent in others: it has not (yet) become fixed.
    Incomplete lineage sorting (ILS)
    A polymorphism that was still present when an ancestral species split, and was then fixed differently in the daughter species. The insertion pattern of that locus then disagrees with the species tree, although nothing went wrong with the data.
    Homoplasy
    The same state reached independently, not by common descent. For SINEs: two separate insertions at the same place (parallel insertion), or a copy removed so exactly that the empty site is restored (precise deletion).

    Why an insertion is such a good character

    Shedlock and Okada (2000, BioEssays) summarised the argument: SINE insertions are irreversible and independent, so a shared copy at the same place diagnoses common ancestry "with extreme confidence". Shedlock, Takahashi and Okada (2004, Trends in Ecology and Evolution) add that most SINE copies are neutral, are not precisely excised, and have a known ancestral state (no copy). Compare a DNA base: a C can become T and back to C, and the same change can happen in two lineages; you need a model of substitution to read it. A SINE copy at one place in a genome of a billion places is unlikely to arise twice, and is rarely removed cleanly.

    A classic use: Nikaido, Rooney and Okada (1999, PNAS) showed with SINE and LINE insertions that whales sit inside the even-toed ungulates and that hippopotamuses are their closest living relatives. Lum et al. (2000, Mol Biol Evol) tested the "irreversible" assumption on seven loci in five cetartiodactyl taxa and found the insertion topology consistent with the tree from the flanking DNA. Before whole genomes, each locus meant cloning, PCR and sequencing; Okada, Shedlock and Nikaido (2004) describe that laboratory workflow. Now thousands of loci can be read from assemblies, as Churakov et al. (2009, Genome Research) did with about 2 million candidate loci for the root of placental mammals.

    Simple explanation with examples: a sticker in a book

    Imagine a book that is copied by hand, again and again, for centuries. One day a copyist glues a small sticker on page 112, between the words "river" and "bank". Every copy made from that copy has the sticker in the same place. Copies made from older versions do not.

    Now you find three copies of the book. Two have the sticker between "river" and "bank"; one has "river bank" with nothing between. You know the two with stickers share a more recent ancestor. You did not need to compare every word of the books, only that one place. And you found the place by the words around it ("river" ... "bank"), not by the sticker: every sticker looks alike.

    Two warnings, which the rest of the section makes precise. (1) Someone could peel a sticker off so neatly that no trace is left: precise deletion. (2) If the copyist's shop had both stickered and unstickered versions on the shelf when it split into two shops, each shop may have kept a different version by chance: incomplete lineage sorting.

    When the pattern misleads

    Try the scenarios "lineage sorting", "precise deletion" and "parallel insertion" above: all three give the same row in the matrix (A and C present, B absent), which supports the wrong pair. The matrix alone cannot tell them apart.

    • Incomplete lineage sorting is the common one and is not an error of the method. Shedlock et al. (2004) stress that such "discordant" loci record ancestral polymorphism and should not be dismissed as noise. It is frequent when speciation events follow each other quickly: in neoavian birds, Suh, Smeds and Ellegren (2015, PLoS Biology) found ILS-derived conflicts at 35 % of 2,118 retrotransposon presence/absence markers on autosomes. For placental mammals, Churakov et al. (2009) found significant support for each of three competing root hypotheses (5, 9 and 8 insertions) and concluded the root is a "soft" polytomy, explained by ILS and/or ancestral hybridisation.
    • Homoplasy is rare but possible. Ray, Xing, Salem and Batzer (2006, Systematic Biology, "SINEs of a nearly perfect character") collected all primate Alu insertion studies and found "very low levels" of lineage sorting, parallel insertions and precise excision. "Very low" is measured on Alu in primates; other groups may differ.
    • How many loci are enough? Kuritzin, Kischka, Schmitz and Churakov (2016, PLoS Computational Biology) give a statistical test (KKSC): if all three species were searched equally and there is no conflict, three elements present in two species and absent in the third give p < 0.05 for that grouping; five are needed if loci were found in one species' genome only. Conflicting loci are evaluated by their symmetry: ILS spreads conflicts evenly over the alternative trees, hybridisation favours one.
    • Polarity needs an outgroup. With two genomes, "present in species 1, absent in species 2" does not say whether the SINE was inserted on lineage 1 or (rarely) deleted on lineage 2. An outgroup genome that lacks it makes the insertion reading the simple one.

    Within a species, young polymorphic insertions are population markers: Shedlock et al. (2004) cite human Alu polymorphisms used for human genetic diversity and ancient demography. Kosushkin et al. (2023, Genes) describe a polymorphic insertion between species as a shared derived character.

    Finding the same place in two genomes: SINE_orth_loc

    SINE_orth_loc is the lab's tool for this ("Search for the orthologous SINE-containing loci in two genome assemblies"). Version v1.0-genes2023 is the workflow of Kosushkin, Korchagin, Vergun and Ryskov (2023, Genes 14:2089), shown on lacertid lizards; the description below is the current version (commit f40fbf2, 30 October 2026), which keeps the same classification rules. It does not search for SINEs itself: it starts from the copies you already have in each genome (section 02, sear2k, or a SINEderella run, section 08).

    1. Inputs
      Two genome FASTA files, the SINE consensus, and a BED file of the copies in each genome (named sp1-SINE.bed, sp2-SINE.bed). In bank mode the consensus file holds many consensuses (subfamilies, composite SINEs) and the copies come from a SINEderella run: only copies with the verdict SINE (assigned, unique flanks) and partial copies are used; every alignment gets its own copy's consensus as the reference row.
    2. Isolated copies only
      Copies closer than FLANK (300 bp) to another copy are grouped (bedtools cluster -d 300) and kept out of the flank mapping: their flanks would contain SINE sequence, not unique DNA. This is the lab's rule that an interspersed copy is defined by unique flanks. (A later step, sine_nest.py, handles SINEs inserted into other SINEs and close pairs separately.)
    3. Map the flanks
      The 300 bp left flank of every isolated copy is mapped to the other genome, in both directions, with minimap2 (-x sr -k 11 -w 3 -p 0.5 -N 50; MAPPER=bwa uses bwa mem, with results within 0.2 %). By default (BOTHFLANKS=1) the right flank is mapped too, and a left-flank hit is kept only where the right flank lands on the same sequence and strand, at most SINE length + 100 bp downstream. Flanks that stay unplaced (in a repeat, for example) are cut again at 600 and 1,000 bp and remapped (ADAPT), stopping before any neighbouring copy.
    4. Cut windows, group them
      Each hit is extended to the right by SINE length + 300 bp. Windows from both directions that overlap are joined into clusters. A cluster of 2 loci is a double (one place in each genome), 3-10 a multi, more a poly (not analysed). Multi clusters go through a two-flank rescue that allows one partner per site and reports ties as ambiguous.
    5. Align and judge
      Each cluster's windows plus the consensus are aligned with MAFFT --op 5. The script ComPair.sh measures the alignment with esl-alipid and classifies it (rules in the toy below).
    6. Write the results
      Alignment bundles per class, a table of every pair, and, for several genomes, a multi-species matrix (outputs further down).

    Example:

    The ComPair.sh rules, in order
    1. The consensus row must start and end with gaps (there must be a left and a right flank); otherwise lfSINE / rfSINE.
    2. Left flank: longer than 150 alignment columns, more than 65 identical bases and more than 65 % identity between the two genomes; otherwise badLF (the flanks are not shown to be orthologous).
    3. SINE in both? Identity between the two SINE regions above 65 %, and more than 100 identical bases between them, between copy 1 and the consensus, and between copy 2 and the consensus. If yes: the right flank must be longer than 150 columns and above 65 % identity: SINE (present in both); else badRF.
    4. Otherwise the SINE may be in one genome only. The overhang at the end of the longer window is cut off; the trimmed right flank must be longer than 150 columns, above 70 % identity and have more than 120 identical bases. Then PM if copy 1 has more bases identical to the consensus than copy 2, else MP. If the right flank fails: badRF.

    Note what "absent" means here: not present enough to pass test 3. A copy of 90 bp, or one too decayed to share 100 identical bases with the consensus, counts as absent. That is why bank mode can lower the 100-base threshold to a share of the consensus length (CP_SINE_FRAC, default 50 % in bank mode: 52 bases for a 105 bp consensus), and why a ComPair "PM" with a short copy on the other side deserves a look at the alignment.

    Not in the toy: a right flank that fails only because of a second indel of 50 bp or more is re-tested with that gap masked (CP_INDEL_MIN); a window clipped by the end of a contig becomes contig_end (sequence missing, not absent); a too-short left flank is repaired first. Threshold names: CP_FLANK_LEN=150 CP_FLANK_NID=65 CP_FLANK_PID=65 CP_SINE_PID=65 CP_SINE_NID=100 CP_CUT_PID=70 CP_CUT_NID=120 (the defaults).

    Why 65 % for flanks? Unrelated DNA is not 0 % identical. Lab notes give a measured background: 2,000 random pairs of 70 bp genomic windows were on average 25 % identical when compared position by position, and an aligner that may open gaps can raise unrelated sequences to about 50 %. A flank threshold must sit clearly above that. Flanks of real orthologues between close species are far more similar.

    Check yourself

    1. Species X and Y both have a SINE copy at a locus; the outgroup has the empty site. What does the locus say? (That X and Y share an ancestor after the outgroup split off, unless it is homoplasy.)

    2. ComPair calls a locus PM, but genome 2 has a 90 bp piece of the SINE at the junction. Why? (The piece shares fewer than 100 identical bases with the consensus, so the "SINE in both" test fails.)

    3. Why are copies within 300 bp of another copy left out of the flank mapping? (Their flanks contain SINE sequence, which maps to many places: no unique anchor.)

    4. One locus out of twenty supports A+C, the rest A+B. Error? (Not necessarily: lineage sorting, which is expected after short internal branches. Test the conflict, for example with the KKSC statistics.)

    06Satellite DNA: monomers, arrays and higher-order repeats

    A SINE copy sits alone, between two stretches of unique DNA (05). Satellite DNA is the opposite: one short sequence copied head to tail, hundreds or millions of times, so that each copy's neighbours are more copies of itself. This section covers the vocabulary, how satellites are found (in reads and in assemblies), how the lab's CLsat workflow handles them, and why a satellite's copies must be turned to a common starting point before you compare them. It matters for a SINE lesson for two reasons: satellites can be built from pieces of SINEs, and a SINE search that does not recognise tandem arrays counts array units as if they were separate insertions.

    Words you need

    Tandem repeat
    Copies of a sequence lying directly next to each other, all in the same orientation: ACGTTACGTTACGTT…. The opposite of interspersed repeats such as SINEs, whose copies are scattered (01).
    Monomer (unit)
    The one sequence that is repeated. Its length is the period. Monomers range from a few base pairs to several thousand. Usage of the size names varies between papers; roughly: microsatellites have units of a few bp, minisatellites tens of bp, and satellites proper usually have units of about 100 bp or more in long arrays.
    Array
    One uninterrupted run of monomers at one place in the genome. An array can be a few hundred bp or several megabases. The same satellite usually has many arrays, on many chromosomes.
    Higher-order repeat (HOR)
    A block of several different monomer variants (call them a, b, c) that is itself repeated as a unit: abc abc abc. Neighbouring monomers differ, but every a is almost identical to every other a. The classic case is human alpha satellite.
    Centromeric satellite
    A satellite in the centromere. In humans the centromere is defined by proteins, not by sequence: it is the part of an alpha-satellite HOR array bound by the kinetochore protein CENP-A.
    Pericentromeric satellite
    A satellite in the regions flanking the centromere (the pericentromere), part of the densely packed, gene-poor heterochromatin. Satellites can also be subtelomeric: near chromosome ends.
    Satellite library
    The set of different satellite families present in one genome. Related species share much of the library but differ in which families are abundant (next heading).

    Rapid turnover and the library hypothesis

    Satellites change fast. Fry and Salser (1977), working on kangaroo-rat satellites, proposed that related species carry a common library of satellite sequences, and that each species amplifies some of them and lets others shrink; this gives each species its own satellite profile. Ugarković and Plohl (2002) review the evidence: satellite families behave as independent units whose copy number and sequence both change, and in some groups the profile changes a lot in a short evolutionary time. Within one family, copies tend to become similar to each other across the genome (concerted evolution).

    Melters et al. (2013) searched 282 species for high-copy tandem repeats and assumed the most abundant one is the centromere repeat, which held for most species with known centromeres. Such repeats were found in almost all animal and plant genomes, but their monomers varied widely in sequence and length (with long PacBio reads they found monomers up to 1,419 bp), and showed little sequence conservation beyond about 50 million years of divergence. In the complete human genome (T2T-CHM13), centromeric and pericentromeric repeats make up 6.2 % (189.9 Mb); each chromosome has one active alpha-satellite HOR array (HOR arrays tend to be large and homogeneous, often thousands of nearly identical HOR units), flanked by pericentromeric regions that can hold smaller arrays of diverged alpha-satellite monomers, other satellites (such as HSat2 and HSat3) and transposable elements (Altemose et al. 2022).

    Satellites made from SINEs

    Vassetzky, Kosushkin and Ryskov (2023) found tandem repeats built from parts of SINEs of three families (Squam1, Squam2, Squam3) in 56 squamate reptile genomes, and called them SINE-derived satellites (sSats). A locus counted as an sSat when it held more than four such tandem copies within 100 bp of each other. Units were roughly 70-290 nt (67 nt for the Squam3-derived sSat3 of snakes) and always shorter than the SINE they came from; each covers a piece of the SINE, for example the 3' part of the head with the B box plus part of the body. Snake sSats were similar in structure and copy number; geckos had a highly diverse set; agamids had none. The sSats are likely subtelomeric. The authors propose that the huge number of SINE copies raises the chance that one piece is duplicated in tandem (by unequal recombination) and then amplified: a "nonrandom process of satellite genesis originating from repetitive SINEs".

    In our projects (unpublished lab notes, bat SINEs). In the horseshoe bat Rhinolophus sinicus, a search with the SINE consensus MEG-RS hit long arrays whose repeating unit is 2,155 bp (GC 67.6 %); only 107 bp of that unit resemble the SINE. The same bat has a satellite with a 140-bp monomer repeated 58 times that is built from the head and body of one of its tRNA-derived SINE families. Both would inflate SINE copy numbers if every array unit were counted as an insertion. This is one reason our SINE workflow keeps only copies with unique flanks and flags arrays (07).

    Reading a dot plot

    A dot plot compares a sequence with itself: position along the sequence on both axes, and a dot wherever a short word at position x is identical to the word at position y. The main diagonal is always full (every word matches itself). A tandem array adds parallel diagonals: a line one monomer length away from the main diagonal, another two monomer lengths away, and so on, filling a square block. In an HOR array the lines one HOR length apart are much stronger than the lines one monomer apart, because only whole HOR units are nearly identical.

    Try it

You choose the monomer length and the HOR period (how many different monomer variants make one HOR unit; 1 = plain array, no HOR). The variants all descend from one random monomer and differ from it by the "variant divergence"; then every copy gets fresh point mutations. The dot plot marks every place where a word of the chosen length is identical in both positions. Below it, the offset profile shows, for each shift d, the share of array bases that equal the base d positions further on. Scaled down: real satellite monomers are usually 100 bp or more, and real arrays much longer.

Raise "mutations per copy" until the diagonals break into dashes; a shorter word recovers them but also fills the flanks with chance dots. This is how a dot plot (or a tool built on the same idea) separates the monomer from the HOR: both periods are real, at different levels.

    Simple explanation with examples: monomer, array, HOR

    Think of beads on a string. A monomer is one bead; an array is one string of beads. If all beads are the same colour, the string repeats every bead. If the beads come in a pattern red-green-blue, red-green-blue, the string repeats every three beads: that block of three is a higher-order repeat. Every red bead looks like every other red bead, but red and green differ.

    A tool that asks "what is the repeating unit?" can therefore give two right answers: one bead (the monomer, about 171 bp for human alpha satellite) or one red-green-blue block (the HOR, several monomers long). Tandem-repeat finders often report the same array at several periods; it is up to you to say which level you mean.

    On a dot plot, a single unique sequence shows only the main diagonal. A plain array shows a square full of evenly spaced diagonal lines. An HOR array shows the same square, but every third (or fourth, …) line is darker.

    Finding satellites in reads

    Long, nearly identical arrays are exactly what assemblers handle worst: reads from different places in the array look the same, so the assembly collapses or breaks them (07). Methods that work on unassembled reads avoid the problem.

    RepeatExplorer2 and TAREAN
    RepeatExplorer2 (Novák, Neumann and Macas 2020) clusters a small sample of short reads by similarity: each read is a point in a graph, reads are joined when they overlap, and groups of densely connected reads become clusters, each one repeat family. It runs on a Galaxy web server (repeatexplorer-elixir.cerit-sc.cz) or from the command line (GitHub kavonrtep/repex_tarean, also as a Docker image, tag 2.3.8). Its tandem-repeat part, TAREAN (Novák et al. 2017), recognises satellites by the shape of the cluster graph: reads from a tandem array overlap all the way round the monomer, so the graph closes into a circle; the circular shape is what flags a cluster as a putative satellite. TAREAN then rebuilds the monomer consensus from the most frequent k-mers in the cluster's reads. Because the reads are a random sample of the genome, the share of reads in a cluster estimates that repeat's share of the genome, without an assembly. The 2020 protocol also covers comparing several species and choosing satellite probes for FISH.
    k-mer counting
    The general idea: count every k-mer in the reads (lesson 01). Satellite k-mers occur thousands of times more often than single-copy k-mers, so they sit far out in the high-count tail of the k-mer spectrum. Chaining the most frequent k-mers that overlap each other spells out the monomer. TAREAN uses this step for its consensus; TRASH (below) uses repeated-k-mer density to find arrays in an assembly.

    Finding satellites in assemblies

    On an assembly, tandem-repeat finders find arrays without knowing the unit: TRF (a genome-wide table of arrays, units up to 2,000 bp), TRASH (monomers and higher-order structure of centromeric arrays) and TideCluster (groups arrays from the whole genome into families and builds consensus units with TAREAN). Their options and outputs are in lesson 03, section 04; a toy TRF is there too. Two points from that section matter here: TRF can report the same array at several periods (the monomer and multiples of it, as in the dot plot above), and an assembly only holds the arrays it managed to assemble.

    The lab's CLsat workflow

    The CLsat workflow (public repository Toki-bio/CLsat_workflow) is the code behind the lab's study of minisatellite-scale satellites (CLsat families) in Darevskia lizards; the manuscript is under revision. It is being turned into a reusable tool, working name satpipe, with one configuration file and 21 stages. It starts from whole assemblies and ends with a catalogue of array loci, each with a family, boundaries and orthology between species, plus a web viewer (CLsat-viewer). The stages, as the repository's documentation and scripts describe them:

    1. Find tandem repeats in every genome
      TRF on each whole assembly with 2 5 7 80 10 50 2000 -h (units up to 2,000 bp).
    2. Keep candidate satellite motifs
      From TRF's table, keep arrays with a period of 80-300 bp and at least 10 copies; each gives one consensus motif. The tool version adds a check, on by default, that cuts a motif that is really two or more units back to one unit (TRF can report an array at a multiple of its unit, for example period 292 for a 146-bp unit); the published run did not have this step.
    3. Drop motifs made of simple repeats
      TRF is run again on the motifs themselves; a motif with more than 50 bp covered by an internal simple repeat is set aside.
    4. Rotate every motif to a canonical start
      A monomer is a circle cut open at an arbitrary point, so the same unit can be reported starting anywhere. Each motif is rotated to its alphabetically smallest rotation, so identical monomers become identical strings. By default only the given strand is used; the repository records that one locus reported on the other strand never clustered for this reason and was added by hand.
    5. Cluster within each species, then across species
      VSEARCH --cluster_fast --id 0.90 per species; each cluster is aligned with MAFFT, rotated to a common start column and realigned; then all species are pooled with the known family consensuses placed first, so each known family anchors its own cluster.
    6. Assign clusters to families: a human decision
      Clusters were assigned to families by inspecting a UPGMA tree of the cluster centres. The repository stores this as a curated table, not as a computed result, and the pipeline stops and asks for it.
    7. Grow each seed into a whole array
      Each family member found by TRF is a seed. From each seed, the array is followed outward window by window (3 monomers per window) with ssearch36 against a query made of three tandem copies of the consensus, so any starting phase aligns. A window counts when it scores at least 50 bits with at least 0.67 of it aligned. Very long arrays switch to big jumps and then pinpoint the end.
    8. One locus universe, re-scored against every family
      Expanded intervals of all families are merged; every locus is scanned again with all family consensuses and given to the best one. A difference of 20 bits marks a contradiction, 15 an ambiguous call.
    9. Orthology from flanks
      600 bp of DNA on each side of each array (never inside another array), compared all against all with ssearch36; a pair needs at least 200 bp aligned at 80 % identity. All-against-all because an array can sit in opposite orientation in two assemblies, so a left flank can match a right flank. Same logic as SINE orthology (05).
    10. Subfamilies, array ends and a common frame
      The first two monomers of an array are aligned to doubled subfamily consensuses (two copies end to end), so the alignment works whatever the phase; the phase of the terminal monomer is read the same way. The last stage rotates every consensus the run writes into the frame of one anchor family.

                                      #   and write the cluster -> family table by hand

    The commands are from the repository README. It lists the outside programs needed: TRF, VSEARCH, MAFFT, FASTA (ssearch36), samtools and bedtools (to cut sequences and intervals out of genomes), GNU parallel (to run jobs side by side) and gawk (a text-processing language; the scripts need this version of awk). Every parameter left out of the configuration file takes the value of the published Darevskia run.

    A common frame before comparing

    Because a monomer is a circle, two copies of the same unit cut at different points look unrelated when aligned end to end: the alignment splits into two blocks. Before families are compared, their consensuses are rotated into one shared starting point (a frame), and monomers are then cut out of the arrays in that frame. Two working rules in the lab: rotate the circular consensus, then re-cut the copies with it, rather than rotating copies already cut from an array; and use a common frame only for families that are similar. For unrelated families "no common frame" exists; satpipe's anchor setting has an independent option for exactly this case, where each family keeps its own frame.

    07Satellites and young SINEs in assemblies

    The two kinds of repeat this lesson is about are exactly the two kinds that genome assemblers handle worst. Young SINE copies are nearly identical, so short-read assemblies stop at them. Satellite arrays are many identical monomers in a row, so assemblies shrink them, break them or lose them. Before you count copies, measure arrays or call a SINE "absent" at a locus, you have to know what the assembly did to these regions. This section shows why it happens, how to measure an array from reads instead of from the assembly, and how to check an assembly with reads.

    Words used in this section

    array
    One satellite locus: many copies of a monomer (the repeated unit) lying head to tail. Section 06 explains monomers and arrays.
    collapsed repeat
    A region where the genome has several copies but the assembly holds fewer, often only one. All the reads from all the copies then pile onto the few copies that are left.
    read depth
    The number of reads that cover a base after the reads are mapped back to the assembly. Over normal single-copy DNA it is close to the average coverage (lesson 1).
    spanning read
    A read that starts in unique DNA on one side of a repeat, runs through the whole repeat and ends in unique DNA on the other side. One such read shows the repeat's real length directly.
    N-run (gap)
    A run of the letter N in a scaffold: sequence the assembler knows is there but could not write out.

    Why young SINEs stop short-read assemblies

    A short-read assembler cuts reads into k-mers and joins k-mers that overlap (a de Bruijn graph, lesson 2, section 03). When thousands of SINE copies are identical, every copy gives the same k-mers, and the graph merges them all into one path with thousands of entries and exits. The assembler cannot tell which exit belongs to which entry, so it stops. The contig coming from the left ends k − 1 bases inside the copy, the contig from the right does the same from the other end, and the middle of the copy is in no contig. The calculator in lesson 2 shows this for real k values.

    Age decides which copies cause it. An old copy has collected its own mutations since it was inserted (section 04), so its k-mers are unique and the assembler walks straight through. Only copies of the youngest, still nearly identical subfamilies break the assembly. Long reads (PacBio HiFi about 15 kb, Oxford Nanopore often longer; lesson 2, section 04) are 50 or more times longer than a 300-base SINE, so every copy is crossed by many reads and the problem disappears.

    Simple explanation with examples: a contig that stops inside a young copy

    Toy genome with two copies of a young SINE (identical, in purple) and one old copy (two mutations, in red), k = 6:

    genome ...TTGCAGGCTCAGTGGACCTA ... GATCCGGCTCAGTGGTTAGC ... CCATGGGATCAGCGGTCAAT... contig 1 ...TTGCAGGCTC ← stops 5 (= k − 1) bases in: the next 6-mer, GGCTCA, occurs in both young copies contig 2 AGTGGACCTA ... ← the other side, also 5 bases deep old copy ...CCATGGGATCAGCGGTCAAT... ← its 6-mers are unique: assembled straight through

    Each contig ends k − 1 bases into the young copy. A scaffolder that later joins contig 1 and contig 2 puts an N-run between them, so the scaffold reads "flank, start of a SINE, NNNN, end of a SINE, flank". The bases of the young copy are not missing from the reads; the assembler just could not place them.

    Why satellite arrays shrink or vanish

    A satellite array is the same problem in its worst form: the repeat is not scattered but stacked. In a de Bruijn graph identical monomers make a loop: the path can go round once, ten times or a thousand times, and nothing in the k-mers says how many. In a long-read assembly (overlap–layout–consensus) the same thing happens when no read is longer than the array: two reads that both lie inside the array overlap in many ways, and the assembler cannot tell how far apart they really are. What you get in the assembly is one of these:

    • Collapsed: the array is written with fewer monomers than it has, sometimes one. All reads from the array map to that short piece, so its read depth is many times the average.
    • Broken: contigs end inside or at the edge of the array, and the array is cut into pieces or into a contig end plus an N-run.
    • Set aside: the array is assembled as a separate small contig made only of monomers, with no unique flank. It cannot be placed on a chromosome and ends up among the small unplaced scaffolds, or is removed.

    The last case matters more than it seems. Many projects remove contigs below a size cut-off before publishing, and those small contigs are exactly where satellite DNA collects. So a published assembly can hold far less of a satellite than the animal does. SINE-derived satellites of squamates are probably subtelomeric, that is, near chromosome ends (Vassetzky, Kosushkin & Ryskov 2023).

    Measuring an array: from the assembly, from depth, from k-mers

    There are three ways to know how long an array is, and they fail in different ways.

    1. Read it off the assembly
      Only trustworthy when the array is resolved: reads span it, or at least the assembly is checked with reads (next part). An array that touches a contig end or an N-run gives only a lower bound: "at least this long".
    2. Read depth
      Map the reads to the assembly, add up the depth over the array (or over the collapsed copy), and divide by the average depth of single-copy DNA:
      copies ≈ depth over the repeat ÷ average single-copy depth
      array length ≈ (read bases on the array) ÷ (average coverage)
      This works whether the array was collapsed or not. Its limits: random sampling (small for long arrays at good coverage, as in the toy), uneven library coverage (a bias that repeating does not reveal), and reads that map equally well to several places, which aligners report with mapping quality 0 and many depth tools then skip. And when one satellite family has many arrays in the genome, depth on a collapsed consensus tells you the total of all arrays, not the length of any one of them.
    3. k-mer counts
      No mapping needed. Count, in the raw reads, the k-mers that come from the monomer. A k-mer that occurs once in a haploid genome is seen about as often as the k-mer coverage of the main peak in the spectrum (lesson 1); a monomer k-mer seen 400 times as often lies in about 400 copies. Monomers in real arrays are not quite identical, so take the middle value (median) over all k-mers of the monomer. Comparing k-mer counts in reads and in the assembly is also how Merqury's copy-number spectrum finds collapsed repeats (lesson 2, section 08).
    Simple explanation with examples: depth as a copy counter

    Say your reads give 30× coverage. Over a single-copy gene, about 30 reads cover each base. Now take a satellite array of 50 identical monomers of 170 bases that the assembly collapsed into one monomer. Reads from all 50 monomers map to that one monomer, so its depth is about 50 × 30 = 1,500.

    single-copy gene depth 30 → 30 ÷ 30 = 1 copy collapsed monomer depth 1500 → 1500 ÷ 30 = 50 copies → about 50 × 170 = 8,500 bases of array same, library covers repeats 20 % less: depth 1200 → 40 copies (wrong, and nothing in the data says so)

    The same arithmetic with k-mers: if 21-mers from single-copy DNA are seen about 25 times and a monomer 21-mer is seen 1,250 times, there are about 1,250 ÷ 25 = 50 copies of it.

    Checking the assembly with reads

    The assembly is a hypothesis; the reads are the evidence. For SINE and satellite regions the checks are:

    • Spanning reads. Map long reads to the assembly and ask for reads whose alignment starts before the array and ends after it. One accurate spanning read shows the array's length and structure directly. If the array in the read is longer than in the assembly, the assembly collapsed it. The limit is coverage: at 10–15× only some arrays happen to be spanned (try it in the toy).
    • Depth along the assembly. Collapsed regions have depth that is a multiple of the average; falsely duplicated regions have half. HMM-Flagger (v1.2.0) reads the read alignments (its quick start uses HiFi), models the depth along the assembly with a hidden Markov model and labels every part as erroneous, falsely duplicated, haploid (correct) or collapsed.
    • Mixed bases in the reads. In a collapsed region the reads come from several copies that differ at a few positions, so at those positions the reads disagree. Vollger et al. (2019) used such paralogous sequence variants to pull collapsed segmental duplications apart. Their script NucFreq plots the most common and the second most common base at each position of the read alignments; a second base carried by many reads is either a heterozygous site or a sign of collapsed copies, and a run of such positions in one region is suspect (its README flags regions where the second base is in at least 10 % of reads at 5 or more positions within 500 bases).
    • k-mers. Merqury compares k-mer counts in the reads with copy numbers in the assembly (lesson 2, section 08). VerityMap (ablab) is built to map HiFi or Nanopore reads to assemblies of very long tandem repeats such as centromeres and reports likely errors from rare k-mers.
    • Empty sites need proof. For presence/absence of SINEs (section 05), "no SINE here" is only an observation when the region is assembled. A gap or a contig end at the insertion site looks like an absence. Only a read that runs from one flank to the other through the empty site proves it.

    What the lab's tools do at assembly gaps and edges

    • SINEderella runs assembly_qc.sh before step 1 (skip it with SKIP_ASSEMBLY_QC=1). It reports the share of N in the whole assembly, the share of N in the first and last 500 bases of every scaffold (N-padded scaffold edges give false SINE hits, says the script's header), N50 and the share of short scaffolds, flags each as ok / caution / warn and gives a verdict SOLID, CAUTION or WARN. Its satellite stage takes satellite arrays out of the SINE analysis as loci, writes them to their own tables and never deletes them (section 06).
    • CLsat_workflow grows each satellite locus outward from a seed until the satellite signal is lost. By default it stops at a scaffold boundary and records that as the reason (past_scaffold_start / past_scaffold_end); such an array is only "at least this long". For orthology it takes 600 bases of flank on each side and classes each flank as unique, telomeric, low-complexity or scaffold-truncated; only unique flanks are compared between assemblies.
    In our projects
    • Desmans (Galemys pyrenaicus, Desmana moschata), short-read assemblies. Many N-runs and contig ends sit inside young copies of the talpid SINE family Tal, about k − 1 bases deep, and some scaffold joins carry the same young SINE on both sides of an N-run, because the scaffolder joined two contigs that each ended in a copy. The reads are there: when the missing middles were restored, the reads covered them at about the depth of the flanks. In 26 long-read assemblies of related mammals there were no N-runs at young SINEs. Numbers are in lesson 2, section 03.
    • Rock lizards (Darevskia), satellite CLsat (work not yet published). We measure the amount of CLsat directly from raw reads and compare it with the assemblies. The two can differ a lot: in one draft assembly most of the satellite sat in the small contigs that a size cut-off removed, and another assembly held almost none of one CLsat family although the animal's reads hold it. Our current approach is "reads first": find the long reads that contain the satellite and use the ones that cross a whole array to define its ends. Most satellite reads lie entirely inside arrays, and the number of arrays that get a spanning read is limited by coverage. Published so far: in a survey of 56 squamate genomes, SINE-derived satellites were highly diverse in geckos but rare in Darevskia (Vassetzky, Kosushkin & Ryskov 2023).

    Syntax checked against the current manuals (samtools 1.25, minimap2 2.31, Jellyfish 2.3.1, HMM-Flagger 1.2.0). Coordinates and file names are examples.

    # 1. Map HiFi reads to the assembly (map-hifi = PacBio HiFi preset; use lr:hq for accurate Nanopore)
minimap2 -ax map-hifi -t 16 asm.fa hifi.fq.gz | samtools sort -@ 8 -o hifi.bam
samtools index hifi.bam

# 2. Spanning reads for an array at scaf12:200001-206800, with 1 kb of flank on each side
#    pos / endpos = first / last reference base of the alignment (samtools filter expressions)
samtools view -c -F 0x904 -e 'pos <= 199000 && endpos >= 207800' hifi.bam scaf12:200001-206800
#   -F 0x904 drops unmapped, secondary and supplementary alignments; -c only counts

# 3. Depth over the array vs over unique flank (-a also prints zero-depth positions)
samtools depth -a -r scaf12:200001-206800 hifi.bam | awk '{s+=$3} END {print s/NR}'
samtools depth -a -r scaf12:190001-199000 hifi.bam | awk '{s+=$3} END {print s/NR}'
#   ratio of the two = how many times more reads the array region attracts (collapse factor)
#   -Q sets a minimum mapping quality: leave it out: reads of a repeat that is present several times in the assembly map with quality 0, and -Q 1 drops them

# 4. k-mer copy number of a monomer, assembly-free: count only the monomer's 21-mers in the raw reads
jellyfish count -m 21 -s 100M -C -t 16 -o sat.jf --if monomer.fa <(zcat R1.fq.gz) <(zcat R2.fq.gz)
jellyfish dump -c sat.jf | sort -k2,2n | awk '{a[NR]=$2} END {print "median count", a[int((NR+1)/2)]}'
#   copies ≈ median count ÷ k-mer coverage of the main (homozygous) peak from lesson 1

# 5. Whole-assembly depth classes (erroneous / duplicated / haploid / collapsed) with HMM-Flagger:
#    follow the three steps in the Flagger README (bam2cov, then hmm_flagger, in its Docker image)

    Pitfalls. (1) Do not filter reads by mapping quality before measuring depth over repeats: where the assembly holds several copies, the reads you need are the ones that map ambiguously. (2) Long reads come in a spread of lengths, so a few reads are much longer than the median; spanning depends on those, not on the median. (3) An array at a scaffold end or next to an N-run has no measured length, only a minimum. (4) Depth on a consensus measures all arrays of that family together. (5) Before calling an assembly "satellite-poor", count the satellite in the raw reads.

    08A complete SINE study, step by step

    The earlier sections each took one piece: what a SINE is, how to find its copies, how to discover new ones, how to split them into subfamilies, how to compare two species, how to keep satellites apart. This section puts the pieces in the order our lab uses them, says which tool does each step, what goes in and what comes out, and what a finished study has to hand over.

    What a finished study delivers

    Our pipeline SINEderella states its aim in its own documentation (docs/AIM.md). We study interspersed repeats: elements whose copies sit in unique flanks. Copies that were duplicated together with their neighbourhood (a segmental duplication, a unit of a tandem array, an insertion hot spot) are not the subject: they are counted at most, never shown. The finished result has two parts:

    1. The technical result: the list of SINE loci of the genome, with a verdict for every copy and its family and subfamily.
    2. A page people can read: an HTML report with links to the alignments, conclusions on parts of the alignments, copy numbers, leak and divergence from the consensus.

    Both are made by the workflow itself, on every run, without manual checking. That is the reproducibility requirement: if you run it again on the same genome and the same consensus bank, you get the same list and the same page. A person still makes the decisions (is this a SINE, where does it end, is this a subfamily), but always by looking at alignments the workflow produced, never at a number alone.

    The steps

    1. Collect the known SINEss01
      Take consensus sequences of SINEs already described for your group and its relatives from SINEBase (Vassetzky and Kramerov 2013, Nucleic Acids Research 41:D83; it gives formal definitions of a SINE and classifies families by their parts) and from Dfam, plus consensuses from papers. This is your bank.
      in: literature, databases · out: bank.fa
    2. Scan the genome for new SINEss03
      SINE-de-novo-genome-scan cuts a SINE database into short pieces and searches each piece against the genome with ssearch36; each hit is widened by 50 bp on both sides and overlapping hits are merged into candidate loci, so hits up to about 100 bp apart join (the README says 500 bp; the code uses the 50-bp padding, s03). The idea: most SINEs share at least a little sequence with some known SINE. To look past the families you already know, mask their copies first and scan what is left (the minus bank).
      in: genome, SINE database · out: merged_loci.fa (candidates)
    3. Compress the candidates and looks04
      SubFam orders thousands of copies so that similar ones sit together, cuts the order into chunks of 50 and writes one consensus per chunk. A sample of 30,000 copies becomes about 600 rows that a person can read in ViewAlign, the lab's alignment viewer. SubFam only orders and averages; deciding which rows form a family or subfamily is the person's call.
      in: candidates (sample) · out: chunk-consensus alignment
    4. Set the boundaries by unique flankss03s05
      A SINE ends where its copies stop agreeing with each other. Past that point, the sequence of independent insertions is flank, and flanks of different copies should be no more alike than two random pieces of the genome. A consensus is built from the copies between those ends (for example with SINE_consensus).
      in: copies with flanks · out: new consensus
    5. Check the heads01
      Is the 5′ part derived from a tRNA, a 5S rRNA or a 7SL RNA? SINEderella's tools/trna_heads.sh compares every consensus with a set of tRNA genes, and compares 20 shuffled versions of it the same way; a head counts only when it scores better than every shuffle. It also applies SINEBase's rule (at least 60 % identity over at least 60 nt), and can add Rfam and tRNAscan-SE. Degraded heads are often missed by the last two; "not detected" is not "not tRNA-derived".
      in: bank.fa, tRNA gene set · out: head origin per consensus
    6. Collect every copys02
      SINEderella step 1 runs sear with each consensus: the genome is cut into 2,500 bp windows (2,000 bp step), and a hit is kept when it covers at least 80 % of the consensus at at least 65 % identity (sear -k query genome 0.8 65 50). Hits of all consensuses are merged into loci. Satellite-like hits are screened out here (s06, s07).
      in: genome, bank.fa · out: merged loci (BED), copies (FASTA)
    7. Assign copies to subfamiliess04
      Step 2 (asSINEment): 10 rounds of ssearch36; a copy is assigned only when the same consensus wins all 10, and its bitscore reaches 0.45 × that subfamily's N-th best score. Step 3 adds per-copy similarity to the consensus, leak and conflict flags, and a soft assignment for copies that missed the strict rule (nothing is thrown away).
      in: copies, bank.fa · out: assignment table
    8. Give every copy a verdicts05
      Flank tests decide what counts as an interspersed copy. Per subfamily, step 7 widens the flank window in 50 bp steps (up to 1 kb) until copy pairs are no more alike there than random genome pairs. Per copy, a flank-twin check compares each copy's flanks with those of the other copies of its family. Result, in loci_verdict.tsv: SINE (assigned, flanks unique), grey (flanks could not be tested) or not_SINE.
      in: assignment table, genome · out: loci_verdict.tsv
    9. Make the report and decide on alignmentss04
      Step 4 draws divergence histograms against the consensus; step 8a builds, per subfamily, an alignment of the 100 best and 100 random SINE loci with 50 bp of left and 70 bp of right flank, consensus on the top row; step 6 writes report.html with copy numbers, leak, divergence, head origin and a ViewAlign link for every alignment. You open the alignments and judge: real subfamily or mixture, right ends or not.
      in: run folder · out: report.html, alignments
    10. Compare species: orthologous locis05
      With a second genome, SINE_orth_loc maps the flanks of each copy into the other genome and sorts the locus pairs into SINE, PM and MP. In its bank mode, the SINE loci come straight from SINEderella's verdict table (sinederella_bed.py); only copies with verdict SINE are used.
      in: two genomes, two SINEderella runs · out: orth_sp1-sp2.tsv, alignment bundles
    11. Loop back
      A new consensus from the minus-bank scan or from a mixed subfamily goes back into the bank: SINEderella --add new.fa searches only the new consensus and re-assigns the copies it touches.

    Where do you start? A step tracker

    Not every study starts at step 1. Pick what you have, and the tracker lists the tool chain in order, with the files each step needs and makes. Tick a step when it is done; the tracker shows what you hold and what comes next. "What if I skip it?" says what goes wrong without that step.

    Simple explanation with examples: why this order?

    Think of a museum collection of beetles. First you read the literature (step 1: known SINEs). Then you sort through unlabelled drawers for anything beetle-like (step 2: de novo scan). You lay specimens out in rows so similar ones are side by side (step 3: SubFam), and decide where one species ends and another begins (step 4: boundaries). Only then do you count every specimen of each species in all the drawers (steps 6-7), and you drop the ones that are just duplicate photographs of the same specimen (step 8: shared flanks = one insertion copied twice).

    Doing it in another order costs you. If you count copies before the boundaries are right, the consensus is too long or too short and copies are missed or merged. If you skip the flank test, a segmental duplication carrying 20 copies of one SINE looks like 20 independent insertions and inflates the count of a "young" subfamily.

    Example from the tracker: start with "Two genomes" and skip the verdict step. The orthology run then also gets copies whose flanks are shared with other copies; their flanks can map to several places in the other genome, and the presence/absence call is less reliable.

    In our projects

    Our work on species A, a rodent, and a close relative walks most of this chain for one tRNA-derived SINE family. Known SINEs: a first bank of the 58 SINEBase consensuses classed as mammalian, then a bank for the family found (its SINEBase consensus plus four subfamily consensuses from the literature). Collect: more than a million loci in each genome, about five in six of them firmly assigned, most to one subfamily. Minus bank: with those loci masked, the de novo scan of the whole remaining genome took about a tenth of the time projected for the full genome and gave tens of thousands of candidates; a 30,000-copy sample went through SubFam into 600 rows per species. That de novo part is marked in our log as preliminary and still waiting for manual review. Orthology: several hundred thousand locus pairs, most with the SINE in both species and the rest split about evenly between the two. Two honest limits: whether a one-species locus is a new insertion or a deletion is not settled (a third, more distant species is the intended outgroup), and this orthology run took all firmly assigned copies, because it was made before the per-copy flank verdicts that bank mode now uses.

    Reproducible on purpose

    • The page is an output, not a write-up. report.html, the alignments and their ViewAlign links are written by the run itself. Nobody edits numbers into it by hand.
    • Random choices are seeded. Samples for SubFam use a fixed seed in our runs; the random genome windows that set the flank background in step 7 are drawn with a fixed seed in the code.
    • Alignments repeat exactly. MAFFT's iterative refinement gave a different alignment on every multi-threaded run; SINEderella now passes --threadit 0 to every such call, which gives the same alignment for any thread count (from its PUBLISH_WORKFLOW.md).
    • Scores that wobble are filtered, not trusted. ssearch36 shuffles sequences to estimate its statistics, so bitscores change a little between runs. The 10-out-of-10 vote keeps only copies whose subfamily does not depend on that wobble (s04).
    • Record what ran. SubFam writes its version and command line into its log; our project logs record the repository commit of each run. SINEderella's manual also describes exporting run results to the public SINEdb pages.

    The numbers in this lesson come from a few SINE families in a few genomes: the tRNA-derived Tal SINEs of talpids (moles, desmans, shrew moles), one SINE family in two rodents (species A and a close relative), a few lab tests on other genomes that are marked as such where they appear, and the published examples cited in each section.
