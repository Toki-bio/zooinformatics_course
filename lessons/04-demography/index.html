Lesson 4 · what one animal's genome says about its ancestors

Population History from One Genome

The two copies of every chromosome in one animal trace back to common ancestors that lived thousands to millions of generations ago, and how long ago they met depends on how large the population was. This lesson reads that record. It starts with the idea behind every method (the coalescent), then builds the input from one diploid genome, runs PSMC and reads its curve, turns model units into years and animals with a mutation rate and a generation time, and compares PSMC with MSMC2, SMC++, cobraa and methods that need many animals. Then come the checks that tell a real feature from an artefact, the two-species case (split time and gene flow with hPSMC, BPP and gIMble), and what a report must state so that others can judge and redraw the curve.

The running examples are two closely related rodents with one male genome each: our nanopore genome of a species called species A here, and the public HiFi assembly of a close relative ("the relative"). All their dates use placeholder rates, which is itself one of the lessons. Questions that need many animals (allele frequencies, recent history in detail, the standard gene-flow tests) are only outlined here; DNA methylation and an in-depth treatment of SINEs and satellites are left for later lessons. Hover over a dotted term to see what it means.

01What one genome remembers

One diploid animal carries two copies of every autosome. Followed back through the generations, the two copies of any stretch of DNA meet in one ancestral copy. How long ago that happened depends on how large the population was: in a small population two copies find their common ancestor quickly, in a large one it takes a long time. Recombination has cut the genome into many short stretches, each with its own ancestor and its own date. Heterozygosity records those dates. This section builds the intuition that every method in this lesson rests on, before any program is run.

Two copies, one ancestor

Coalescence
Two gene copies, traced back in time, "coalesce" in the generation where they come from the same single parental copy. From there back they share one history. The mathematical model of this backward process is the coalescent.
TMRCA
Time to the most recent common ancestor: how many generations back the two copies coalesce. In this lesson it is always the TMRCA of the two copies inside one animal, the maternal and the paternal one.
Effective population size (Ne)
The size of an idealised population (random mating, constant size, every individual equally likely to be a parent) that would give the same rate of coalescence as the real one. It is not a head count of the animals alive, and methods based on the coalescent measure exactly this: Spence et al. (2018) define the effective size these methods infer as "the inverse coalescence rate for a pair of haplotypes".
Haplotype
One of the two copies of a chromosome (or of a stretch of it): the one from the mother or the one from the father.
Simple explanation with examples: two copies of one gene meeting in the past

Take one gene in one mouse. Its maternal copy came from one of the mother's two copies, which came from one of a grandparent's two copies, and so on. The paternal copy has its own path. Picture both paths going back through a population of 1,000 mice, that is 2,000 copies of the gene in each generation. In each generation the two paths pick their parental copy, and the chance that both land on the same copy is 1 in 2,000. On average that takes 2,000 generations; sometimes it happens after 50 generations, sometimes after 6,000.

Now make the population 100,000 mice: the chance per generation drops to 1 in 200,000, and the paths wander apart for about 200,000 generations on average. During all that time mutations accumulate on both paths. So the two copies of a gene in an animal from a large population differ at more sites: a large population leaves a heterozygous genome, a small one a homozygous genome.

small population mother's copy ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGG father's copy ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGG ancestor few generations back: 0 differences large population mother's copy ACGTCAGCATGACCGTAGACGTTACG father's copy ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGG ancestor far back: 3 differences

A genome is not one gene. Every few hundred to few thousand bases, recombination in some ancestor switched which ancestral copy the stretch came from, so each stretch has its own ancestor and its own TMRCA. One genome therefore holds a large sample of TMRCAs, and their spread tells you when the population was large or small.

How fast two copies find their ancestor

In the idealised population of Ne diploid individuals there are 2Ne copies of each autosomal stretch. Going back one generation, the chance that the two copies come from the same parental copy is one in 2Ne. This is the starting point of the PSMC model (the method of s03), which measures time in units of 2N0 generations (N0 is a reference size that the program estimates, close to the most recent size) and sets the coalescence rate at time t to 1/λ(t), where N(t) = N0 λ(t) is the size at that time and λ the size relative to N0 (Li & Durbin 2011, model notes in the PSMC repository).

chance of coalescing per generation = 1 / (2 Ne) mean TMRCA = 2 Ne generations

The waiting time is very variable: with a constant size it follows an exponential distribution, so its spread is as large as its mean. With Ne = 10,000, the mean TMRCA is 20,000 generations, but half of all stretches coalesce within about 13,900 generations (2Ne × ln 2) and one in twenty takes longer than 60,000. That spread is not noise to be averaged away: it is the information. If the size changed over time, the coalescence rate changed with it, and the TMRCAs pile up in the periods when the population was small.

Heterozygosity is a clock

While the two copies are separate, each collects mutations. If the mutation rate is μ per base per generation and the TMRCA is T generations, the two copies have been evolving independently for 2T generations in total, so they differ at about 2μT of their bases. A stretch with a recent common ancestor has almost no heterozygous sites; a stretch with an old one has many. Averaged over the genome under a constant size this gives the classic expectation for the expected heterozygosity per base, written θ:

heterozygosity of a stretch ≈ 2 μ T genome average (constant size) ≈ 2 μ × 2 Ne = 4 Ne μ = θ

Run it backwards and one number already gives a size. Take a genome with 1.5 heterozygous SNVs per kb (0.0015 per base; a round example value). With an illustrative mutation rate of 5.4 × 10−9 per base per generation, Ne = 0.0015 / (4 × 5.4 × 10−9) ≈ 69,000; a genome with twice the heterozygosity gives twice that size. These are long-term averages over the whole history the genome samples, and they scale with the mutation rate you assume (s04). The rest of the lesson is about getting the history instead of one average.

Recombination cuts the genome into many histories

Two copies with a TMRCA of T generations are connected through 2T meioses. Each meiosis can place a crossover anywhere, so the longer ago the ancestor, the more often the stretch has been cut, and the shorter the piece that still shares that one ancestor. With a recombination rate of r per base per generation, the piece has an exponentially distributed length with mean 1/(2rT) bases (the PSMC model notes give this segment length as exponential with rate ρt in scaled units, which is the same thing).

By that arithmetic a mammal genome of a few gigabases is a mosaic of millions of such pieces. Li & Durbin (2011) put it this way: a diploid genome "harbors hundreds of thousands of independent loci, each with its own TMRCA between the two alleles", and the history can be read "by studying how the local density of heterozygous sites changes across the genome". The pieces are too short to see one by one: each holds only a few heterozygous sites. Methods like PSMC never cut the genome into pieces; they weigh all possible TMRCAs along the sequence at once with a hidden Markov model (s03).

When the population size changes

If Ne changed through time, the per-generation chance 1/(2Ne) changed with it. Going back from today, the chance that a stretch has not yet coalesced by generation T is the product of the "no" answers of all earlier generations, which for sizes constant within periods gives:

P(not yet coalesced by T) = exp( − Σ over periods of generations in the period before T / (2 Ne of that period) )

A period with a small Ne is a period with a high coalescence rate. A bottleneck therefore catches most stretches that are still separate when it starts, and a deep or long one catches nearly all of them. That has two consequences you can see in the toy below: the coalescence times pile up inside the bottleneck, and few stretches are left to tell anything about the time before it. A large recent population, in contrast, leaves few coalescences in the recent past, so the recent part of the history rests on little data.

What to notice. (1) Constant: the TMRCAs spread over two orders of magnitude around 2Ne = 100,000 generations; the chromosome is heterozygous almost everywhere, and the zoom shows pieces of a few hundred bases. (2) Bottleneck (Ne 3,000 from 2,000 to 20,000 generations): almost all coalescences fall inside the bottleneck, heterozygosity drops about ten-fold, the pieces become several kb long, and the histogram shows almost nothing older: the genome has forgotten most of what happened before. (3) Old bottleneck (Ne 5,000 from 20,000 to 200,000 generations): nearly every piece that is still separate at 20,000 generations coalesces soon after, so this genome says almost nothing about the large population before 200,000 generations, and its heterozygosity is low although the recent population is large. (4) Expansion: a large recent population means few recent coalescences, so the recent past is poorly sampled. (5) Press new animal: the 50-kb window heterozygosity jumps around between animals with the same history; one genome is one draw.

From the mosaic back to the history

The toy runs forward: history → TMRCAs → heterozygous sites. Demographic inference runs it backwards. PSMC (s03) reads the density of heterozygous sites along the genome, infers the distribution of TMRCAs, and turns it into a coalescence rate per period, reported as Ne. Three limits follow directly from the picture above:

  • It is a coalescence rate, not a census. The curve is the inverse of the rate at which two copies coalesce. It equals a population size only if the population was one randomly mating unit. In a population split into partly connected groups, a curve of changing size can appear even when no group ever changed size (Mazet et al. 2016 call the curve the "inverse instantaneous coalescence rate", IICR). Spence et al. (2018) add that a structured population has a larger effective size than a randomly mating one of the same census size. More in s06.
  • Sharp changes come out blurred. Li & Durbin (2011) simulated a ten-fold drop at 100,000 years and PSMC spread it "over the preceding 50,000 years".
  • The edges are thin. Few pieces coalesce very recently (recent pieces are long, so there are few of them) and few survive very far back. In Li & Durbin's simulations of a human-like history the estimate was precise between 20,000 years and 3 million years ago; more recently or further back "few recombination events are left" and power drops. The window for another species depends on its size history, mutation rate and generation time (s04).

Two things one genome cannot give at all: the history of the last few hundred generations in detail (that needs many individuals, so that many pairs of copies coalesce recently; s05), and the allele frequencies that population-genetic tests between species rely on (s07).

In our projects

One male each of two closely related rodents: species A (our long-read genome) and a close relative with a public HiFi chromosome-level assembly ("the relative" in the rest of this lesson). Counted over the callable sequence, the relative had about 1.7 times as many heterozygous SNVs per kb as species A, so by the formula above its long-term Ne is about 1.7 times larger at any mutation rate the two share. The heterozygosity is far from uniform: about three times as much of the species A genome as of the relative's lies in runs of homozygosity (50-kb window method; lesson 3, s09). Those runs are the pieces with very recent TMRCAs; the rest of the genome holds the older history that the following sections read.

02Preparing the input: one diploid genome

PSMC does not read your reads, your VCF or your assembly. It reads one long string of letters, one letter per 100 bases, that says only "here the two chromosome copies differ", "here they do not", or "here we do not know". Everything you decide while making that string (which reads count, which bases you trust, which chromosomes you leave out) goes straight into the curve. This section builds the string step by step, with the exact commands and defaults of the tools, and shows with a toy genome how loose filters invent heterozygous sites and strict ones throw real ones away.

What the input is

Diploid consensus
One sequence per contig in which every base is written as what the animal carries on both copies. Where the two copies differ, the base is written as a two-base IUPAC code (an A/G heterozygous site becomes R). Bases you do not trust are written in lower case or as N.
Callable bases
Positions where the reads were good enough that a heterozygous site, if present, would have been seen (lesson 3, s09). PSMC treats every base outside them as missing, not as homozygous.
psmcfa
The PSMC input file, written by fq2psmcfa. It looks like FASTA, but each letter stands for a 100-bp bin: K = at least one heterozygous site in the bin, T = no heterozygous site, N = (almost) no callable bases. The K here has nothing to do with the IUPAC K (G or T); it is just the letter Heng Li chose.
diploid consensus ACGTTAGCRTTAGGCATCCAG... nnnnnnnnnnnnnnnn... GGCTTA... psmcfa (100-bp bins) K N T
Simple explanation with examples: why PSMC only needs "differ or not" per 100 bases

PSMC looks along the genome for how far apart the differences between your animal's two chromosome copies are. Where the two copies trace back to a common ancestor a long time ago, mutations have had time to pile up, and differences are close together. Where the common ancestor is recent, differences are rare, so long stretches have none. The pattern of "dense" and "sparse" stretches is what PSMC turns into a history (s03).

For that it does not need to know which bases differ, only roughly where. So the genome is cut into 100-base boxes and each box gets one of three marks. In a mammal with about 2 differences per 1,000 bases, most boxes are T (no difference) and about one in five to ten is K. A box you could not read gets N, and PSMC simply skips it.

The danger is a box with the wrong mark. A sequencing error or two merged repeat copies turn a T into a false K: the animal looks more diverse, and the population looks larger. A real difference that your filter removed turns a K into a false T: the population looks smaller. A missing box marked T instead of N has the same effect. Good input means: as few wrong marks as possible, and as many honest Ns as needed.

1. Mapping and duplicates

Mapping and variant calling for one genome were covered in lesson 3, s09; use the same BAM. Two points matter here. First, map to a purged primary assembly (lesson 2, s06): if both haplotypes of a region are still in the assembly, each copy collects the reads of one haplotype and looks homozygous, which PSMC reads as a recent common ancestor. Second, in short-read libraries made with PCR, several reads can be copies of one DNA fragment. Mark them with samtools fixmate -m and samtools markdup (command in lesson 3). Both later tools skip reads flagged as duplicates by default: bcftools mpileup skips UNMAP,SECONDARY,QCFAIL,DUP, and mosdepth excludes flag 1796 (the same four).

2. Route A: the PSMC README route (bcftools + vcf2fq)

The PSMC README makes the diploid consensus in one pipe. Its example uses the 2011-era syntax (samtools mpileup … | bcftools view -c); with current bcftools the same thing is bcftools mpileup | bcftools call -c.

# bcftools 1.25, PSMC (lh3/psmc, 2025). Example for a mean depth of 30x: -d 10 (a third), -D 60 (twice)
# autosomes.bed = the autosomal contigs only (no X/Z, Y/W, mitochondrion); a .bed file is read as 0-based
bcftools mpileup -R autosomes.bed -f asm.fa -Ou aln.bam \
  | bcftools call -c -Ov \
  | vcfutils.pl vcf2fq -d 10 -D 60 | gzip > diploid.fq.gz

fq2psmcfa -q20 diploid.fq.gz > diploid.psmcfa
  • Why call -c and not call -m. vcf2fq reads three fields of the INFO column (where the caller writes extra values for each position) of every line: FQ (is the site heterozygous, and how sure), MQ (the RMS mapping quality) and DP (depth). In the bcftools source, FQ is written only by the original consensus caller (-c); the newer multiallelic caller (-m) writes MQ but no FQ, so its output does not fit vcf2fq (our reading of both sources; we did not run this combination).
  • All sites, not just variants. Do not add -v to call. vcf2fq needs a line for every position; positions without a line (no reads) become n.
  • What vcf2fq does (defaults -d 3 -D 100000 -Q 10 -l 5): a base is written in upper case only if its depth is between -d and -D and its RMS mapping quality is at least -Q; otherwise it is written in lower case, which fq2psmcfa treats as missing. Five bases (-l) on each side of a called indel are also put in lower case. A heterozygous SNV is written as its IUPAC code. The quality line of the FASTQ is the consensus quality.
  • Depth rule of thumb (README): "set -d to a third of the average depth and -D to twice". Measure the mean on the autosomes only.
  • Two traps. bcftools mpileup keeps at most 250 reads per position (-d 250), so for very deep data raise it above twice the mean, or the upper depth bound never acts. The README example also uses -C50; the current bcftools manual says it does "not recommend use of this parameter unless you fully understand the impact of it", so we left it out.

3. Route B: a modern caller plus a callable mask

DeepVariant (Illumina, HiFi, ONT) and Clair3 (ONT, HiFi) call long reads better than bcftools (lesson 3, s09 has their commands; for any non-human genome Clair3 needs --include_all_ctgs). Their VCFs list variants, not every base, so the mask has to come from elsewhere: a depth/mapping-quality BED from mosdepth, minus whatever else you exclude. The PSMC README shows the way for VCF + BED input (written for dipcall, a tool that calls variants by comparing two assembled haplotypes with a reference, but it works for any one-sample VCF): put the heterozygous sites into the assembly as IUPAC codes, put everything outside the mask in lower case, then bin.

# 1. callable mask: mosdepth 0.3.15, MAPQ >= 20, depth 10-60x for a 30x mean (bins are half-open: 10:61 = 10..60)
export MOSDEPTH_Q0=LOW MOSDEPTH_Q1=CALLABLE MOSDEPTH_Q2=HIGH
mosdepth -n -Q 20 --quantize 0:10:61: -t 4 cov aln.bam
zcat cov.quantized.bed.gz | awk '$4=="CALLABLE"' | grep -v -w -f sex_and_mt_contigs.txt > callable.bed
bedtools subtract -a callable.bed -b simple_repeats.bed > mask.bed      # optional: TRF / low complexity

# 2. heterozygous, biallelic PASS SNVs inside the mask (add an allele-fraction filter as in lesson 3 if wanted)
bcftools view -f PASS -v snps -m2 -M2 -g het -T mask.bed -Ov -o het.vcf merge_output.vcf.gz

# 3. consensus with IUPAC codes, lower case outside the mask, then 100-bp bins (seqtk 1.5; vcf2snp.pl is in psmc/utils)
seqtk seq -U asm.fa > asm.upper.fa                     # soft-masked assembly? make it upper case first
vcf2snp.pl het.vcf > het.snp                           # contig, position, ref base, IUPAC code
seqtk mutfa asm.upper.fa het.snp | seqtk seq -cM mask.bed -l80 - > diploid.fa
fq2psmcfa diploid.fa > diploid.psmcfa

A soft-masked assembly silently masks every repeat. fq2psmcfa treats every lower-case base as missing. Many assemblies are delivered soft-masked, with repeats in lower case (lesson 3, s04). seqtk mutfa keeps the case of the bases it does not change, so on a soft-masked assembly all interspersed repeats (often 30 to 50 % of a mammal genome) become N bins without anyone deciding so. Whether you want that is a decision (see below), not an accident: convert to upper case first (seqtk seq -U) and mask repeats on purpose with a BED.

5. fq2psmcfa and its 100-bp bins

What fq2psmcfa does, read from its source (lh3/psmc, utils/fq2psmcfa.c):

  • Every lower-case base, every N, and (in FASTQ input) every base with quality below -q counts as missing. The default is -q 10; the README uses -q20. With FASTA input there is no quality line, so -q does nothing.
  • A base counts as heterozygous if its letter stands for exactly two bases (R Y M K S W).
  • The sequence is cut into bins of 100 bases (-s). A bin becomes N if more than 90 % of its bases are missing, K if any of its non-missing bases is heterozygous, and T otherwise. A bin with 15 good bases and no heterozygous site therefore counts as a full homozygous bin; some pipelines (ours too) use a stricter rule, such as N when fewer than 50 bases are callable.
  • A whole contig is left out if fewer than 10,000 of its bases (-g) or less than 20 % of them are good. Short contigs of a fragmented assembly disappear here without a message.

Because a bin only says "at least one", two heterozygous sites 30 bases apart count once. At mammal heterozygosity this loses little; at the 2 % divergence between two species it saturates the bins, which is why the pseudo-hybrid analysis in s07 uses smaller bins.

6. Checks before you run PSMC

  • How much is left. The share of N bins is your missing data. Compare it with the 25 % guide above, and report it with the callable length.
  • Heterozygosity. Het sites per callable kb, and the share of K bins, should match what you measured in lesson 3, s09. A much higher value points to false heterozygous sites; a much lower one to missed sites or unmasked missing data.
  • The X as a free control. In a male mammal (female bird), the X (Z) outside the PAR has no real heterozygous sites. Run the same filters on it: whatever heterozygous sites remain are an estimate of your false-positive rate.
  • Missed sites at low depth. The README calls not correcting for low coverage "the most common pitfall when using PSMC". If a known share of heterozygous sites is missed (the FNR), PSMC behaves as if the mutation rate were lower; psmc_plot.pl -M "sample=0.1" corrects a plot for 10 % FNR, or you divide θ0 (the scaled mutation rate PSMC estimates, s04) by (1 − FNR) in your own scaling. Li writes that he has not found a reliable way to estimate the FNR; he suggests downsampling a deep genome to the lower coverage and measuring it, but has not done this himself.
  • A second filter set. Make a second psmcfa with different but reasonable filters (for example with and without an allele-fraction filter). Parts of the curve that change between them are not supported by the data (s06).
  • File integrity. Check downloaded reads with their md5 checksum (a fingerprint of the file's content, published by the archive) and gzip -t, not by file size.

03PSMC: reading population size from one genome

PSMC (the pairwise sequentially Markovian coalescent, Li & Durbin 2011) takes the heterozygous sites of one diploid genome and returns a curve of "effective population size" through time. It is the most used method of this lesson and the one every other method is compared with. This section explains what the program actually does, what each option means, how to bootstrap it, how to plot it, and what the curve can and cannot tell you.

The idea in four terms

Coalescence and TMRCA
Follow the two copies of one piece of chromosome back in time. At some generation they come from the same ancestral copy: they coalesce. The time back to that copy is the TMRCA (time to the most recent common ancestor). The older the TMRCA, the more mutations have built up on the two lineages, so the more heterozygous sites that piece carries.
Segments
Recombination cuts the chromosome into pieces with different ancestors, so the TMRCA changes along the genome. Neighbouring positions usually share an ancestor, which is why a genome can be read as a mosaic of segments, each with its own age.
Coalescence rate and population size
In a single randomly mating (panmictic) population of N diploid individuals, two lineages coalesce with probability 1/(2N) per generation. A small population makes coalescence fast (many young segments); a large one makes it slow (many old segments). PSMC turns the distribution of segment ages back into N through time.
Hidden Markov model (HMM)
A model that walks along the sequence, at each step in one of several hidden states it cannot see directly (here: the TMRCA of that position), and scores what it does see (heterozygous or not) under each state. The Markov part means the state at the next step depends only on the current one; in PSMC a state change is a recombination event.
Simple explanation with examples: how one genome holds many ages

Line up the two copies of a chromosome of one mouse and mark every difference. You will see stretches with almost no differences and stretches where they are dense:

copy 1 ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGGATCCAGTTACAGGCTACCTAGTACGGTAGCT copy 2 ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGGACCCAGTTATAGGCTGCCTAGTACGATAGCT |--- young segment: few hets ---||---- old segment: many hets ----|

The left piece was inherited from an ancestor who lived, say, 20,000 generations ago; the right piece from one who lived 200,000 generations ago and had ten times as long to collect mutations. In a 2.5-Gb genome there are millions of such pieces. If the population was small 50,000 years ago, many pieces will have coalesced then, and the genome will hold an excess of segments of that age. PSMC finds the population-size history that best explains how often each age appears.

Two limits follow directly. Very young segments are rare (two copies rarely share an ancestor that recent unless the population is tiny), so the recent past is poorly sampled. Very old segments are rare too, because most pairs of lineages have found their common ancestor long before, and they are short (recombination had a long time to cut them), so the far past is poorly sampled as well. And each segment carries only a few heterozygous sites, so the age of any single segment is known only roughly: the curve is a blurred image of the history.

What PSMC does, step by step

  1. Input: one line of bins per sequence
    The callable genome, cut into 100-bp bins: T = no heterozygous site, K = at least one, N = not enough callable bases. How to build this .psmcfa file is in s02.
    fq2psmcfa · our own script
  2. Hidden states: discretised coalescence times
    Time is cut into atomic intervals (64 with the usual setting). Each bin's hidden state is the interval its TMRCA falls in. The chance of a K bin depends on that state: older TMRCA, more mutations.
  3. Transitions: recombination
    Moving to the next bin, a recombination can change the TMRCA. How often it changes and to which interval follows from the coalescent with the current population sizes.
  4. Fit by EM
    The EM algorithm adjusts the scaled mutation rate θ0, recombination rate ρ0 and one relative size λ per parameter group, round after round (-N).
    psmc
  5. Scale and plot
    Everything comes out in units of 2N0 generations; a mutation rate and a generation time turn it into years and individuals.
    psmc_plot.pl · s04

The time intervals and the -p pattern

PSMC cuts time between 0 and a maximum Tmax (option -t, in units of 2N0 generations) into n atomic intervals whose boundaries grow exponentially: fine near the present, coarse in the past. From the source (core.c):

tk = 0.1 × ( eβ·k − 1 ), β = ln(1 + 10·Tmax) / n, k = 0 … n (last interval: Tmax to ∞)

Estimating a separate size in each of 64 intervals would overfit, so the -p pattern ties neighbouring intervals to one shared parameter. In "4+25*2+4+6" each number is a group and 25*2 means 25 groups of 2:

The .psmc output

A text file with one block per EM round, each block starting with RD and the round number (RD 0 = the starting values). The lines that matter:

  • MM: messages, including the version (MM Version: 0.6.5-r…), the pattern and the number of bins used (sum_L) and heterozygous bins (sum_n).
  • LK: the log-likelihood of the data; it should rise and level off over the rounds.
  • TR θ0 ρ0: scaled mutation and recombination rate per bin. θ0 sets the scale of everything: N0 = θ0 / (4 μ s), with μ per site per generation and s the bin size (100).
  • RS k tk λk …: one line per atomic interval: its start time (units of 2N0), the relative size λk, and, in our reading of the source, the number of recombinations the model expects in that interval (sum it over each parameter group to check the README's "about 10" rule). Size = λk × N0; time = tk × 2N0 generations.

Bootstrap

A single curve has no error bars. splitfa cuts every sequence of the .psmcfa into pieces of 500,000 bins (50 Mb at 100-bp bins; the second argument changes it; a remainder shorter than half a piece stays attached to the last piece). With -b, PSMC draws pieces at random with replacement until it has the original total length, and fits that. Twenty to a hundred such rounds, drawn as thin lines around the main curve, show how much the curve would change if the genome had been a different sample of segments. Li & Durbin used 5-Mb pieces; the README example uses 100 rounds.

What bootstraps do not measure. They resample the same calls under the same model. False heterozygous sites, a bad callable mask, a pattern that overfits the first interval, or population structure move every bootstrap line together, and the band stays narrow. Those need the separate checks of s06.

What the y axis really is

PSMC estimates how fast two lineages coalesced at each time and reports the inverse of that rate, scaled as a population size. Mazet et al. (2016) named this quantity the IICR (inverse instantaneous coalescence rate) and showed that it "is equivalent to a population size only in panmictic models". With subpopulations exchanging migrants, a population of constant size can give a curve with apparent bottlenecks and expansions, and Li & Durbin themselves offered population structure as an alternative reading of the human hump between 60 and 250 thousand years ago. So the y axis is an effective size, a summary of how related the two genome copies were, not a count of animals. A change in it can mean a change in numbers, in connectivity between populations, or both (s01).

Reading a PSMC curve

  • The middle is the most reliable part. For their human data Li & Durbin wrote that "more recently than 20 kya or more anciently than 3 Mya few recombination events are left in the present sequence", and the estimate there "is not as accurate and has large variance". Those numbers are for humans at μ = 2.5 × 10-8 and 25 years per generation; for your species the limits move with N0, μ and g. In our species A genome (below) the youngest intervals did not survive the checks; as a rule of thumb for mammals, do not interpret the last 10 to 20 thousand years unless several independent checks agree.
  • Sharp events come out smooth. A sudden drop appears as a slope starting earlier than the real event, and a short bottleneck as a shallow dip or not at all. Read the timing of a change to within its neighbouring intervals, not to a year.
  • A hump is not necessarily growth. Rising IICR can mean more animals or less connected subpopulations (Mazet et al. 2016). A sharp recent peak followed by a collapse can be an artefact of the first parameter group (s06).
  • Compare like with like. Two curves on one figure need the same μ, g, bin size, pattern and comparable call filters; otherwise their difference may come from the settings.

04From model units to years: mutation rate and generation time

PSMC never sees a year or an animal. It reads where the heterozygous sites lie along one genome and reports times and population sizes in its own units, measured against an unknown reference size. To turn them into years and numbers of individuals you supply two numbers from outside the genome: the mutation rate and the generation time. This section shows the arithmetic, where those two numbers come from, what happens to the curve when they change, and how to report the uncertainty they bring.

What PSMC reports

The .psmc output file (s03) holds, for every iteration, a TR line and a block of RS lines. The PSMC README says: "All these parameters are scaled to 2N0". The three numbers you need are:

θ0 (theta zero)
The second column of the TR line: the scaled mutation rate per bin. In population-genetics units θ = 4Nμ: four times the population size times the mutation rate. It is, roughly, how often two copies of the genome differ per base, which is why it carries the heterozygosity of the animal.
tk
Third column of each RS line: the start of time interval k, in units of 2N0 generations (0.5 means "N0 generations ago").
λk (lambda)
Fourth column of each RS line: the population size in interval k relative to N0 (λ = 2 means twice N0).
N0
A reference effective population size that is never known by itself. Every time and every size in the output is a multiple of it.

Li and Durbin (2011) and the PSMC README convert with a per-site, per-generation mutation rate μ and the bin size s (100 by default):

N0 = θ0 / (4 μ s)
generations ago Tk = 2 N0 tk years ago = Tk × g
Ne in interval k = λk N0

Here g is the generation time in years: the average age of the parents when their offspring are born. Notice what the formulas imply. θ0, tk and λk come from the genome and are fixed. μ sits in the denominator of N0, and N0 multiplies both times and sizes; g multiplies only the times.

Simple explanation with examples: from θ0 to a number of mice

Take a PSMC run like the one in s03, with a round example value of θ0 = 0.08 per 100-base bin. Assume μ = 5.4 × 10-9 per site per generation (an illustrative value, see below) and g = 1 year.

N0 = 0.08 / (4 × 5.4e-9 × 100) = 0.08 / 2.16e-6 = 37,037 an interval starting at t = 0.5: T = 2 × 37,037 × 0.5 = 37,037 generations = 37,037 years at g = 1 the same interval with λ = 1.5: Ne = 1.5 × 37,037 = 55,600 double μ to 1.08e-8: N0 = 18,519 → T = 18,519 years, Ne = 27,800 (both halved) double g to 2 years: N0 = 37,037 → T = 74,074 years, Ne = 55,600 (only time doubles)

The interval and the λ did not change. Only the ruler did. The genome itself fixes products such as 2N0μt; you can trade a larger population for a smaller mutation rate and the data look identical.

The README also gives a way to plot without μ at all: time as the pairwise divergence dk = 2μTk = tkθ0/s, and size as θk = 4Nkμ = λkθ0/s. Such a plot never has to be redrawn when somebody publishes a better mutation rate.

Where the mutation rate comes from

There are two kinds of estimates, and they answer slightly different questions.

  • Direct (pedigree) rates. Sequence parents and offspring (a trio) or lines kept for many generations (a mutation-accumulation experiment), and count new mutations that neither parent carried. The result is per site per generation: exactly what PSMC needs. It is a modern, short-term rate, measured in a few individuals, often of a laboratory strain.
  • Phylogenetic (long-term) rates. Divide the sequence divergence between two species by twice their split time, which itself comes from fossils or a dated tree (the factor 2 because both lineages accumulate changes: d = 2μT, as in the PSMC README). The result is per site per year, averaged over millions of years, and only as good as the calibration date.

The two can disagree. When the first trio studies gave a human rate of about 1.2 × 10-8 per generation (Kong et al. 2012), it was "approximately half" of the older value from fossil calibration (Scally and Durbin 2012). Halving μ doubles every date scaled with it, so the timing of human prehistory inferred from genomes had to be revised. Li and Durbin (2011) had used 2.5 × 10-8 per generation and 25 years; they noted the "uncertainty of the per-year mutation rate 1.0 × 10-9 (= 2.5 × 10-8/25)" as an important caveat.

For a wild species without its own estimate you borrow a rate from a relative, and the 40-fold spread across vertebrates is a reminder that the borrowed number is a guess. Bergeron et al. (2023) also found that generation time, age at maturity and fecundity are the main life-history traits behind that spread, so a rate measured in a fast-breeding species may not transfer to a slow one.

One more thing acts like a lower mutation rate: heterozygous sites you failed to call. Li and Durbin (2011) state that "missing heterozygotes uniformly at a probability p is equivalent to reducing the neutral mutation rate". Low coverage or strict filters (s02) therefore push the curve the same way a smaller μ would. psmc_plot.pl has -N (one false-negative rate) and -M "sample=0.1" (per sample) to correct for it, and the README calls an uncorrected low coverage "the most common pitfall when using PSMC".

Generation time

For a wild mammal the generation time is rarely measured. It is set from life-history data (age at first breeding, lifespan, how many years an animal breeds) and is longer than the age at first breeding whenever animals breed for more than one season. Two rules follow from the formulas:

  • With a per-generation μ, g moves only the time axis: years = generations × g. Population sizes do not depend on it.
  • With a per-year rate, convert first: μgen = μyr × g. Then the years no longer depend on g (it cancels), but the population sizes do: a longer generation time gives a smaller Ne.
per generation: years = 2 N0 t g = θ0 t g / (2 μ_gen s) Ne = λ θ0 / (4 μ_gen s)
per year: years = θ0 t / (2 μ_yr s) Ne = λ θ0 / (4 μ_yr g s)

Reporting the uncertainty

The bootstrap band from s03 shows how much the curve changes when you resample pieces of the genome. It says nothing about μ and g, which multiply every value at once. For a species without its own rate this second uncertainty is usually the larger one. Report it separately:

  1. State μ, its unit (per generation or per year), and where it comes from; state g and its basis. Say plainly if either is borrowed or a placeholder.
  2. Give the rescaling rule so readers can convert: "for another μ multiply all times and sizes by 5.4 × 10-9/μ; for another g multiply times by g".
  3. Show a small grid of scalings (as in the figure above), or a second axis in model units (divergence d and θ), next to the main figure.
  4. Make claims that do not depend on the constants: the order of events, ratios ("the trough is about 2.5 times below the old plateau"), and the comparison of two genomes that share the same μ and g. Absolute dates matched to a climate event need the constants and inherit their uncertainty.
  5. Never overlay curves from different papers without re-scaling them to the same μ and g.

It needs gnuplot; -R keeps the text tables (prefix.0.txt for the main run, one per bootstrap) whose first two columns are years and Ne in units of 104, which you can plot with any other program.

In our projects

All our species A curves were scaled with μ = 5.4 × 10-9 per site per generation and g = 1 year, and every figure says these are illustrative placeholders, not values verified for this species. The μ is the same number as the C57BL/6 laboratory-mouse estimate of Uchimura et al. (2015); no rate has been measured for species A or its close relatives, and we have no measured generation time. Re-scaling to the long-read mouse rate of 7.86 × 10-9 divides every time and size by 1.46. Our recommended display is a grid of μ 4.6 to 7.9 × 10-9 per generation and g 1 to 2 years.

The constants matter most for dates. Suppose a split time scaled with the placeholder constants comes out at 1 million years, while an independent dated phylogeny puts the same split at 2 million years. Both can be true only if the yearly rate is half the placeholder, 2.7 × 10-9: for example a per-generation rate of 5.4 × 10-9 with a generation time of 2 years. Whether such a generation time is realistic has to come from the species' life history; the arithmetic alone cannot decide it. Our own two-species estimates (s07) carry exactly this uncertainty.

05Beyond PSMC: MSMC2, SMC++, cobraa and SFS methods

PSMC (s03) reads one diploid genome and returns one curve. Other methods read the same genome differently, or need more genomes: MSMC2 compares many haplotypes and two populations, SMC++ uses many unphased genomes, cobraa asks whether the curve is really a size change or old population structure, and site-frequency-spectrum methods summarise a whole sample of animals. This section says what each one needs, what it answers, and why running three of them on one genome is not three independent confirmations.

New words for this section

Haplotype
One of the two copies of a chromosome in a diploid animal. One genome = two haplotypes; four animals = eight.
Phasing
Knowing which allele of every heterozygous site sits on which copy, along the whole chromosome. A normal VCF genotype 0/1 is unphased; 0|1 is phased. Phase comes from trios (parents sequenced), from long reads spanning several heterozygous sites, or statistically from a reference panel. A switch error is a place where the phase jumps from one copy to the other by mistake.
Coalescence rate
How fast two lineages, traced back in time, meet in a common ancestor. In one panmictic population the rate is 1/(2Ne) per generation, so the inverse of the rate reads as a population size. Cross-coalescence is the same rate for one haplotype from population A and one from population B.
Site frequency spectrum (SFS)
A table of how many SNPs are carried by 1, 2, 3, … of the sampled chromosomes. It needs a sample of individuals; it ignores where the SNPs are along the genome. Folded SFS counts the rarer allele when the ancestral allele is unknown.
Composite likelihood
A likelihood made by multiplying pieces that are not truly independent (for example, the same chromosome analysed with different animals as the focus). It works for point estimates, but its raw value overstates the evidence.
Simple explanation with examples: why more genomes see more recent time

The two copies of a chromosome in one animal usually meet in a common ancestor tens of thousands of generations back. Recent events (the last few thousand generations) are rarely "seen" because few pieces of the genome have such a young common ancestor. Take eight copies from four animals instead: among the 28 possible pairs, the first pair to meet does so much more recently. The original MSMC models exactly this first meeting, so its curve reaches closer to the present (Schiffels & Durbin 2014); MSMC2 instead combines the coalescence rates of all the pairs, which also gains recent resolution.

2 copies (1 animal) pairs: 1 first meeting: typically old 8 copies (4 animals) pairs: 28 first meeting: much more recent 50 copies (25 animals) SFS classes: 1..25 (folded) → rare alleles = recent history

The SFS works the same way: a SNP carried by only one of fifty chromosomes is a young mutation, one carried by half of them is old. With one animal the folded spectrum has one class: "heterozygous". That single number is the heterozygosity, and it holds no time information at all.

MSMC2: many haplotypes, and two populations

MSMC2 (Schiffels & Wang 2020) is the current version of MSMC (Schiffels & Durbin 2014). It estimates the coalescence rate between pairs of haplotypes and combines all pairs you select with -I. Three uses:

  • One diploid. Two haplotypes, one pair: the model is then essentially PSMC (the MSMC2 tutorial calls this "PSMC mode"). It is a second implementation, with its own time intervals, of the same data, not a second data set.
  • Several phased diploids of one population. All pairs of haplotypes (-I 0,1,2,3). More haplotypes give more resolution in recent time. The author does not think it "feasible to go beyond 16 haplotypes", and warns that with limited phasing "you will get problems in very recent times". Unphased animals can still be run, pairing only the two haplotypes inside each animal (-I 0-1,2-3).
  • Two populations. Three runs: within population 1, within population 2, and across (only pairs with one haplotype from each, plus -s to skip sites with ambiguous phase). combineCrossCoal.py merges them, and the relative cross-coalescence rate follows.
RCCR(t) = 2 λ01(t) / (λ00(t) + λ11(t)) ≈ 1: one population → 0: fully separated

λ00 and λ11 are the coalescence rates within each population, λ01 the rate across. The tutorial warns that even a sharp split comes out as a gradual "smear" around the true time, so the midpoint (0.5) is a summary, not the split date. MSMC-IM (Wang et al. 2020) fits an isolation-with-migration model (two populations that split and then keep exchanging migrants; s07) to these three rates to estimate gene flow over time.

The third column of a multihetsep line is "the number of called sites since the previous segregating site, including the current site" (tutorial). It carries the mask: without it MSMC2 would read every gap as a long homozygous stretch. Output columns are time_index left_time_boundary right_time_boundary lambda; years = left_time_boundary / μ × g, and Ne = (1/λ) / (2μ) (tutorial; scaling in s04). The tutorial reduces the pattern to -p 1*2+15*1+1*2 for a single chromosome "to avoid overfitting": fewer free intervals for less data.

SMC++: many unphased genomes

SMC++ (Terhorst, Kamm & Song 2017) follows one distinguished animal along the genome, exactly like PSMC, and adds the allele counts of all other animals at each site (the "undistinguished" sample) as extra frequency information. It needs no phasing, scales to hundreds of genomes, and by default fits a piecewise-constant curve (--spline piecewise, 8 knots in the source); --spline pchip or cubic gives a smooth curve, as in the paper. With one animal it is a single-genome method with a different model and regularisation from PSMC. Running vcf2smc once per animal with -d makes each animal the distinguished one in turn; estimate then combines them as a composite likelihood (the README suggests 2 to 10 distinguished animals).

# SMC++ 1.15 (Docker image terhorst/smcpp: a packaged, ready-to-run copy of the program and everything it needs). The VCF must be bgzipped and tabix-indexed (bgzip -c x.vcf > x.vcf.gz; tabix -p vcf x.vcf.gz).
# Mask = regions that are NOT callable; without it gaps are read as homozygous.

From the README: vcf2smc ignores FILTER and QUAL (filter the VCF first), ignores indels, takes the reference allele as ancestral, and treats sites absent from the VCF as homozygous. The FAQ answer to "SMC++ claims that my population crashed in the very recent past" is long runs of homozygosity: a real recent crash, selective sweeps, or uncalled regions not masked. --missing-cutoff (-c) recodes long homozygous runs as missing, "a fairly crude approach" for when a mask is impossible. split fits a clean split: no gene flow after it.

cobraa: size change, or old structure?

A PSMC curve is the inverse instantaneous coalescence rate (IICR) of the two haplotypes. Mazet et al. (2016) showed that it equals a population size only in a panmictic model, and that any structured history has a panmictic size history with exactly the same pairwise coalescence times. From the PSMC curve alone the two cannot be told apart. The toy below shows this.

cobraa (Cousins, Scally & Durbin 2025) extends PSMC with an explicit structured model: going forward in time, an ancestral population splits into A and B at time T2, and they rejoin at T1, the present lineage taking a fraction γ of its ancestry from B. The authors show that the PSMC transition matrix (how the coalescence time changes between neighbouring positions) "has information that can distinguish a structured model from a panmictic model, even if they have matching coalescence rate profiles". They compare the best structured fit with the best panmictic fit as ΔL = LS − LU, scanning pairs of T1, T2, and found ΔL near 0 for panmictic simulations. The README: "We recommend comparing the fit of the model to a panmictic model as in PSMC, to see if admixture is well supported."

# cobraa (GitHub, commit ab85ea7). Input: the same multihetsep files as MSMC2; -in takes several files
# panmictic fit (no -ts/-te: the PSMC model)
python cobraa.py -in ctg*.mhs -D 32 -b 100 -its 30 -spread_1 0.05 -spread_2 50 -o pan_ | tee pan.log
# structured fit: admixture at time index 10, split at index 18 (indices into the D intervals)
python cobraa.py -in ctg*.mhs -D 32 -b 100 -its 30 -spread_1 0.05 -spread_2 50 \
    -ts 10 -te 18 -gamma_fg 0.2 -o st_10_18_ | tee st_10_18.log
# "log likelihood for this iteration = ..." is printed every iteration; ΔL = last structured - last panmictic

Give -D 32 explicitly: the README calls 32 the default, but in the code the default is 30. Output <prefix>final_parameters.txt: time boundaries and coalescence rates of A and B; years = time / μ × g, Ne = (1/λA) / μ (README). If heterozygosity is very high (θ, roughly the share of heterozygous bases, > 0.01) reduce the bin size -b so that bins with more than about 3 heterozygous sites are rare (README). Check that the log-likelihoods are finite numbers.

A better likelihood is not yet evidence. The paper's simulations used constant mutation and recombination rates along the genome; the authors call this assumption false and cite earlier work in which realistic rate variation had "only negligible consequences for SMC-based inference". Real genomes do have regions of higher and lower diversity, and the structured model has 4 more parameters than the panmictic one (37 vs 33 in the paper). In our two rodent genomes (below), structureless simulations with the observed variation of heterozygosity along the genome gained as much log-likelihood from the structured model as the real data did. Simulate your own null, with your genome's variation, before reading ΔL.

SFS methods: a sample of animals, no positions

Methods based on the SFS need several individuals per population: one animal gives a folded spectrum with a single class. They do not need phasing, and SNPs from reduced-representation sequencing can work (Liu & Fu 2020). Their weakness is the opposite of PSMC's: many animals, but no information from linkage along the genome.

  • Stairway Plot 2 (Liu & Fu 2015, 2020): fits a size history without a predefined model, from a folded or unfolded SFS. A blueprint file holds everything: nseq (number of sequences, 2 × diploids), L (all callable sites, including monomorphic ones, where all sampled sequences carry the same base), whether_folded, the SFS itself, mu and year_per_generation. It uses two thirds of the sites to fit (pct_training: 0.67) and the rest to choose the number of change points, repeats this ninput: 200 times, and reports the median with 75 % and 95 % pseudo-confidence intervals.
  • fastsimcoal2 (Excoffier et al. 2013; fsc28, 2023): you write the model (template .tpl with parameter names, search ranges in .est), give the observed (joint) SFS, and it finds the parameters by composite likelihood, computing expected spectra by simulation. Any model can be written: splits, migration matrices, bottlenecks, admixture, unsampled ("ghost") populations. The manual's batch script runs 50 independent estimations per data set; keep the best.

06Pitfalls and checks

A PSMC curve is the last link of a long chain: reads, variant calls, a callable mask, bins, a model with a time pattern, and finally a mutation rate and a generation time. Every link can bend the curve, and most of them bend it without changing the bootstrap band. This section lists what bends single-genome curves, shows the main effects on a toy genome you can spoil yourself, and gives the checks that tell a real feature from an artefact, with what they showed for our two rodent genomes.

Words used in this section

False negative (missed het)
A true heterozygous site that the pipeline calls homozygous, usually because too few reads carried the second allele, or because the site was filtered out. The share of true hets missed is the false-negative rate (FNR).
False positive (false het)
A site called heterozygous where the two genome copies are in fact the same: a sequencing error shared by many reads, a misaligned read from a similar copy elsewhere (a paralog), or an assembly error.
Artefact
A bump, dip or slope in the curve that comes from the data or the method, not from the history of the animals.
Sensitivity test
Running the same analysis again with one thing changed (fewer reads, another caller, a mask, another time pattern) to see which parts of the curve move. A feature that survives the reasonable alternatives is called robust.
Simple explanation with examples: why each error moves the curve in its own direction

PSMC reads the genome as a mosaic of pieces and dates each piece by how many heterozygous sites it carries (s01, s03): few hets = the two copies met their common ancestor recently; many hets = long ago. The curve is then built from how many pieces fall into each age class. So any error that changes the het count of a piece changes its apparent age.

Missing hets everywhere makes every piece look younger, so the whole curve slides toward the present and down; the PSMC README says this "has the same effect as smaller mutation rate". False hets sprinkled evenly matter most for the pieces that had few hets to begin with: the recent coalescences vanish from the record, and the recent population looks larger than it was. A collapsed duplication adds pieces that look extremely old. A run of homozygosity adds pieces whose ancestor lived a few generations ago.

Spoil a toy genome and watch the curve bend

A toy genome of 2 Gb in 20,000 blocks of 100 kb. Each block gets one common-ancestor age (TMRCA) drawn from the true history (dashed line), and true hets in proportion to that age, at μ = 5.4 × 10-9 per base per generation. The estimate (solid blue line) dates each block from its observed het count, then turns the share of blocks in each age class into a coalescence rate and a size, as PSMC does in principle. It is not PSMC: real blocks are much shorter than 100 kb and PSMC finds them with an HMM; the grey line is what the toy estimator recovers from clean data. Spoil the data with the sliders.

What to notice. (1) False hets at only 0.05 per kb (5 per block, under 3 % of all hets) already lift the most recent part of the curve: the young blocks are the ones with few hets, and they no longer look young. (2) Missing 30 % of the hets moves the whole curve down and to the left, the same as a lower mutation rate; the shape survives. (3) ROH add blocks with no hets; in this estimator they only fill the youngest age class, and masking them changes the rest very little. Now add false hets as well: the "empty" ROH blocks get a few hets each, look a few thousand generations old, and a false recent decline appears; masking the called ROH removes that decline. Raise the false hets to 0.2 per kb or more and the ROH caller (under 0.2 het per kb over 300 kb) starts to miss the runs. (4) Collapsed duplications (here 6 false hets per kb in each duplicated block) lift the older part of the curve. (5) "New genome" redraws the history's random outcome: the recent end changes from genome to genome, the old part hardly at all.

  1. Subsample the coverage

Simple explanation with examples: what "simulate and re-infer" proves

Suppose your curve shows a sharp peak 30,000 years ago and a crash after it. Simulate genomes under a history without the peak, only with the crash, and run PSMC on them. If the peak comes back, PSMC makes it out of the crash, and your peak is not evidence of anything (this is how Hilgers et al. 2025 showed the default-pattern peaks are artefacts). If the peak does not come back, the data really demand it, under the model. Then try a different story that could produce the same data, such as a population split into connected groups at constant size: if that also reproduces your curve, one genome cannot tell the two stories apart.

In our projects

Species A (our ONT genome, one male) and the relative (public HiFi assembly, somewhat lower depth), both scaled with the placeholder μ = 5.4 × 10-9 per generation and one year per generation.

Patterns. Splitting the first window left the species A curve unchanged except in the youngest intervals, including its recent low point and the bump before it; only the youngest interval ran away to values in the millions, i.e. it is not constrained. In the relative the default and split patterns agreed in shape beyond the youngest intervals (the peak height differed by about 15 %) and disagreed in the youngest ones. Simulated nulls. The same idea, simulating a history without the feature, showed that cobraa's preference for a structured model on both genomes is matched by simulations without any structure (s05). Bootstraps: 20 rounds per curve.

07Two species: divergence time and gene flow

With one genome from each of two sister species you can ask two questions the single-genome curves of the earlier sections cannot answer: when did the two species stop exchanging genes, and did they exchange any afterwards? The idea is simple. A copy of a chromosome from species X and a copy from species Y can only trace back to a common ancestor before the species split, unless genes crossed between them later. How far back their shared ancestry lies, along the whole genome, holds the split time and the traces of gene flow. This section shows four ways to read it (hPSMC, MSMC2 cross-coalescence, BPP and gIMble), what each needs and assumes, and how far apart their answers were for our two rodent genomes.

The words we need

Coalescence
Following two copies of a stretch of DNA back in time until they meet in one ancestral copy. The time back to that meeting is the coalescence time. Inside one population it is, on average, 2Ne generations for a diploid population of effective size Ne (see s01 and s03).
Split time (divergence time)
The time when the ancestral population divided into the two species and, in the simplest model, gene exchange stopped. It is younger than the average coalescence time of the two species' DNA, because after the split the two copies still have to wait in the ancestral population before they meet.
Gene flow (migration)
Genes moving from one species into the other after the split, by hybridisation and backcrossing. Models describe it either as a steady trickle (isolation with migration, IM) or as a single pulse (introgression). The rate m is the share of a population's genes that come from the other species each generation; N·m is the number of immigrant genomes per generation.
Pseudo-diploid (hybrid) genome
An artificial "F1 hybrid" made in the computer by pairing one haploid copy of species X with one haploid copy of species Y, position by position. Every difference between the two copies is treated as a heterozygous site.
Simple explanation with examples: two villages that stopped marrying

Imagine one village that split into two over a river 1,000 years ago, after which nobody crossed. Pick one person from each side and trace their family trees back: their nearest shared ancestor must have lived before the split, so more than 1,000 years ago, and in the old village it took some extra time on top of that, depending on how big the old village was. Do this for many independent pieces of the genome and you get a whole distribution of meeting times, none of them younger than 1,000 years.

Now suppose that a few people still crossed the river each generation. Some pieces of the two genomes then share an ancestor who lived after the split, on whichever side. Those young meetings blur the sharp edge at 1,000 years: the edge moves toward the present and becomes a slope. The more crossers, the more blurred; with one or more crossers per generation, the two villages look like one (Cahill et al. 2016 found no detectable split in that case).

species X copy ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGGATCCAGCTACAGG species Y copy ACGTTAGCGTGACCTTAGACGCTAGGATCCAGTTACAGG ^ ^ ^ differences = "heterozygous" sites of the hybrid

A real F1 hybrid would carry exactly this. hPSMC builds it from two genomes, then lets PSMC read the meeting times from the density of differences, as in s03.

Bin size: the 100-bp default fails between species

A PSMC bin is "K" if it holds at least one difference. Within one genome at 0.2 % heterozygosity, about one 100-bp bin in five is K, and the model can see where long stretches of low or high density begin and end. Between two species the two copies differ at, say, 2 % of sites; then almost every 100-bp bin holds a difference, the sequence becomes "KKKKKKK…", and PSMC has nothing left to read. If differences fall at random, the share of K bins is about

share of K bins ≈ 1 − e−d·b d = differences per base, b = bin size

At d = 2 %: 86 % for 100-bp bins, 33 % for 20-bp bins, 18 % for 10-bp bins. Smaller bins keep the information, at the cost of a five to ten times longer input and run. The toy below prints this for the divergence it produces.

Reading the curve: simulations, not the eye

Going back in time, the hPSMC curve sits at infinity (no shared ancestry is possible) until the split, then falls to the ancestral population size. A real PSMC curve does not jump: PSMC works with broad time intervals and cannot place each change in ancestry exactly, so even a clean split gives a slope spread over a wide time range. The hPSMC authors therefore read the curve against simulated ones (README, step 5):

  1. Read the pre-divergence Ne from the plateau on the old side.
  2. Simulate pseudo-diploids with that Ne and a series of split times, run the same PSMC on them.
  3. Find the simulated split times whose curves cross yours between 1.5 and 10 times the pre-divergence Ne. The estimate is the narrowest range of non-crossing simulations around them. The paper calls these thresholds "a reasonable starting point for interpretation, but may not be appropriate for all datasets."

Check the hard-coded simulation settings. hPSMC_quantify_split_time.py writes ms commands with values fixed in the script, chosen for humans: generation time 25 years, μ = 1 × 10−9 per site per year, recombination 10−8 per site per generation, 40 replicates of 5 Mb, 10-bp bins, and an instant split with no gene flow (-I 2 2 2 -ej). Edit them for your species, or write the simulation yourself (msprime), matching μ, bin size, recombination and the PSMC -p pattern of your real run. If gene flow continued after the split, the matching split time is the end of gene flow, not the first separation.

A split, with and without gene flow, as hPSMC sees it

Two species split from one ancestral population. Take one copy of a chromosome piece from each species and follow them back in time. Before the split (older times) they wait in the ancestral population; after it (younger times) they can meet only if one of them has moved into the other species by gene flow, and then they wait in that species. The figure computes the exact distribution of their meeting time for this model, and the curve PSMC would aim at: the IICR. The thick steps average it over 24 broad time windows, as a piecewise-constant model does; real PSMC from finite data is smoother still. Times are in years at the placeholder rate μ = 5.4 × 10−9 per generation and 1 year per generation (as in s04).

When gene flow happens

What to notice. (1) Without gene flow no copy pair meets after the split: the IICR is infinite there and drops to the ancestral size exactly at the split; even then the stepped curve shows the change only to the nearest window. (2) Add a little gene flow (N·m of 0.01 to 0.1, one immigrant every 100 to 10 generations): 14 to 67 % of the genome now meets after the split (default sliders), and the curve comes down from infinity long after the split, toward the present. Its crossing of 10 × and 1.5 × Ne moves toward the present: read naively, the split looks younger, and what is dated is closer to the end of gene flow. (3) At N·m of 1 or more the two species behave almost like one population and the rise nearly disappears (compare Cahill et al. 2016). (4) "First half, then stopped": the rise sits near the end of gene flow, not at the split. (5) The divergence d and the share of K bins in the readout: with T = 1 My and an ancestral Ne of 300k the two copies differ at about 1.7 % of sites, and a 100-bp bin is K most of the time.

Toy rules (what the figure computes)

Two lineages, one in each daughter species, both of size Nd. Going back in time, each lineage moves to the other species at rate m = (N·m)/Nd per generation while gene flow is on (symmetric). When both are in the same species they join at rate 1/(2Nd); a lineage can also move out again. Older than the split, both are in the ancestral population and join at rate 1/(2NA). The probability that they have not joined by time t is computed exactly (a three-state Markov chain), on a fine logarithmic time grid. IICR(t) = 1 / (2 × rate of joining at t among pairs not yet joined). The stepped curve uses 24 windows equally spaced on a log scale from 12 ky to 12 My, with the average rate in each window. Divergence d = 2μ × mean joining time (in generations); K share = 1 − e−d·b. Not modelled: recombination, mutation noise, finite genome length, the PSMC fit itself, and unequal gene flow in the two directions.

2. MSMC2: cross-coalescence rate

MSMC2 estimates coalescence rates for pairs of haplotypes you choose with -I. For two species you run it three times, on the same input files, and combine the results (MSMC2 README; msmc-tools tutorial):

msmc2 -I 0,1,2,3 -o withinA chr*.multihetsep.txt
msmc2 -I 4,5,6,7 -o withinB chr*.multihetsep.txt
msmc2 -s -I 0-4,0-5,0-6,0-7,1-4,1-5,1-6,1-7,2-4,2-5,2-6,2-7,3-4,3-5,3-6,3-7 -o across chr*.multihetsep.txt
combineCrossCoal.py across.final.txt withinA.final.txt withinB.final.txt > AB.combined.final.txt
# relative cross-coalescence rate per time interval (columns of the combined file):
#   rCCR = 2 * lambda_01 / (lambda_00 + lambda_11);  years = left_time_boundary / mu * g

The relative cross-coalescence rate (rCCR) is about 1 while the two samples behave as one population and falls to 0 once they are fully separated; the time at which it passes 0.5 is often quoted as a split time. The tutorial warns that "even in simulations with a sharp separation, MSMC would not produce an infinitely sharp separation curve, but introduces a 'smear' around the true separation time". -s skips sites whose phase is ambiguous, "recommended for cross population analysis". MSMC2 options as listed in the README of v2.1.4: -i iterations (default 20), -p time pattern (default 1*2+25*1+1*2+1*3), -t threads.

With one unphased genome per species the cross pairs cannot be built: you do not know which allele of A sits on the same haplotype as which allele of B. Running MSMC2 on one haploid of each species (two haplotypes in total) is the same pseudo-diploid as hPSMC, and the result is the same kind of curve.

3. BPP: many short loci, a Bayesian model

BPP does not walk along the genome. It takes thousands of short, separate alignments (loci), each with its own gene tree, and asks which species tree parameters explain them all: the split time τ and the population sizes θ, in units of mutations per site. It is a Bayesian MCMC program, so you give priors and get posteriors. The manual names four analyses by two switches; "A00" (speciesdelimitation = 0, speciestree = 0) estimates θ and τ on a fixed tree, which is what a two-species split needs.

θ = 4Nμ (μ per generation) τ = T × μ (T and μ in the same time unit) years = τ / μper year

One diploid per species is enough: unphased diploid sequences are written with IUPAC codes at heterozygous sites and declared unphased with phase = 1 for that species; BPP then sums over the possible phases (manual: "phase"). Tiley et al. 2023 showed by simulation that divergence times and ancestral sizes can be estimated accurately "even when only a single diploid individual per species is sampled", provided gene flow is modelled when present.

The manual's examples use inverse-gamma priors with shape a = 3, a broad distribution; set the mean from rough numbers: θ about the heterozygosity per site; τ somewhat less than half the between-species divergence per site (the two copies differ by about 2τ plus the ancestral θ; our rule of thumb from θ = 4Nμ). Rerun with a prior mean several times larger and smaller: if the posterior follows the prior, the data do not inform that parameter.
  • Migration in 4.8. Version 4.8.0 changed the migration parameter from M (migrants per generation) to w = m/μ; old control files with migprior must be updated, and the manual gives M = θY·w/4 for converting.
  • Convergence. Run several chains with different seeds and compare them; look at the effective sample size of every parameter (column ESS* of BPP's summary table, or Tracer), and run longer if it is low.
  • Install. Use the binary release from GitHub (the README links bpp-4.8.7-linux-x86_64.tar.gz); in our setup the conda package of that name was not the program.

A migration rate above zero is not yet evidence for gene flow. Model misfit of other kinds (variation in mutation rate or in Ne along the genome, linked selection, wrong loci) can push it up. Check that it survives different locus sets and prior choices, and simulate data without gene flow under the fitted MSC model and run them through the same analysis: if those also give w > 0, the signal is not informative (the same lesson as the cobraa null in s05).

4. gIMble: blocks and isolation with migration

gIMble was written for the case of one diploid genome per species. It cuts the genome of each between-species sample pair into short blocks of a fixed number of callable bases (64 by default; the paper chose it so that a block holds on average about one heterozygous site per species) and counts, in each block, four kinds of variable sites:

hetA, hetB
Heterozygous in one species only.
hetAB
Heterozygous in both (a polymorphism the species share).
fixed
Homozygous in both, but for different bases: a fixed difference.

The tally of block types (the blockwise site frequency spectrum, bSFS) is compared with its exact probability under three models: strict divergence (DIV), migration only (MIG) and isolation with migration (IM) with gene flow in one stated direction. It then estimates the population sizes, the split time T (in generations) and the migration rate me. The same tallies in windows along the genome give the barrier scan: windows where gene flow is much lower than the genome average (Laetsch et al. 2023).

# gIMble 1.0.3 (README). Input from gimbleprep: VCF, BED of callable sites, genome file, samples CSV (add 2 population IDs by hand)
gimbleprep -f ref.fa -b bams/ -v raw.freebayes.vcf.gz -k
gimble parse -v gimble.vcf.gz -b gimble.intergenic.bed -g gimble.genomefile -s gimble.samples.csv -z analysis
gimble blocks -z analysis.z -l 64
gimble windows -z analysis.z -w 500 -s 100
gimble tally -z analysis.z -k 2,2,2,2 -l blocks_kmax2 -t blocks
# fit IM with migration B->A backwards in time; bounds for Ne (-A -B -C), T (generations), me; -u = mu per generation
gimble optimize -z analysis.z -l IM_BA_opt -d tally/blocks_kmax2 -m IM_BA -r A -u 5.4e-09 \
  -A 10_000,1_000_000 -B 10_000,1_000_000 -C 10_000,2_000_000 -T 0,5_000_000 -M 0,1e-5

The README designs gimbleprep around a FreeBayes VCF and BAMs; the -A/-B/-C/-T/-M bounds above are placeholders to adapt, not values from the documentation. Migration direction in the model names is backwards in time (IM_BA: lineages move from B to A going back, i.e. genes flowed from A into B forward in time; check the help of gimble optimize). The paper builds confidence intervals and false-positive rates for barrier windows from coalescent simulations (gimble simulate), because the composite likelihood alone overstates certainty.

What one genome per species cannot do

  • D-statistics (ABBA-BABA) and relatives test introgression by comparing two sister lineages with a third lineage and an outgroup (Hibbins & Hahn 2022). Between a single pair of sister species there is no third lineage to compare, so these tests do not apply to "did A and B exchange genes?".
  • Direction and timing of gene flow are weakly identified with two diploids: the four methods above assume a model (one pulse, constant trickle, one direction) and estimate within it.
  • Ancestral structure can mimic gene flow and vice versa, as for single-genome curves (s01, s06).

08Reporting a demographic analysis

A PSMC curve is only as good as the choices behind it: which reads, which calls, which mask, which time pattern, which mutation rate and generation time. A reader cannot judge the curve, or redraw it with a better mutation rate, unless every one of those choices is written down. This section lists what a methods paragraph must say, how to draw the figure, which sentences a single genome cannot support, and which files to share. The tool at the end builds a methods paragraph from your choices and marks what is missing.

Simple explanation with examples: why a reader needs all of this

Think of the curve as a reading on an instrument that has several dials. A paper that prints the reading without the dial positions cannot be checked or compared. Three common cases:

Paper A: "PSMC (default settings), μ = 2.5e-8, g = 25" ^ which pattern? ^ these are psmc_plot.pl's human values; source for a vole? Paper B: "Illumina 12x, bcftools, PASS" ^ below ~18x: heterozygous sites lost, Ne too low (Nadachowska-Brzyska 2016) Paper C: "100 bootstraps confirm the bottleneck at 20 kya" ^ bootstraps resample the genome; they do not test the pattern, the calls or μ

With the dial positions written down, a reader can redo the scaling with a better μ in two minutes, or see that a peak sits exactly in the window the default pattern is known to distort.

2. Drawing the figure

  • Log time axis. PSMC's time windows are spaced on a logarithmic scale (the source sets them as α(eβk − 1)), so a linear axis squeezes everything except the oldest windows into the left edge. A log axis for Ne is usual too.
  • Units in the axis labels, μ and g in the caption. "Years ago (μ = 5.4×10⁻⁹ per site per generation, g = 1 year)". Or draw the curve in scaled units, which need neither: time as pairwise divergence d = t × θ₀ / s and size as θ = λ × θ₀ / s (PSMC README, appendix I; θ₀ is the scaled mutation rate PSMC estimates, s the bin size). A reader then multiplies by their own μ.
  • Bootstraps as thin lines behind the main curve, so the reader sees where they spread.
  • Several μ values (or a stated range) when μ is uncertain. On log–log axes a different μ slides the whole curve along a diagonal, without changing its shape.
  • Mark the time range you do not interpret, rather than cutting it off silently or letting the most recent window run up the y axis.
  • A title inside the image and a colour legend above the plot. Figures get copied into slides and reviews without their captions. Keep one colour per species in every figure of the paper.

3. What a single-genome curve cannot support

The curve is a smoothed estimate of how fast the two copies of the genome found common ancestors at each time in the past, its IICR. It equals the effective population size (Ne) only if the population was one randomly mating unit; subdivided populations of constant size can produce curves that look like growth or decline (Mazet et al. 2016; Chikhi et al. 2018). Write accordingly.

Do not writeWhy notWrite instead
"The population declined 18,500 years ago."Times come from discrete windows and scale with 1/μ and with g, and both are uncertain for most species (s04)."A decline between about 15 and 25 thousand years ago at μ = …, g = …; between … and … over the μ range shown."
"About 10,000 animals lived then."Ne is not a census, and under population structure even Ne is not what the curve shows."Ne (read from the inverse coalescence rate, assuming one panmictic population) fell to about 10,000."
"The Last Glacial Maximum caused the decline."A coincidence in time, on a time axis that moves whenever μ or g change. Structure changes can produce the same shape."The decline overlaps the Last Glacial Maximum under our scaling." Claim a cause only with independent evidence: fossils, other species scaled the same way, or a fitted model with and without structure.
"Ne increased again in the last 5,000 years."Few coalescences happen that recently; bootstraps spread widely, and the first window is where the default-pattern artefact sits (for human data Li & Durbin 2011 wrote that "more recently than 20 kya ... few recombination events are left")."The most recent ~10,000 years are not resolved by this analysis."
"The species split 1.2 million years ago, where the curves separate."Two single-genome curves can separate long after the split, or before it under structure (s07).Use a method built for divergence (hPSMC with calibration simulations, BPP) and report it as in s07.
"100 bootstraps confirm the bottleneck."Bootstraps resample segments of the same genome. They are blind to call errors, the time pattern and μ."The feature is present in all bootstraps, in both pattern reruns and in the read-half replicate" (list what you actually ran).

4. Sharing inputs and outputs

The reads go to a public archive (lesson 2, s13). That is not enough to redo a PSMC, because the calls, the mask and the settings are where analyses differ. Share these small files as well, in a general data repository with a DOI (for example Zenodo or Dryad):

PSMC input (.psmcfa)
One character per 100-bp bin, so about 25 MB for a 2.5-Gb genome before compression. With it, anyone can rerun PSMC with other patterns.
Callable mask (BED)
The regions counted as callable, and the list of excluded contigs.
Heterozygous calls (VCF)
After filtering, with the filter command. A gVCF is better if you have it.
PSMC outputs (.psmc)
Main run and every bootstrap. The file header (MM lines) records the PSMC version, the pattern and the number of iterations, so it documents itself.
Plotted numbers
The time and Ne table behind each line. psmc_plot.pl -R keeps the tables it draws from (prefix.N.txt); without -R it deletes them.
Commands and scripts
Every command with tool versions, and the script that converts to years with μ and g.
