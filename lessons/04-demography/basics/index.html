Lesson 4 · the basics

Population History from One Genome: the basics

The main ideas in a few short blocks, with things to try. The full lesson has the details, commands and sources.

1Any two gene copies share an ancestor

A gene copy is one copy of one stretch of DNA, so N animals hold 2N copies. Each copy comes from one copy in the generation before. Follow two copies back and they reach the same parent copy: they coalesce. The pictures show six toy populations; time runs down the page, and each copy picked its parent at random.

Blue and orange: the two copies followed back. Red ring: where they meet.

2Small populations meet sooner

Each generation, the second line picks the same parent as the first with a chance of 1 in 2N. Few copies means a high chance. Below, 300 pairs were followed back in each of three populations; the bars count how many generations each pair waited.

The dashed line is the average wait.

3Older ancestors mean more differences

After the lines split, each collects random changes in the DNA, called mutations. The copies differ at almost every mutation on both lines, so more differences mean an older ancestor. Below, 40 bases are drawn for each of 4 animals. Red: a base where the mother's and father's copy differ.

4A genome is many stretches, each with an ancestor

In each generation recombination can cut a chromosome and join its halves to pieces from the other copy. So a chromosome becomes a row of stretches, each with its own ancestor. Each bar below is one stretch: width is its length, height is how long ago its ancestor lived.

Height is on a logarithmic scale: each gridline is ten times higher.

5Many stretches show past population size

If the population was small in some period, many stretches had their ancestor then. Counting the meetings in each period gives a size for it. The method PSMC reads this signal from a real genome. Each panel is one animal with 3,000 stretches; the method does not see the grey line.

Top: stretches per time. Bottom: grey, the true size; blue, the size read back.

6More stretches make a steadier curve

The same history is read from 100, 1,000 and 10,000 stretches, six animals each. Periods with few meetings read poorly, and none gives no reading. The curve shows the effective size, not a head count.

Grey: the size we set. Blue: one line per animal. Error: the typical factor of miss.
