Lesson 5 · marks on the DNA that the letters do not show

DNA Methylation from Long Reads

Many cytosines in an animal's genome carry a small methyl group. It does not change the sequence, but it helps keep transposons silent, marks which parent a gene copy came from, changes with tissue and age, and over evolutionary time wears CpG sites away. Long-read machines can see the mark directly on each native DNA molecule. This lesson starts with the biology and how it differs between animal groups, then shows how nanopore and PacBio reads detect methylation and how the calls are stored, how to turn reads into one methylation level per CpG, how to get methylation for each parental copy, how to read it against genes, transposons and satellites, how to compare samples and what epigenetic age clocks need, and how to check and view the results.

Our own long reads of one rodent species (species A) arrived as plain FASTQ without raw signal or modification tags, so no methylation can be called from them; the lesson uses that as its cautionary example of what to ask a sequencing provider to keep, and works with toy data and public human data instead. The footprint of past methylation is still visible in our data: CpG sites in young bat SINEs change about six times faster than other sites. Bisulfite and array methods are only outlined here, and SINEs and satellites themselves are treated in depth in a later lesson. Hover over a dotted term to see what it means.

01What DNA methylation is and why zoologists care

The same genome sits in every cell of an animal, yet a liver cell and a neuron use different genes, a transposon copy stays quiet for millions of years, and only the mother's or only the father's copy of some genes is switched on. Part of that cell memory is written on the DNA itself, as small chemical marks on some bases. The commonest mark in vertebrates is a methyl group on cytosine. Long reads can see it directly, which is what this lesson is about. This first section explains what the mark is, where it sits, what it does, and what a zoologist can and cannot read from it.

A methyl group on a cytosine

5mC
5-methylcytosine: a cytosine (C) with a methyl group (CH3) added at carbon 5 of the ring. The base still pairs with G, so the DNA sequence is unchanged; the mark sits on top of it. That is why it is called epigenetic: information on the DNA that is not part of the sequence.
CpG
A C followed directly by a G on the same strand (the "p" is the phosphate between them). In vertebrates nearly all 5mC sits at CpG. CpG reads the same on both strands (5′-CG-3′ on one, 5′-CG-3′ on the other), so a methylated CpG carries a methyl on both strands.
DNMT
DNA methyltransferase, the enzyme that adds the methyl group. The canonical animal enzymes are DNMT1, DNMT3A and DNMT3B (Lyko 2018). Methylation is put down new (de novo) by DNMT3 and kept by the maintenance enzyme DNMT1 (Bewick et al. 2017). After replication the new strand of a methylated CpG is unmethylated at first, and maintenance methylation restores the mark, which is how the pattern survives cell division.
Methylation level
A tissue sample holds DNA from thousands of cells, and at one CpG some copies carry the mark and some do not. The level of a site is the share of copies that are methylated, from 0 to 100 %. A long read is one DNA molecule from one cell, so it shows the mark of that one copy (s02, s03).
5hmC
5-hydroxymethylcytosine: 5mC oxidised by the TET enzymes (a family of enzymes; TET1 was the first shown to do this) (Tahiliani et al. 2009). It is a step on the way to removing the mark and is abundant in some cells: Kriaucionis & Heintz (2009) measured it at 0.6 % of all nucleotides in cerebellar Purkinje neurons. Bisulfite sequencing, the classic short-read method, cannot tell 5hmC from 5mC (Huang et al. 2010); some long-read models report the two separately (s02).
6mA
N6-methyladenine: a methyl on adenine. Bacteria carry plenty of it. It was reported in several animals, starting with the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans (Greer et al. 2015), but a later test that traced where the signal came from found that bacteria explained most 6mA in insect and plant samples, and found no evidence of high 6mA in Drosophila, Arabidopsis or humans (Kong et al. 2022). Treat an animal 6mA claim as unproven until contamination is ruled out.
Simple explanation with examples: a mark that rides on the sequence

Think of the genome as a printed book and methylation as sticky notes on some letters. The notes do not change the text, but they tell the reader "skip this page". Every cell copies the book before it divides, and a copying enzyme also copies the sticky notes onto the new book, so a liver cell's daughters keep the liver cell's notes.

sequence (both strands) 5′ ...A T C G T T A C G G A... 3′ 3′ ...T A G C A A T G C C T... 5′ copy 1 (one cell) m m both CpGs methylated copy 2 (another cell) m . one methylated copy 3 . . none level at each CpG 67 % 33 % = methylated copies / all copies

A short read after bisulfite treatment and a long read both look at single copies; the level is what you get when you count many of them. A level of 50 % can mean that half the cells are methylated, or that in every cell one of the two parental copies is methylated and the other is not. Only reads that also tell the two copies apart can separate those two cases (s04).

Methylation leaves a scar in the sequence: CpG depletion

A methylated C can lose its amino group by spontaneous deamination and become T. Over evolutionary time this turns CpG into TpG (or CpA on the other strand), so genomes that methylate their CpGs lose them. Bird (1980) proposed this as "the likely cause of the CpG deficiency in heavily methylated genomes". The measure is the ratio of observed to expected CpG, where "expected" is what the C and G content alone predicts:

CpG observed / expected = (number of CpG × N) / (number of C × number of G) N = length of the sequence

This is the definition used by Gardiner-Garden & Frommer (1987), as quoted in the UCSC Genome Browser's CpG-island track description; Zhou et al. (2020) use the same formula for whole genomes. Random sequence gives about 1. Human DNA gives about 0.25 (Zhou et al. 2020). Across 53 genomes, larger genomes held more transposable elements and had lower CpG observed/expected, in the transposons and in the rest of the DNA (Zhou et al. 2020). Bewick et al. (2017) used CpG observed/expected to predict whether an insect methylates its DNA, and the prediction matched the bisulfite data in all 18 species they could check.

The same scar dates repeats. Young copies of a CpG-rich transposon family have lost many CpGs since they were inserted, much faster than their other sites changed; very old families have lost almost all of them. Lesson 3 corrects repeat ages for exactly this effect (lesson 3, s04).

CpG islands

Against that depleted background stand short stretches where CpG is common: CpG islands. Gardiner-Garden & Frommer (1987) found them at the 5′ ends of all housekeeping genes and many tissue-specific genes in the vertebrate sequences they screened, and discussed the idea that they persist because they are kept free of methylation in the germline, so their CpGs do not decay. Saxonov et al. (2006) later found that CpGs in high-CpG promoters do mutate less often, as expected if they are unmethylated in the germline. Their criteria, as the UCSC track gives them: length over 200 bp, G+C content of 50 % or more, and CpG observed/expected above 0.6. Takai & Jones (2002) noted that this definition also catches many Alu copies and proposed a stricter one (over 500 bp, G+C 55 % or more, observed/expected 0.65 or more) that picks out gene starts better. In the human genome 72 % of promoters fall in a high-CpG class (Saxonov et al. 2006). Any threshold is arbitrary, so always say which definition you used.

Where methylation sits and what it does

Jones (2012) summarises the main point: methylation is often called a silencing mark, but "the function of DNA methylation seems to vary with context". In a vertebrate the main contexts are these.

  • Promoters with a CpG island are normally unmethylated: in mammals methylation covers the genome except at CpG islands near promoters (Bewick et al. 2017). Methylation of a promoter island goes with silencing during X inactivation, imprinting and the silencing of transposons (Takai & Jones 2002). When a vertebrate promoter is methylated, the gene is more often a lineage- or tissue-specific one (Keller et al. 2016).
  • Gene bodies (exons and introns) are methylated like the rest of a mammalian genome (Bewick et al. 2017). Jones (2012) treats them as a context of their own, where methylation does not simply mean silence. In invertebrates, methylation sits mostly in the bodies of a subset of genes and goes with their expression (Keller et al. 2016); gene-body methylation is the form shared by plants and animals (Zemach et al. 2010).
  • Transposable elements carry most of the 5mC in mammalian DNA. Their promoters are inactive when methylated, and over time C to T mutations at the methylated sites destroy many copies (Yoder, Walsh & Bestor 1997). The repeat classes are in lesson 3, s02.
  • Satellites. The classical satellite DNA next to human centromeres is normally heavily methylated. In ICF syndrome, caused by mutations in DNMT3B, satellites 2 and 3 are almost unmethylated and the heterochromatin beside the centromeres of chromosomes 1, 9 and 16 becomes unstable (Xu et al. 1999). Satellites are introduced in lesson 3, s02.

One parent's copy: imprinting and X inactivation

Two cases use methylation to treat the two parental copies differently, and both matter for s04.

  • Genomic imprinting. For some genes only the mother's or only the father's copy is expressed. It is known in therian mammals (marsupials and placentals) and not in monotremes; marsupials have fewer imprinted loci (Renfree, Suzuki & Kaneko-Ishino 2013). In placentals the switch is usually a differentially methylated region whose methylation is set in the egg or sperm. In marsupials some imprinted genes have such regions (H19, IGF2, PEG10) and others appear to rely on histone marks instead (Renfree et al. 2008).
  • X inactivation. In female placental mammals one X is silenced, and the CpG islands of the silenced X are methylated, which is considered to stabilise the silence. Marsupials inactivate the father's X in all tissues. In the common wallaroo the CpG island of the X-linked G6PD gene was unmethylated in females and males, although the father's copy was completely silent, which suggests that island methylation is not needed to keep an X silent there (Loebel & Johnston 1996). Sex chromosomes in an assembly are covered in lesson 2, s12.

Why a zoologist cares

  • Age of wild animals. Methylation at some CpGs changes steadily with age, and models built on such sites, epigenetic clocks, estimate age from a skin biopsy, blood or even faeces. Reviews for ecologists: De Paoli-Iseppi et al. (2017) and Parrott & Bertucci (2019). Examples: 712 known-age bats of 26 species (Wilkinson et al. 2021); a pinniped clock from 171 blood and skin samples with a median error of 1.7 years (Robeck et al. 2023); "universal" mammal clocks from 11,754 methylation arrays of 59 tissue types in 185 species (Lu et al. 2023). A clock must be calibrated on animals of known age, and calibration animals can mislead: a clock built on laboratory house mice gave wild mice consistently higher epigenetic ages for a given body mass (Hanski et al. 2024). The pinniped and universal clocks were built with a methylation array that reads a fixed panel of conserved CpGs (37,492 in Robeck et al. 2023), not with long reads.
  • The history of repeats. CpG decay tells young repeat families from old ones, and the strength of the decay tells you whether the lineage methylated its repeats in the germline (box below; s05).
  • Gene regulation and parental conflict. Promoter methylation, imprinting and X inactivation can be read allele by allele from long reads of one animal (s04).
  • Comparisons between tissues, individuals and environments (s06), with the limits below.

How much of it is inherited?

Methylation is copied through cell divisions within one animal. Between generations of mammals most of it is erased twice: in the germ cells and again in the early embryo. Heard & Martienssen (2014) conclude that "these two rounds of epigenetic erasure leave little chance for inheritance of epigenetic marks". Imprints survive the embryo round but are reset in the germline, and some repeat copies escape erasure. Transgenerational inheritance is well documented in plants and in nematodes; in mammals proof of a purely epigenetic basis is "generally lacking", and many studies show only inter-generational effects, such as a mother's diet acting on the fetus she carries. Horsthemke (2018) adds that genetic, ecological and cultural inheritance confound such studies. For a zoologist: a methylation difference between two populations is a description of those animals in their environment and tissue, not by itself evidence of heritable adaptation. Section s06 deals with how to compare samples.

02How long reads see methylation

A methyl group on a cytosine does not change the letter: a 5mC is still read as C. So a machine that reports only letters cannot see it. Short-read methods solve this with chemistry before sequencing. Long-read machines measure a physical signal while they read each native DNA molecule, and the methyl group nudges that signal. Whether you can ever get methylation out of your data therefore depends on one practical question: did the signal, or a call made from it, survive into the files you received?

The two long-read platforms most used for methylation measure different things. Bisulfite and EM-seq are the short-read reference methods; they are listed only for comparison (section 07 returns to them as a check).

Nanopore: the current changes at a methylated base

In a nanopore sequencer a protein pore sits in a membrane with a voltage across it. Ions flowing through the pore make a tiny current. A motor protein feeds one DNA strand through the pore, a base at a time. Several neighbouring bases sit in the narrow part of the pore together, so the current at each step depends on a short word of bases, a k-mer, not on a single base. The basecaller turns this staircase of current levels into letters. A methyl group on a C changes the shape and charge inside the pore a little, so every step whose k-mer contains that C sits at a slightly different level. The early methods (Simpson et al. 2017, Rand et al. 2017) showed this with DNA methylated in the test tube; today's models are neural networks trained the same way, on DNA of known methylation.

PacBio sees the same methyl group in a different way. The polymerase builds a new strand on the template and each added base gives a flash of light. The IPD is the pause before a base is added and the pulse width is how long the addition takes (PacBio BAM specification). A modified base in the template changes these times around it.

All levels, shifts and times are invented to show the principle; real k-mer sizes, levels and shifts depend on the pore, chemistry and model. Switch the methyl group on, then raise the noise.

A real model does not look at one step. It takes the signal over a window of bases around each candidate site and outputs a probability that the C is modified. Because the shift is small next to the noise, the answer for a single read is a probability, not a yes or no; certainty comes from many reads at the same site (section 03).

Calling modified bases with Dorado

For Oxford Nanopore data the basecaller is Dorado (version 2.1.2, 26 August 2026). Modified bases are called during basecalling, from the raw signal: you add a modification model to the basecalling model. Dorado reads only pod5 input; fast5 support was removed in version 1.0.0 (May 2025), and fast5 files must first be converted to pod5. In the source code the modification caller takes chunks of signal, not letters, which is why a FASTQ file cannot be re-called for methylation.

# basecall + 5mC/5hmC at CpG sites, from raw signal (pod5), on a GPU; output: unaligned BAM with MM/ML tags
dorado basecaller sup,5mCG_5hmCG pod5s/ > calls.bam

# the same, pinning the basecalling model version (the modification model then matches it)
dorado basecaller sup@v5.2.0,5mCG_5hmCG pod5s/ > calls.bam

# add the move table (signal-to-base map) if other tools will need it later
dorado basecaller sup,5mCG_5hmCG --emit-moves pod5s/ > calls.bam

The first argument is a model complex: a speed (fast, hac or sup, from fastest to most accurate), an optional @version, then modification models separated by commas. Modification models are tied to the basecalling model version, and only one modification model per base type is allowed (so sup,4mC_5mC,5mC_5hmC is refused: both act on C). PacBio: calls made on the instrument

For HiFi reads, the ccs step, run with --hifi-kinetics, stores averaged timing values for each strand of the molecule separately as per-base tags (fi/fp for the forward strand, ri/rp for the reverse). PacBio's jasmine (version 26.1.3) then turns them into calls:

  • 5mC at CpG sites: one call per CpG pair, written on the forward strand, using timing from both strands; if either strand's timing is missing, no 5mC calls are made for that read. Reads with fewer than 2 passes (--min-passes) get no calls.
  • 5hmC at CpG sites (strand by strand) and 6mA (for the Fiber-seq assay) with separate models.
  • Output: the same MM/ML tags as Dorado.

PacBio's Revio page states that "every standard run delivers on-instrument 5mC, 5hmC, and 6mA methylation calling". So a modern HiFi delivery in BAM form normally already carries the calls; the timing values themselves may or may not be kept in the delivered file. For counting calls per CpG, jasmine's README now recommends PacBio's MethBat and calls the older pb-CpG-tools deprecated (section 03).

How the calls are written: MM and ML tags

Both platforms use the same two tags, defined in the SAM tags specification (hts-specs). MM says which bases were assessed and ML gives a probability for each. A tag such as MM:Z:C+m?,3,1; reads: base C, on the same strand as the read (+), modification m (5mC), then a list of how many Cs to skip before each called C. ML:B:C,249,4 then gives one number from 0 to 255 per called C; the number N means a probability between N/256 and (N+1)/256. The flag after the code says what the skipped Cs mean: . (or nothing) "assume unmodified", ? "not assessed, no information". Positions count from the 5′ end of the read as it was sequenced, so the tags do not change when an aligner flips a read to the reverse strand.

Simple explanation with examples: bold letters in a retyped letter

Think of a hand-written letter in which some words are underlined. The underlining is the methylation; the words are the DNA letters. A nanopore or PacBio machine "reads the paper": it can feel the underlining. A basecaller that only outputs letters is a typist who retypes the letter in plain text. The words survive, the underlining is gone, and no amount of rereading the typed copy brings it back.

paper (signal): the CG site in this CG island typed (FASTQ): the CG site in this CG island underlining lost typed + notes: the CG site in this CG island MM:Z:C+m?,0,1; ML:B:C,240,20 "first C: 94 % underlined; third C: 8 %"

The MM/ML tags are the typist's notes in the margin: "the first C was underlined, quite sure; the third C probably not". A FASTQ file has no margin. To get the notes, either keep the paper (the raw signal, pod5) so the letter can be read again with a better typist, or ask the typist to write the notes the first time (basecalling with a modification model, delivered as BAM).

Bisulfite is a different trick: before typing, you rub the paper with a chemical that turns every non-underlined C into a T. Now the plain typed copy shows methylation as letters, and a plain FASTQ is enough. That is why short-read methylation data can be FASTQ, while long-read methylation data cannot.

Why a plain FASTQ loses the information

A FASTQ record holds four things: a name, the letters, a separator and one quality character per letter (see lesson 1). There is no field for a probability per base of being modified, and the raw signal is not stored at all. Once reads are delivered only as FASTQ, methylation cannot be recovered from them: there is nothing to re-call. (Methylation can still leave faint traces as extra basecalling errors at modified sites, which can look like SNPs; section 07 covers this. That is a nuisance, not a measurement.)

03From reads to per-site methylation

After basecalling (section 02) every read carries its own modification calls in the MM and ML tags: one probability per CpG for one molecule. A biologist usually wants one number per CpG for the animal: what fraction of the DNA molecules in the sample were methylated there. This section covers the two steps in between: aligning the reads to the genome without losing the tags, and stacking the reads at each CpG and counting, the pileup. It uses modkit for Oxford Nanopore data and PacBio's tools for HiFi data.

The words

Pileup
All reads that cover one position of the reference, stacked on top of each other. A pileup program walks along the genome and, at each position, counts what the stacked reads say.
Methylation level
At one CpG: the number of reads called methylated divided by the number of reads with a usable call there. It is a fraction of molecules, not a probability for one molecule. Tissue is a mix of cells and each cell has two copies of each chromosome, so a level of 60 % can mean "60 % of the molecules in the tube were methylated at this site".
bedMethyl
The table a pileup writes: one row per CpG (or per CpG and strand), with its position, coverage, counts and percentage. The format comes from the ENCODE project's bisulfite pipelines; modkit writes an extended version with extra count columns.
Pass threshold
The minimum confidence a read's call must have to be counted. Calls below it are not thrown into "unmethylated"; they are set aside as failed and do not enter the percentage.

Step 1: align without losing the tags

The MM tag lists modified positions by counting bases from the start of the read as it came off the machine. Two things can break that link during alignment (SAM tags specification, MM/MN entries):

  • The tags are not carried over. Most aligners read FASTQ, and a FASTQ has nowhere to keep MM and ML (section 02). Start from the unaligned BAM the basecaller wrote, and use an aligner path that copies the tags.
  • The read is shortened. When one read aligns in pieces, aligners report the extra pieces as supplementary alignments and often hard-clip them: the unaligned ends are cut out of the stored sequence. The positions in MM then point at the wrong bases. The MN tag records the read length when the tags were written, so tools can detect this; with soft clipping (minimap2 and Dorado flag -Y) the sequence stays whole.

Step 2: count reads per CpG

modkit pileup (version 0.6.4, 11 June 2026) reads an aligned, sorted and indexed modBAM (a BAM with MM/ML tags) and writes one bedMethyl row per reference position and modification. Three options shape the table for CpG work:

--cpg
Report only reference CpGs. It is shorthand for --motif CG 0: count the C of CG on the top strand, and the C opposite the G on the bottom strand. Needs --ref.
--combine-strands
A CpG is palindromic: its partner on the other strand is also a CpG, and in vertebrates the two are usually methylated together. This option adds the bottom-strand counts to the top-strand position and writes the strand as . "indicating that the strand information has been lost". Roughly doubles the coverage per row. The help text adds that it "requires that the BAM records have correct MN tags".
--modified-bases 5mC 5hmC
Which modifications to report (with --ref, the faster code path in version 0.6+). A 5mCG_5hmCG model gives three classes per C (unmodified, 5mC, 5hmC), so each site gets an m row and an h row. --combine-mods adds them into one "modified" count if you only want modified versus not.

How modkit decides which calls to count

For each read at each C, the model gives probabilities for every class; the probability of "unmodified" is 1 minus the sum of the modification probabilities (modkit FAQ). The read's call is the most likely class, and its confidence is that highest probability. A call is counted only if its confidence is at least the pass threshold; otherwise it goes into the Nfail column. Because the confidence is the maximum, a read with 5mC probability 0.05 has confidence 0.95 and counts, confidently, as unmethylated. What fails are the uncertain calls in the middle.

By default modkit does not ask you for the threshold. It samples about 10,042 reads (option -n) spread over the genome, collects the confidences of their calls, and sets the threshold at the value below which the lowest 10 % of them fall (--filter-percentile, default 0.1). So by design about one call in ten is dropped. You can instead fix it with --filter-threshold C:0.8, give a separate value per modification with --mod-threshold, cap the estimate with --max-filter-threshold, or switch filtering off with --no-filtering. The documentation recommends letting modkit estimate the value, and suggests modkit modbam sample-probs --hist to look at the distribution "in some cases such as exotic organisms or specialized assays", which for a model trained on human and model-organism data is a fair description of a wild mammal (our reading).

One documented trap: Dorado omits very low modification probabilities from all-context models (--modified-bases-threshold, default 0.05 in the source), and the MM flag . says such skipped Cs are canonical. modkit then treats them as 100 % canonical, which "can affect the estimation of the pass threshold".

Checking the calls before the pileup

Two quick modkit commands describe a modBAM before the long pileup run. modkit modbam check-tags lists which modification codes the file contains (useful when a provider's BAM arrives without documentation). modkit modbam summary prints, per base and code, how many calls there are and how many pass the threshold, and the threshold it estimated (for example pass_threshold_C 0.7089844 in the documentation's example). modkit modbam sample-probs prints the threshold at chosen percentiles (default 10, 50 and 90) and, with --hist --out-dir, histograms of the probabilities. A healthy model gives two peaks, near 0 and near 1; a broad middle means many calls will fail, and suggests the model and the data do not match (for example a model run on another platform's signal, section 02). In version 0.6.4 these subcommands sit under modkit modbam; older tutorials write modkit summary and modkit sample-probs.

PacBio HiFi: MethBat and pb-CpG-tools

PacBio's jasmine writes the same MM/ML tags (section 02), but PacBio has its own pileup tools. jasmine's README (May 2026) recommends MethBat (v1.1.0) "for methylation pileup and downstream analysis" and says pb-CpG-tools "is deprecated in favor of MethBat for new analyses". You will still meet pb-CpG-tools (v3.0.0) in published methods, so both are described here. One difference from nanopore data matters for this section: a HiFi read is a consensus of both strands of the molecule, and jasmine "integrates signal from both strands into a single prediction" and "reports one call per CpG dyad on the forward strand" (a dyad is the pair of Cs of one CpG, one on each strand). So for HiFi the strand combining already happens in the caller, and both PacBio pileup tools write one unstranded row per CpG.

  • pb-CpG-tools, aligned_bam_to_cpg_scores reports one value per CpG (no strand column), for all reads and, if reads carry haplotype tags, for each haplotype (section 04). Its default model mode feeds the distribution of probabilities at a CpG to a built-in machine-learning model; the source code (v3.0.0) shows it looks at a window of 11 neighbouring CpGs that includes the called one, and summarises each CpG as a 20-bin histogram of read probabilities. The result is an estimate, not a count of reads. --pileup-mode count calls every read with probability above 0.5 methylated and the rest unmethylated, with no failed class. In the source, every read base aligned at the site counts towards coverage, and a read with no probability there is counted as unmethylated, so a read carrying a T (a C>T SNP) also counts as unmethylated. Minimum coverage defaults to 4 reads, minimum mapping quality to 1.
  • MethBat, methbat pileup writes one bgzipped BED per modification (5mC, 5hmC, 6mA) with cov, mod_count, unmod_count, diff_base_count and haplotype rows in one file; 5mC rows are unstranded. Defaults: at least 4 reads with modification information per site, mapping quality at least 1, and 20 bases trimmed from each read end. A --preset pbcpgtools exists only to reproduce pb-CpG-tools count mode.

Our inference, not tested by us: modkit reads the standard MM/ML tags and should also run on HiFi modBAMs, which would let ONT and PacBio samples go through one pileup tool. Check the modbam summary output first if you try it.

Simple explanation with examples: a show of hands with some unclear votes

Think of each CpG as a question put to a room of DNA molecules: "are you methylated?" Each read is one molecule raising or not raising its hand. The basecaller is the person counting hands, and for each molecule it writes how sure it is: 0.97 means "clearly a hand", 0.05 means "clearly no hand", 0.5 means "I couldn't see".

The pileup is the tally. modkit's rule is: count only the votes you are reasonably sure about, in either direction, and put the unclear ones in a separate pile (Nfail). The result, "7 of 10 clear votes were hands", is the methylation level, 70 %.

Example from the toy above, CpG 2 with the threshold at 0.70 and strands combined: of 11 reads, 1 has a deletion, 5 are clear (0.91, 0.86, 0.71 yes; 0.30 and 0.12 no) and 5 are unclear (0.62, 0.58, 0.52, 0.45, 0.40). The level is 3 of 5 = 60 %, resting on only 5 reads. Change the threshold and different reads count, so the level moves. A site where most votes are unclear does not have a reliable level, however carefully you count.

Combining strands is like counting the same question in two rooms that should agree: the two Cs of one CpG sit on opposite strands, and in vertebrates they are usually methylated together, so their votes are added.

04Methylation on each parental copy

A diploid animal carries two copies of every autosome, one from its mother and one from its father. At most CpG sites both copies carry the same mark. At some places, though, one copy is methylated and the other is not, and the average over all reads then reads "50 %", a number that describes neither copy. Long reads can solve this, because one read can carry both the methylation calls and the heterozygous SNPs that show which copy it came from. This section shows how to sort reads by parental copy, how to get methylation for each copy, and what the result can and cannot tell you about the mother and the father.

The words

Haplotype
The sequence of one of the two copies of a chromosome: the alleles that sit together on the mother's copy, or on the father's.
Phasing
Working out which alleles of heterozygous sites sit together on the same copy. Result: a VCF where genotypes are written 0|1 or 1|0 (a bar instead of a slash) instead of the unphased 0/1. Heterozygous SNVs and their calling are covered in lesson 3, s09.
Phase block
A stretch in which every heterozygous site is phased relative to every other one. Between blocks the link is lost: nothing says whether block 2's "first copy" is the same parental copy as block 1's. In the VCF, the block ID is the PS (phase set) field.
Haplotagging
Labelling each read with the copy it came from, using the phased SNPs it covers. WhatsHap and LongPhase write the label as the BAM tag HP:i:1 or HP:i:2, plus PS for the block. A read that covers no heterozygous site gets no label.
Allele-specific methylation (ASM)
A site or region where the two copies differ in methylation. Its causes include parent of origin (imprinting), X inactivation, and the DNA sequence of the copy itself.
Genomic imprinting
Expression of a gene from only one parental copy, chosen by the parent it came from. It is controlled by differentially methylated regions (DMRs) whose marks are set in the egg or the sperm; the controlling DMR of a cluster of imprinted genes is called an imprinting control region (ICR).
X inactivation
In female mammals one of the two X chromosomes is silenced in each cell. In placental mammals CpG islands on the inactive X are methylated.
Simple explanation with examples: why "50 % methylated" can mean two different things

Think of a CpG site as a light switch in each cell's two copies of a chromosome. A bulk measurement only counts how many switches are on. "Half of the switches are on" can mean two very different houses:

House A (imprinted region): mother's copy ● ● ● ● ● father's copy ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ → every cell the same House B (no allele effect): mother's copy ● ○ ● ○ ● father's copy ○ ● ○ ● ○ → random per site both houses: 5 of 10 = 50 %

A short read covers one or two CpG sites and cannot tell the houses apart. A long read covers the whole region and a heterozygous SNP nearby, so it shows both the pattern (all on, or all off) and the copy it came from. Sorting reads by copy turns House A into two clean numbers, about 100 % and about 0 %, while House B stays at 50 % on both copies.

Where parent-specific methylation shows up in animals

  • Imprinted genes. Imprinting has been found in placental mammals and in marsupials, and not (as yet) in the egg-laying monotremes (Renfree et al. 2009, 2013). Marsupials have fewer imprinted loci, and some marsupial imprinted genes have no DMR at all and are silenced by histone marks instead; H19, IGF2 and PEG10 do have DMRs in marsupials (Renfree et al. 2008, 2013). The marks are set in the germline, so in a body tissue an ICR is expected to be close to 100 % on one copy and close to 0 % on the other. Gigante et al. (2019) found known ICRs in mouse samples from nanopore reads at an average coverage of about 10×.
  • X inactivation in females. In placental mammals each cell silences one X at random, and CpG islands on the silenced X are methylated (Loebel & Johnston 1996; Peeters et al. 2016). Because the choice is random, a bulk tissue mixes cells that silenced the mother's X with cells that silenced the father's, and both copies average about 50 % at an X CpG island unless the tissue is skewed (most cells chose the same X; Peeters et al. 2016 discuss causes, including the mouse Xce locus). Rodent extra-embryonic membranes and all marsupial tissues silence the father's X, but Loebel & Johnston (1996) found an X-linked CpG island of the common wallaroo unmethylated in both males and females, and concluded that marsupial X inactivation does not need CpG island methylation.
  • Sequence-dependent ASM. Many allelic methylation differences follow the DNA sequence of the copy (for example a SNP that destroys a CpG or a transcription-factor site), not the parent. Onuchic et al. (2018) found thousands of such loci in human tissues. In one animal you cannot tell this from imprinting: both give one methylated and one unmethylated haplotype. Parents' genomes, or the same locus in several individuals, separate the two.

The workflow

  1. Aligned reads with methylation tags
    A sorted, indexed BAM whose reads still carry their MM/ML tags (modification tags) after alignment (s03).
    dorado / samtools fastq -T + minimap2 -y · pbmm2
  2. Call heterozygous SNVs
    On the same reads or on better ones from the same animal (lesson 3, s09).
    Clair3 · DeepVariant
  3. Phase the SNVs
    Long reads link neighbouring heterozygous sites into phase blocks.
    whatshap phase · longphase phase
  4. Haplotag the reads
    Each read gets HP and PS tags; its methylation tags are kept.
    whatshap haplotag · longphase haplotag
  5. Pile up methylation per haplotype
    One bedMethyl table per copy, plus one with all reads.
    modkit pileup --phased · methbat pileup · aligned_bam_to_cpg_scores (PacBio)
  6. Compare the copies
    Within each phase block, regions where HP1 and HP2 differ by a lot; check known imprinted genes first.
    your own script · modkit dmr (s06) · IGV (s07)
# whatshap 2.8. One sample, read groups not trusted: --ignore-read-groups.
# --reference is "highly recommended" for error-prone long reads (WhatsHap guide).

samtools view -c -d HP:1 hap.bam; samtools view -c -d HP:2 hap.bam   # reads per copy

# (before v0.6.0 this was --partition-tag HP; the docs say --phased replaces it)

What can go wrong

  • HP1 is not "the mother". WhatsHap sets HP from the order of alleles in the phased genotype (1|0 means haplotype 1 carries the ALT allele). That order is arbitrary in each phase block. HP1 in block 1 and HP1 in block 2 can be different parents. modkit's --phased splits by HP only, so a genome-wide hp1.bedmethyl is a patchwork of both parents (our reading of the two documentations). Compare HP1 with HP2 within a block, never across blocks.
  • Parent of origin needs extra information. Parents' reads (a trio), or known imprinted DMRs used as anchors: Akbari et al. (2023) assigned the parent of origin of whole human chromosomes from nanopore methylation at known imprinted loci plus Strand-seq phasing, without parental DNA. For a wild species without a list of imprinted loci, use parents' genomes, or say that HP1/HP2 is unassigned.
  • Each copy gets half the depth. At 30× total, each copy has about 15× minus untagged reads. Summarise over regions (a DMR, a CpG island), not single CpGs.
  • Hemizygous X and Y. In a male, the X has one copy and no heterozygous sites, so nothing can be phased there; find these contigs by half depth (lesson 2, s12). The same holds for the Z in female birds.
  • Tissue is a mixture. Imprinted loci vary little between tissues (Akbari et al. 2023), but other ASM can differ between cell types, and X inactivation is a mosaic. A 50/50 per-copy result on the X is expected, not a failure.
  • Wrong phasing looks like weaker ASM. A switch error in the middle of a DMR mixes the copies and pulls both towards 50 %. A DMR that is 90/10 on one side of a block boundary and 50/50 on the other is worth a look in IGV, coloured by HP and PS (as the WhatsHap guide shows).

Methylation can also help phasing and assembly checks

The logic of the toy runs the other way, too. Where heterozygous SNPs are too sparse to link two phase blocks, CpG sites whose methylation differs between the copies can act as extra "alleles". MethPhaser (Fu et al. 2024) does this on SNV-phased nanopore data and reports a 78 % to 151 % longer phase-block N50 at a phasing accuracy of 83.4 % to 98.7 % on human cell lines. LongPhase does it in one step: longphase modcall calls allele-specific 5mC sites from MM/ML tags, and longphase phase --mod-file phases them together with the SNPs; its README reports longer blocks with no rise in switch errors at 60×. For a diploid assembly, methylation at known imprinted regions is how Akbari et al. (2023) gave whole haplotypes a mother or father label; for an assembly with hap1 and hap2 (lesson 2, s07) the same idea could label them, but only in species where imprinted regions are known (our inference; we have not tried it).

# LongPhase 2.1.2: SNP + 5mC co-phasing (README example; output names follow -o)
longphase modcall -b reads.bam -r asm.fa -t 8 -o modcall
longphase phase -s snps.vcf --mod-file modcall.vcf -b reads.bam -r asm.fa -t 8 -o phased --ont   # --pb for HiFi

Both tools were tested on human data. MethPhaser's authors write that the concept "can also be extended to non-human diploid genomes"; we know of no test in a wild species.

Simple explanation with examples: reading the per-copy result

Suppose a region gives, inside one phase block, hp1 92 %, hp2 6 %, combined 49 %. One copy is methylated and the other is not. What you know: this animal has allele-specific methylation here. What you do not know from these numbers alone: whether HP1 is the mother's copy, and whether the difference comes from the parent (imprinting) or from the sequence of HP1 itself.

If the same region gives hp1 48 %, hp2 52 %: no allele effect, or (on the X of a female) random inactivation. If it gives hp1 90 %, hp2 10 % on one side of a PS boundary and 10 %, 90 % on the other: the labels swapped at the block boundary, which is normal.

The genome-wide picture in mammals

In most mammalian cells, most CpGs are methylated. Ziller et al. (2013), with 42 whole-genome bisulfite data sets from 30 human cell and tissue types, put it at 70-80 % of all CpGs, outside germ cells and early embryos. The small rest is not spread evenly: it sits in short, CpG-dense stretches that are almost free of methylation. Plotted as a histogram of per-CpG methylation, a mammalian genome therefore has a tall peak near 100 % and a small second peak near 0 %: the distribution is bimodal. Gershman et al. (2022) saw exactly this with nanopore reads of the human cell line HG002 (median 75 % methylation, "with a secondary peak of unmethylated CpGs").

The cell type matters. The same study found the other cell line, CHM13 (from a hydatidiform mole), "dramatically hypomethylated" at a median of 36.8 %. For a wild animal this means: write down the tissue, and compare samples only from the same tissue (section 06).

Genes: unmethylated promoters, methylated bodies

CpG islands
Stretches where CpG is not depleted. The classical definition (Gardiner-Garden & Frommer 1987) is at least 200 bp with G+C of 50 % or more and an observed/expected CpG ratio over 0.6; Takai & Jones (2002) proposed a stricter one (over 500 bp, G+C at least 55 %, ratio at least 0.65) that excludes most Alu SINEs. Islands sit at the 5' ends of all housekeeping genes and many tissue-specific genes, and they are "predominantly nonmethylated" (Deaton & Bird 2011). A methylated island means a silenced gene.
Promoters without islands
Gardiner-Garden & Frommer found islands at all housekeeping genes but only at "many" tissue-specific genes; the rest have CpG-poor promoters. With few CpGs there, a promoter value rests on few sites and is noisy.
Gene bodies
Inside transcribed genes, methylation is high, and highest in actively transcribed genes: "low promoter methylation and high gene-body methylation in highly expressed genes" (Ball et al. 2009). Gene-body methylation is old: Zemach et al. (2010) found it conserved between plants and animals across 17 eukaryotes, while the targeting of methylation to transposons was not conserved.

The usual way to show this is a metaplot (meta-profile): line up all genes at their transcription start site (TSS), turn genes on the minus strand around so that upstream is always to the left, and average methylation in small distance bins. A mammalian profile dips to near zero at TSSs with islands and climbs back to high values in the gene body.

Repeats: methylated, and the young ones carry the signal

Methylation of CpG in transposable elements keeps them silent; Zhou et al. (2020) argue that this is what lets genomes accommodate TEs at all, and across 53 genomes they found that larger genomes have more TEs and fewer CpGs (lower CpG observed/expected) in both TEs and host DNA. The price is mutation: methylated C in CpG turns into T by deamination much faster than other bases change (lesson 3, section 04, the CpG rule of repeat landscapes). So the age of a copy decides how much methylation it can show:

  • Young copies are nearly identical to their source and keep almost all their CpGs. They carry a dense methylation signal. In the human genome the youngest L1s (L1Hs) show a methylation peak at their 5' end followed by a hypomethylated (less methylated than its surroundings) TSS (Hoyt et al. 2022), and Ewing et al. (2020) found young L1s demethylated in liver cancer and, in some normal tissues, at L1 copies that were expressed; SVA retrotransposons were "near-universally methylated".
  • Old copies have lost most of their CpGs to C→T changes. Hoyt et al. describe older, truncated elements with "low CpG content and higher divergence". Their few remaining CpGs may still be methylated, but there are too few of them for a precise per-copy value.
  • Satellites (tandem arrays, lesson 3, section 04). In the complete human genome, active alpha-satellite arrays have "generally high CpG methylation" and beta and gamma satellites dense methylation (Altemose et al. 2022). Inside each active array the long-read maps found a stretch of reduced methylation, the centromere dip region (CDR), where the centromere protein CENP-A is most enriched (Altemose et al. 2022; Gershman et al. 2022). The toy below has one such dip in its satellite.

Mappability: why repeats were a blind spot, and how long reads help

A methylation value is only as good as the placement of the reads behind it. A 150-bp read from a young L1 copy matches hundreds of places equally well; the aligner gives it a mapping quality (MAPQ) of 0, and it is discarded. Its CpGs get no data, or worse, data pooled from many copies. On the complete human genome, the 165 Mb that short reads cannot map at all were "enriched in SDs and satellite DNA" (segmental duplications), and nanopore reads measured 3.18 million more CpGs (about 10 %) than short-read bisulfite data (Gershman et al. 2022). For centromeres the authors used reads longer than 50 kb.

The rule is simple: a read is placed uniquely if it contains enough differences from every other copy, or if it is long enough to run out of the repeat into the unique DNA on both sides. Long reads do the second. Two settings in the tools follow from this:

  • Filter by MAPQ before the pileup. modkit pileup uses primary alignments only, but its help (v0.6.4) has no mapping-quality option, so filter the BAM first (samtools view -q 10). methylartist filters at MAPQ 10 by default (-q/--min_mapq).
  • Require reads that leave the repeat. methylartist segmeth --excl_ambig ignores reads that align entirely within a segment, so only reads anchored in flanking DNA count. Use it when you score TE copies.

    --modified-bases 5mC 5hmC --combine-mods --ref genome.fa --log-filepath pileup.log

# bedMethyl columns used below: 2 = CpG start (0-based), 10 = valid reads, 11 = % modified, 12 = modified reads

With --combine-mods, 5mC and 5hmC are counted together as "modified" (section 03 explains when to keep them apart).

1. Average over regions (promoters, gene bodies, islands)

# genes from a GFF3 into BED (tab-separated: chrom, start-1, end, attributes, ., strand)
awk -F'\t' 'BEGIN{OFS="\t"} $3=="gene"{print $1,$4-1,$5,$9,".",$7}' genes.gff3 | sort -k1,1 -k2,2n > genes.bed
# modkit: pooled % = modified calls / valid calls over all CpGs in each region (needs the bgzipped, indexed file)
modkit stats sample.cpg.bed.gz --regions genes.bed --min-coverage 5 -o genes.stats.tsv
# bedtools 2.31 alternative: sum modified (col 12) and valid (col 10) calls per region; "." = no CpG with data
bedtools map -a genes.bed -b sample.cpg.bed.gz -c 12,10 -o sum,sum > genes.meth.tsv

bedtools map needs both files sorted by position. Pooling counts (sum of modified ÷ sum of valid) weighs each CpG by its depth; averaging the per-CpG percentages (-c 11 -o mean) weighs every CpG equally. Say which one you used.

Simple explanation with examples: reading a methylation map like a street map

Think of each CpG as a street lamp that is either off (methylated, the default) or on (unmethylated). Seen from a plane at night, a mammalian genome is mostly dark, with small bright spots: the CpG islands at the doors of genes. That is the bimodal histogram: many dark lamps, a few bright ones, very few half-lit.

A metaplot is like standing at the front door of every house in town at once and asking: how bright is it 5 km before the door, at the door, and 5 km into the house? You must turn every house so its door faces the same way (the strand flip), or the hallway and the garden get mixed up.

gene on + strand: upstream ......[TSS]=====body=====> gene on - strand: <=====body=====[TSS]...... upstream after the flip: upstream ......[TSS]=====body=====> (d = -(CpG - TSS))

Transposons are copies of the same house built at different times. New ones still have all their lamps (CpGs); in old ones most lamps have been ripped out by the CpG→TpG mutation, so you can hardly tell whether the few left are on or off. And with short reads you cannot even tell which of 500 identical new houses you are looking at, so the newest street is missing from the map.

Pitfalls

  • Collapsed repeats in the assembly. If many young copies were collapsed into one in the assembly (lesson 2, section 06), the reads of all of them pile onto that one copy, with high depth and a mixed value. Check depth over the copy before trusting it.
  • Your individual is not your reference. A CpG of the reference that is TpG in your animal (a SNP, or a C→T change in a young TE copy) has no call; modkit counts such reads as Ndiff (column 17) or Nnocall (column 18). Methylation on each parental copy is in section 04.
  • Few CpGs, big noise. A copy with 3 CpGs at 10× has 30 calls; one with 60 CpGs has 600. Set a minimum of valid calls per copy and report how many copies passed.
  • Tissue and individual. Promoter and young-TE methylation change between tissues and in disease. One sample shows one state.
  • Other animals differ. The mammalian picture (methylated almost everywhere except islands) is not universal: across 17 eukaryotes, gene-body methylation was conserved but the targeting of transposons was not (Zemach et al. 2010), and the CpG-decay footprint in TEs is weaker in fish and an insect than in mammals (note below). Do not assume the mammalian pattern for a new lineage; measure it.
In our projects: methylation history written in the repeats. Without any methylation calls, the lab's SINE work already measures the footprint of germline methylation. Substitutions were counted at CpG sites of each SINE family consensus versus all other sites. In young families (non-CpG substitution density under 0.08), CpG sites changed a median 5.8 times faster in Rhinolophus sinicus (up to 8.8 in family r9), 6.3 in Rousettus leschenaultii and 5.8 in human, close to the published Alu value of about 6 (Xing et al. 2004). Old bat families (MEG-T2, MEG-RS and others) have ratios of only 0.9-1.9: their CpGs are already gone. Zebrafish gave 2.7 and the insect Timema 1.5. In the toy above, these old families would be the dots in the lower right of the CpG plot. The code is in the public SINE-discriminator repository. Whether our long reads could place young SINE and L1 copies uniquely depends on read length: the reads of species B and C are short on average (lesson 2), enough to span a SINE with flanks but not a full-length L1, while the reads of species A are several times longer; but they carry no methylation calls in any case (section 02).

06Comparing samples: differential methylation

Once every CpG of a genome has a methylation level (s03), the next question is usually a comparison: does this region differ between males and females, between two tissues, two populations, young and old animals, or the mother's and the father's copy? This section explains what the comparison tools compute, why the number of animals matters more than the number of reads, how a statistically "significant" difference can be biologically trivial, which hidden differences between samples (tissue, age, sex) can fake a result, and what epigenetic age clocks can and cannot do with the few animals a zoologist usually has.

In our projects. The lab has no methylation data to compare yet: none of our genomes has modification calls (s02), and the species A genome is a single male. Everything below therefore uses toy data and the tools' own examples. For population questions in the species A project we already concluded that more individuals had to be sequenced; the same holds for any methylation comparison (our inference, explained below).

The words

Methylation level
At one CpG in one sample: reads that call the C methylated divided by all reads with a call there (modkit's percent modified, s03). Over a region: the same ratio with the calls of all CpGs in it added up.
DML / DMR
A differentially methylated locus is a single CpG whose level differs between the groups; a differentially methylated region is a stretch of neighbouring CpGs that differ together. Regions are what biologists usually report, because neighbouring CpGs tend to share their state and one CpG alone is noisy.
Effect size
How big the difference is, in a unit you can interpret: here, level in group A minus level in group B (0.80 − 0.60 = 0.20, "20 percentage points").
Significance
How sure you can be that a difference is not chance. A p-value is the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if there were no true difference. Small p means "unlikely to be chance", not "large" or "important".
Biological replicate
Another animal of the same group. Not another read, not another library of the same animal (that is a technical replicate). Only biological replicates tell you how much animals of one group differ among themselves.
False discovery rate (FDR)
Among everything you call "different", the share that is not. Testing millions of CpGs at p < 0.05 gives tens of thousands of chance hits, so p-values are corrected, usually with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.
Simple explanation with examples: why 100 reads from one animal are not 100 animals

Imagine you want to know whether mice from the mountains are heavier than those from the lowlands. You catch one mouse from each place and weigh each of them a hundred times. The hundred weighings tell you very precisely what these two mice weigh. They tell you nothing about how much mice vary within a place, so you cannot say whether the difference belongs to the places or to the two individuals.

Reads are the weighings. At one CpG, 30 reads of an animal measure that animal's methylation level very well. If the level of this CpG differs naturally between animals (it often does, by tissue, age, sex, genotype, or for no known reason), a test that pools the reads of the group treats animal-to-animal differences as if they were group differences:

group A, 1 animal: 24 of 30 reads methylated = 0.80 group B, 1 animal: 15 of 30 reads methylated = 0.50 pooled-read test: p ≈ 0.015 "significant" but: the same CpG in three more animals of group A reads 0.55, 0.62, 0.71

With several animals per group, the test compares the spread between groups with the spread within groups, and the false signal disappears. The toy below lets you try this.

The workflow

  1. One table per sample
    For each animal (or each parental copy, s04): a bedMethyl table of CpG counts from the same pipeline, the same reference and the same basecaller model (s03, s07).
    modkit pileup · bgzip · tabix
  2. Look before testing
    Coverage per sample, overall level per sample, a clustering or principal-component plot of all samples: an outlier animal or a batch effect shows up here, not in a DMR list.
    modkit modbam summary · R / Python
  3. Explore: one sample against another
    Region scores, single-site scores and segmentation into "same" and "different" blocks. Quick and needs no replicates; a ranking, not a statistical answer about groups.
    modkit dmr pair · modkit dmr multi
  4. Test groups of animals
    A model of counts with animal-to-animal variation, other factors (sex, age, tissue) in the design, corrected for testing many CpGs.
    DSS · dmrseq · methylKit · metilene
  5. Filter by effect size and look at the data
    Keep DMRs that are both well supported and large enough to matter; plot the per-animal levels of each one you report.
    IGV · your own plots (s07)

1. modkit dmr: comparing bedMethyl tables

modkit dmr (modkit v0.6.4) compares bedMethyl tables made by modkit pileup. Its documentation states the purpose plainly: "to enable exploratory data analysis of methylation patterns", with scores that are "simple and interpretable". It has two subcommands and three uses:

  • dmr pair with -r regions.bed: one sample against another over regions you supply (CpG islands, promoters, repeat families from s05).
  • dmr multi: every pair among several named samples, regions only; with --ref-sample each sample is compared only with one reference sample. Samples given the same name are combined.
  • dmr pair without -r: single CpGs, optionally joined into segments by a two-state HMM (--segment). This is the mode for the two parental copies of one animal (s04).
# input: bgzip-compressed, tabix-indexed bedMethyl per sample (modkit 0.6.4 docs)
modkit pileup A1.bam - --cpg --ref asm.fa -t 16 --log-filepath A1.pileup.log | bgzip -c > A1.bed.gz
tabix -p bed A1.bed.gz

# 1) regions, one sample against another
modkit dmr pair -a A1.bed.gz -b B1.bed.gz -r regions.bed --ref asm.fa --base C \
  --header -t 16 -o A1_vs_B1.regions.bed --log-filepath dmr.log

# 2) all pairs of named samples over the same regions (-o is a directory here)
modkit dmr multi -s A1.bed.gz A1 -s A2.bed.gz A2 -s B1.bed.gz B1 -s B2.bed.gz B2 \
  -r regions.bed --ref asm.fa --base C --header -t 16 -o dmr_multi/

# 3) single CpGs, replicates repeated with -a / -b, plus HMM segments
modkit dmr pair -a A1.bed.gz -a A2.bed.gz -b B1.bed.gz -b B2.bed.gz --ref asm.fa --base C \
  --header --segment segments.bed -t 16 -o sites.bed --log-filepath dmr.log

--base C picks which modified base is compared (all modifications of C, for example 5mC and 5hmC, unless --single-code names one). --min-valid-coverage (default 0) drops sites with fewer valid calls. Use --header and read columns by name: the column order differs between region, single-site and segment output, and the docs' own example table has one more column than their schema list.

With repeated -a/-b, the help text of --cap-coverages says that three replicates of 30× "would normally" give a total coverage of 90; dmr multi combines samples with the same name. 2. Statistical tests across animals: DSS and others

DSS (Bioconductor, release 2.60.0) was written for bisulfite sequencing, but it needs only, per sample and CpG, the number of reads (N) and the number of methylated reads (X), which a bedMethyl table holds in columns 10 (Nvalid_cov) and 12 (Nmod). It models counts with a beta-binomial distribution, estimates the animal-to-animal spread (the dispersion) of each CpG while borrowing strength across all CpGs (a shrinkage estimator from a Bayesian hierarchical model; Feng et al. 2014), and tests each CpG with a Wald test.

# bedMethyl -> DSS input (chr, 1-based pos, N, X); check that column 4 is "m" in your file
zcat A1.bed.gz | awk 'BEGIN{OFS="\t"; print "chr","pos","N","X"} $4=="m" {print $1,$2+1,$10,$12}' > A1.dss.txt
# R, DSS 2.60 (function defaults read from the DSS source)
library(DSS)
ids  <- c("A1","A2","A3","B1","B2","B3")
dat  <- lapply(ids, function(s) read.table(paste0(s, ".dss.txt"), header = TRUE))
BSobj <- makeBSseqData(dat, ids)

# two groups; smoothing borrows information from CpGs within smoothing.span (default 500 bp)
dml  <- DMLtest(BSobj, group1 = c("A1","A2","A3"), group2 = c("B1","B2","B3"), smoothing = TRUE)
dmrs <- callDMR(dml, delta = 0.1, p.threshold = 0.01)   # defaults: delta 0, p.threshold 1e-5, minlen 50, minCG 3, dis.merge 100, pct.sig 0.5

# other factors in the design (Park & Wu 2016): test group while accounting for sex and age
design <- data.frame(group = c("A","A","A","B","B","B"), sex = c("F","M","F","M","F","M"), age = c(1,2,3,1,2,3))
fit  <- DMLfit.multiFactor(BSobj, design = design, formula = ~ group + sex + age)
test <- DMLtest.multiFactor(fit, coef = "groupB")
dmrs2 <- callDMR(test, p.threshold = 0.01)              # delta is not allowed with multifactor results

With delta = 0.1, callDMR scores each CpG by the posterior probability that the true difference exceeds 0.1 rather than by its p-value. A DMR must be longer than minlen bp, contain more than minCG CpGs, and at least pct.sig of its CpGs must pass. The vignette says it is very difficult to choose a natural and rigorous threshold for DMRs and recommends trying several. (In the source, dis.merge is lowered to minlen when it is larger, so with the defaults DMRs closer than 50 bp are merged.) The six-animal design above is a toy: with six animals and four terms (intercept, group, sex, age) only two degrees of freedom remain.

One animal per group. DMLtest refuses to run with one sample in a group unless smoothing = TRUE (or equal.disp = TRUE). The method for this case (Wu et al. 2015) uses neighbouring CpGs as "pseudo-replicates" to estimate dispersion. That makes the test possible, but it still cannot tell a difference between groups from a difference between the two individuals.

Try it

Two groups of animals, 2,000 CpGs: what does a test find?

A toy genome of 200 regions with 10 CpGs each. Each CpG has a baseline level (most around 0.8, some near 0.1, as in a mammalian genome). In 20 regions (the true DMRs) group B differs from group A by the true difference. Every animal has its own level in every region, scattered around its group's level by the animal-to-animal variation (a standard deviation); reads per CpG follow a Poisson distribution around the coverage, and each read is methylated with the animal's probability. The test is either a pooled-read test (all reads of a group added up, two-proportion z-test; this is what a read-count test without replicates does) or a per-animal test (a moderated t-test on each animal's level; it borrows strength across units as DSS does for its dispersions, but it is a simple stand-in, not DSS itself). Both are corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg at FDR 5 %. The plot is a volcano plot: each dot is one tested region (or CpG), placed by its observed difference (left-right) and its strength of evidence, −log10 of the p-value (up; 2 means p = 0.01, 6 means p = 0.000001).

What to notice. (1) Set animal-to-animal variation to 0: the pooled-read test is then fine, and more coverage helps it. (2) Put the variation back to 0.05 and switch to pooled reads: false positives appear, and raising coverage makes it worse, because more reads make every chance difference between a few animals look certain. (3) With the per-animal test, one animal per group cannot be tested at all, two gives little power, and adding animals helps much more than adding coverage beyond about 10×. (4) Regions find true DMRs that single CpGs miss, because the reads of 10 CpGs add up. (5) Set the variation to 0.02, the true difference to 0.05, 10 animals and 60× coverage: a difference of 5 points becomes "significant" in most true DMRs; the effect-size filter then decides whether you care. (6) Raise the variation to 0.10 and watch how many animals you need for the same power.

3. Effect size and significance are different questions

A p-value mixes two things: how large the difference is and how much data you have. With deep coverage and many CpGs per region, a difference of 1 to 2 percentage points becomes "highly significant"; with few reads, a difference of 30 points may not be. Report both, and decide in advance what size of difference you consider biologically meaningful (DSS's delta, modkit's Cohen's h lower bound, or a plain filter on the difference). 4. Hidden differences between samples (confounders)

A confounder is a difference between your groups other than the one you are studying, which also changes methylation. Every DMR list should be read against this list:

  • Tissue and cell composition. Each cell type has its own methylation profile. A tissue is a mixture, and if the mixture differs between groups (more white blood cells in sick animals, more fat in a liver sample), the levels differ without any cell changing. In human blood, cell composition changes with age and explains much of the variation in methylation, and it is tangled with age at the CpG level (Jaffe & Irizarry 2014). Compare the same tissue, sampled the same way.
  • Age. Many CpGs change with age; that is what epigenetic clocks use (below). If one population was sampled as juveniles and the other as adults, age-related CpGs appear as population DMRs.
  • Sex. In female mammals one X is inactivated and methylated on many promoters (s01, s04). Unbalanced sexes give X-linked "DMRs" and dilute others; analyse sex chromosomes separately (lesson 2, s12), or put sex in the design.
  • Genotype. A SNP that destroys a CpG in some animals removes the site in them (the read shows TG or CA, not CG). In a pooled comparison this looks like a loss of methylation; in a phased animal it shows up as an allele-specific difference (s04).
  • Batch and platform. Different flow cells, basecaller models, read quality or DNA extraction between groups. Sequence the groups mixed, not group after group, and record the basecaller and model for every sample (s07).

If a confounder cannot be avoided, put it in the model (DSS DMLfit.multiFactor), which needs more animals than terms. If it is perfectly tied to the groups (all population A animals are adults, all population B juveniles), no statistics can separate the two.

5. Epigenetic clocks: estimating age from methylation

An epigenetic clock predicts an animal's age from the methylation levels of a set of CpGs. It is built, not discovered: you need many animals of known age, measured at the same CpGs, and a regression that picks the useful sites.

  1. Training animals of known age
    Animals whose age is known independently (marked at birth, captive records, tooth layers). The age error of these animals sets a floor on the clock's accuracy.
  2. Methylation at many CpGs
    An array, targeted sequencing or whole-genome data. The mammalian methylation array used for cross-species clocks measures up to about 36,000 CpGs per species, chosen because their sequence is conserved across many mammals (Arneson et al. 2022).
  3. Elastic net regression
    A penalised regression of (transformed) age on all CpG levels; most coefficients become zero, leaving tens to hundreds of CpGs. Horvath's human pan-tissue clock (2013) used glmnet with alpha 0.5, lambda by cross-validation, and kept 353 CpGs.
  4. Test on animals not used for training
    Report the median absolute error in years and the correlation with true age. With few animals, leave-one-out cross-validation is often the only option.

Scale of the published examples: Horvath's clock was developed using about 8,000 human array samples of 51 tissues and cell types, with a median error of 3.6 years in test data (correlation 0.96). The pan-mammalian clocks of the Mammalian Methylation Consortium used 11,754 arrays from 185 species and 59 tissues (Lu et al. 2023). A review of wildlife clock design (Newediuk et al. 2025) notes that wildlife clocks "often rely on datasets with no more than a few dozen samples", that inaccurate training ages bias any estimate of age acceleration, that clocks are most accurate when the training animals resemble the animals to be aged (tissue, sex, population, age range), and that leave-one-out cross-validation estimated test error as well as a separate hold-out set in their polar bear data. A systematic review and meta-analysis across more than 40 species (Le Clercq et al. 2023) found methylation-based age estimates more accurate than telomere length.

07Quality checks and viewing methylation

A methylation table looks equally convincing whether it is right or wrong: every CpG gets a percentage. This section shows how to check that the numbers deserve trust before you interpret them, how to look at methylation on single reads and along a chromosome, and what to keep from a sequencing run so that methylation can still be measured years later.

Step 0: does the file carry methylation at all?

Methylation calls travel inside the reads, in two tags described in section 02: MM (which bases carry which modification) and ML (how likely each one is). A file without these tags has no methylation in it, whatever its name says. Check three things before any analysis:

# 1. Which modification tags are present, and are they valid? (modkit 0.6.4)
modkit modbam check-tags reads.bam -n 100000 --out-dir tag_check
# 2. Which basecall model and which modification model made them?
samtools view -H reads.bam | grep '^@RG'
#    Dorado writes them into the read-group DS field:
#    ... basecall_model=dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v5.2.0 modbase_models=..._5mCG_5hmCG@v2
# 3. Did you receive FASTQ? Then look at a header: tags survive only if someone copied them
head -1 reads.fastq

check-tags counts records whose tags are broken (and exits with an error if there are any, unless given --permissive) or do not follow the SAM tag specification, and lists which base carries which code (for example C+m?: 5mC on cytosine). Modkit otherwise skips unusable records with only a log message, so this is the step that tells you how much of your data is silently ignored. FASTQ is a text format without a place for tags: they reach a FASTQ header only if they were copied on purpose, for example with samtools fastq -T MM,ML, and reach an alignment only if the aligner copies header comments (minimap2 -y). Methylation is most often lost not in the laboratory but in a file conversion.

In our projects: the long reads of species A (a rodent) arrived as one FASTQ whose headers carry no basecaller model, no read group, no MM/ML tags, and no raw signal files came with them. Step 0 ends there: there is nothing to check, and the methylation of this animal cannot be read from these files. Our request to the provider for the raw signal and run details is recorded in the lab project log as still pending.

Call probabilities: is the model sure?

For every C in a CpG the basecaller gives a probability that it is 5mC (and, with a 5mCG_5hmCG model, 5hmC). Modkit computes the probability that the base is unmodified as one minus the sum of the modification probabilities, and keeps a call only when the most likely class reaches a pass threshold. Calls below it are not removed from the read; they are counted as failed. By default modkit estimates the threshold from your data: it samples the call probabilities and takes the value below which the lowest-confidence 10 % lie (option --filter-percentile of pileup, called --filter-quantile in modbam summary; default 0.1). So about one call in ten fails by design, and the threshold it prints is itself a quality number: the lower it is, the less sure the model is about its weakest tenth.

# distribution of call probabilities, with histograms (HTML) and a table, from ~10,000 reads
modkit modbam sample-probs reads.bam --num-reads 10000 --hist -o probs_out
# the thresholds that pileup would use, per base
modkit modbam sample-probs reads.bam --num-reads 10000

The modkit documentation shows output such as C 10 0.6972656: the 10th-percentile threshold for cytosine calls was 0.70 in that example dataset. The docs recommend letting modkit estimate the threshold, and inspecting the histograms when working with "exotic organisms or specialized assays", which describes most zoological genomes. Our reading of the shape (not a rule from the docs): a model that recognises its input puts most probabilities near 0 and near 1; a big hump in the middle means it is guessing.

The summary table

modkit modbam summary reads.bam --num-reads 20000
# only CpG positions where the read base matches the reference (needs an aligned, indexed BAM)
modkit modbam summary aligned.bam -r genome.fa --cpg --num-reads 20000

The output starts with lines beginning with # (bases with calls, reads used, modification codes, the pass threshold per base) and then a table with one row per base and code: - for unmodified, m for 5mC, h for 5hmC. pass_frac is the share of passing calls with that code; all_frac the share of all calls. Two quick checks: the codes are the ones you expected from the model name, and the m fraction is plausible for your animal and context (section 01). Without --cpg or --matched-only, modkit also counts calls on read bases that do not match the reference.

Coverage per site

modkit pileup (section 03) writes a bedMethyl row per site. Columns 10 to 18 are the QC columns:

Nvalid_cov (col. 10)
Calls that passed the threshold: modified + other modification + unmodified. The percentage in column 11 is calculated only from these. A site with 3 valid calls can only be 0, 33, 67 or 100 %.
Nfail (col. 16)
Calls below the threshold. If one region has a much higher failed share than the genome average, the model is unsure there. Repeats, intergenic regions (the DNA between genes) and regions with few CpGs are where a benchmark of nanopore callers found calls least accurate (Liu et al. 2021).
Ndiff, Ndelete (col. 17, 15)
Reads with another base or a deletion at the site. Many of them at one site point to a SNV that destroys the CpG on one parental copy, or to a reference error.
Nnocall (col. 18)
Reads with the right base but no modification call, for example because a CpG-only model sees a CG changed to CA in that read.

How much coverage is enough depends on the question. In a 2026 benchmark on human saliva (Tiras et al.), single-read estimates were unstable, at least 5× clearly improved agreement with an array reference, and 10× was often the best compromise; higher limits gave little and removed many sites. Count how many CpGs pass your minimum before choosing it.

Strand balance

A CpG reads CG on both strands, and in vertebrates its methylation is usually symmetric: if one C is methylated, so is the C on the other strand (the Jasmine documentation states this and therefore makes one call per CpG pair). Nanopore reads each come from one strand, so modkit pileup without --combine-strands gives separate + and − rows for each CpG. Two checks follow:

  • Read numbers: about half the reads at a site should come from each strand. A strong excess on one strand means a library or mapping problem in that region.
  • Percent methylated: + and − should agree within sampling noise. A consistent difference across many sites points to a model problem, not to biology; real hemi-methylation needs special (duplex) data to be shown.

Once both look fine, sum the strands (--cpg --combine-strands): this doubles the coverage per CpG. PacBio HiFi data from Jasmine already gives one call per CpG pair, so there is no strand check to make.

Controls: places where you know the answer

A quality check needs a control: a sample or region where the true methylation is known, so that the difference between measured and true is the error.

Amplified DNA
A polymerase copies DNA from ordinary nucleotides, so DNA amplified by PCR or whole-genome amplification (WGA) has no methylation. Every 5mC call in it is a false positive. Sequencing a little amplified DNA of the same animal on the same platform with the same model gives the false-positive rate directly. Simpson et al. (2017) trained one of the first nanopore 5mC callers on synthetically methylated and unmethylated DNA in the same spirit.
CpG islands
Short CpG-rich stretches, mostly at gene starts, that are predominantly unmethylated in vertebrates (Deaton & Bird 2011). The promoter islands of housekeeping genes should be near 0 % in every tissue. Restrict pileup to them with --include-bed.
Imprinted regions (mammals)
At an imprinting control region one parental copy is methylated and the other is not (Tucci et al. 2019), so unphased data give about 50 %, and reads split by haplotype (section 04) should give one copy near 100 % and the other near 0 %. Examples are the regions at H19/IGF2 and PEG10, which are methylated on one parental copy even in marsupials (Renfree et al. 2008, section 01); find their orthologs in your assembly by projection (lesson 3, section 07). Imprinting is known in marsupials and placental mammals, not in monotremes (section 01), so this control is not available in a lizard or a fish.
Synthetic standards
Oxford Nanopore published (October 2024) a validation set of synthetic DNA with known canonical, 5mC and 5hmC positions as raw signal files, to be called with Dorado and scored with modkit validate. It is a test of the model, not of your sample, but it is the public example of what a ground truth looks like.

The model is part of the measurement

A methylation value is a result of the sample and the model that called it. A Dorado model name records both parts, for example dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v5.2.0_5mCG_5hmCG@v2: pore and chemistry (r10.4.1_e8.2), speed, accuracy level (sup, the slowest and most accurate), basecall model version, then the modification model (5mC and 5hmC in CpG context) and its own version. Dorado's model list contains several versions of this one modification model, and a 2026 benchmark (Kulkarni et al.) found that tools and model versions differ, that the newest model is not always the best for CpG methylation, and that read quality and basecalling mode also change the result. Liu et al. (2021) found that part of the disagreement between nanopore and bisulfite sequencing comes from 5hmC, which bisulfite cannot tell apart from 5mC (section 02).

Practical consequences (our advice): compare samples only when they were called with the same model and version (section 06); write the model name into every table and figure legend; when a new model appears, re-call all samples or none. A modification model is trained on one platform's signal: an Oxford Nanopore model cannot be assumed to work on reads from another platform.

In our projects: the long reads of species B and C were made on a CycloneSEQ instrument with the provider's own basecaller and model, yet an Oxford Nanopore R10.4.1 Medaka model was used on them for polishing, which we have flagged as questionable. The same trap applies to methylation: whether CycloneSEQ offers 5mC calling at all we have not verified. In species A, heterozygous SNP calls seen in only one half of the reads had a high Ti/Tv; we suspect, without proof, that some are 5mC-related C→T basecalling errors. Methylation that the basecaller does not report can still show up as false variants.

Viewing reads in IGV

IGV (desktop 2.19.8; a 3.0 beta is also out) colours bases on reads that carry MM tags (the ML tag is optional). The options appear under Color by in the alignment track menu, separately for each modification type found. The display rules, from the IGV user guide:

  • Reads are grey on the forward strand and sage green on the reverse strand, and can be grouped by strand (Base Mods tab of the preferences).
  • A base is coloured only if its modification probability exceeds a threshold; with several modifications, the most likely one wins. The user guide gives 0.5 as the default and says reads are grouped by strand, but the preferences file in the IGV source code (2.19.8 and 3.0 beta 7) sets the threshold to 0 and grouping off. Check View > Preferences > Base Mods before you read colours. Transparency shows the probability from ML.
  • Mono-colour (default) colours only modified bases. Two-colour also colours likely unmodified bases (blue by default), taking their probability as one minus the sum of modification probabilities. For 5mC this gives red = methylated, blue = unmethylated, the same convention IGV uses for bisulfite data, so the two kinds of experiment look alike.
  • Since 2.17, IGV checks each MM tag against the number of matching bases in the read and hides invalid tags (a Base Mods preference, on by default, can turn the check off). Reads that suddenly show no colour may have broken tags; run check-tags.
  • IGV 2.17 and later also open bedMethyl files directly, so pileup results can sit under the reads.

What to keep from every sequencing run

Methylation can be added to old reads later only if the run's raw information still exists. Ask for these at the time of ordering, not afterwards:

  1. Raw signal files
    pod5 (or the older fast5) for nanopore; with them any future model can be run again. They are much larger than FASTQ: by our own rough estimate, roughly ten times the size of the reads. For PacBio, ask for the HiFi BAM with kinetics, or with the MM/ML tags written on the instrument; Jasmine's 5mC model needs the kinetics of both strands.
  2. Unaligned BAM, not FASTQ
    The BAM keeps MM/ML, the read group with the model names, and on request the move table (--emit-moves) that some later tools need.
  3. Model names and versions
    Basecaller name and version, basecall model, modification model. If the provider did not use a modification model, ask whether they can re-call with one.
  4. Run details
    Instrument, flow cell, library kit, read filtering, number of runs; the sequencing summary and run reports.
  5. A control, if methylation is a goal
    A little amplified DNA of the same animal on the same run, as an unmethylated control.
In our projects: the first four points are exactly what the lab asked the species A provider for after the fact (raw signal; whether a 5mC/5hmC model was used, and BAMs with MM/ML; a re-call with a 5mCG_5hmCG model and --emit-moves if not; run details and reports). The lizard sequencing sheet lists a Darevskia armeniaca PacBio HiFi run in a folder ending _met, but the read file is a FASTQ; whether it was meant as a methylation run, and whether the original BAM kept kinetics or MM/ML, we have not checked. A FASTQ cannot answer that: ask for the BAM.
Simple explanation with examples: why a methylation map needs its own quality checks

Think of a weather station that reports a temperature every minute. A number always appears, even if the thermometer sits in the sun or the battery is low. You trust the record only after checking the instrument (is it the right sensor, is it calibrated?), the readings (how jumpy are they?), and known cases (does it read 0 °C in melting ice?).

Methylation calls are the same. The model is the sensor: it must match the sequencer. The probability histogram shows how jumpy the readings are. Coverage is how many readings you average. Strand balance compares two thermometers that should agree. The controls are the melting ice: amplified DNA should read 0 % methylated, an imprinted region should read 50 % in bulk and 0 / 100 % per parental copy.
