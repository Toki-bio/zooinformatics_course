Lesson 5 · what changes when you have a whole sample

Population History from Many Genomes

Lesson 4 read history from one genome. This lesson asks what a sample of many genomes adds: genealogies for the whole sample, recent history from shared segments, selection, and the separate records of the Y chromosome and mitochondrial DNA. It also asks what SNP arrays can and cannot support, because many cohorts have arrays and not sequence.

Most methods here were built for human data, and the examples come from there. The last section reports what failed on a real array-based cohort from Asia: which outputs could not support a claim, how we found out, and what data would change that.

01From one genome to many

In lesson 4 one diploid genome gave one curve of population size through time. With many genomes you can ask more: who is related to whom, in which stretch of the chromosome, how recently, and whether a gene variant spread faster than chance allows. There are four families of methods for that. This section is a map: what each family reads, what data it needs, and a helper that lists which named tools can take the data you have. The details of each family come in the later sections.

New words for this section

Genealogy (tree)
The family tree of a set of chromosome copies at one place in the genome. Tracing two copies backwards, they meet in a common ancestor; the time of that meeting is the coalescence time (lesson 4, s01).
Recombination
The shuffling of the two parental copies when eggs and sperm are made. After a recombination the next stretch of the chromosome can have a slightly different tree, so the genealogy changes along the genome.
Ancestral recombination graph (ARG)
The trees of all stretches of the genome joined into one object. Section 02 explains it properly.
Phased data
Data in which every heterozygous site is assigned to one of the two copies of the chromosome (lesson 4, s05). Unphased data only say "this animal carries A and G here".
Linkage disequilibrium (LD)
The non-random association of alleles at two nearby sites on the same chromosome. It fades with distance, because recombination breaks the association.
Identity by descent (IBD)
A stretch of chromosome that two animals both inherited, unbroken by recombination, from one common ancestor, so the two copies are identical apart from new mutations (section 05).
Ancestral allele, polarisation
At a variable site, which of the two alleles is the old one and which is the new mutation. It is usually decided by comparing with a related species (an outgroup). Some tools need it, others do not.
SNP array
A chip that reads one fixed list of variable sites, not the whole genome. A whole-genome sequence reads all sites. What that difference does to the result is section 04.
Simple explanation with examples: why a second genome changes the question

One animal carries two copies of each chromosome, so there is one pair of copies to compare and one "time of common ancestor" per stretch of genome. Four animals carry eight copies, so there are 28 pairs, and the tree for each stretch can be drawn. Three new kinds of question become possible.

pairs 2 copies: 1 8 copies: 28 100 copies: 4,950 (n copies give n(n-1)/2 pairs) tree at each stretch of the genome, how all the copies connect shared a long stretch identical in two animals = a recent common ancestor decay how fast association between two nearby sites fades = a record of population size

Each family below reads one of these. They use the same genomes but look at different features, so they answer different questions and break in different ways. When two families agree, that is more convincing than when two tools of one family agree.

02What an ancestral recombination graph is

With many genomes we can ask more than how fast two lineages meet. We can ask who is related to whom, at every position of a chromosome, and when their ancestors lived. The answer is not one family tree but a long series of trees that change along the sequence. Joined together, these trees form an ancestral recombination graph (ARG). This section explains what that object is. The next one, ARG tools, shows how programs try to estimate it from real data.

One position, one tree

A nucleotide is inherited from exactly one parent, so each position in a sample of chromosomes has a single line of descent back through time. Follow these lines from several sampled copies and they join at common ancestors. The result is a tree, as in lesson 4, but with more than two tips. It is called the local tree of that position. The tips here are haplotypes, single copies of a chromosome, so three diploid animals give six tips.

Neighbouring positions usually travelled together through the same ancestors, so they share a tree. But at a crossover, an offspring chromosome took its left part from one parental chromosome and its right part from the other. The two sides of that point, the breakpoint, have different ancestors from there on back.

Recombination cuts one branch and moves it

The tree on the right of a breakpoint is not a new, unrelated tree. It equals the tree on the left except for one branch, which is cut off and re-attached elsewhere. Computer scientists call this a subtree prune and regraft. Three things can happen:

  • The shape changes. The branch joins a different part of the tree, so the groups of closest relatives change.
  • Only an age changes. The branch joins the same branch again, higher or lower. Shape stays, age of one junction moves.
  • Nothing changes. The lineage comes back to the same ancestor. The recombination really happened, but no tree shows it. The same is true of a breakpoint in a stretch of an ancestor that passed nothing on to the sample.
Try itWalk along a chromosome. A simulated 100 kb stretch in six haplotypes. Every history here is drawn at random from the model, and its true breakpoints are known.

What to notice. (1) Step through the breakpoints: each time, one branch moves and the rest of the tree stays. (2) The closest relative of a haplotype changes along the sequence, so "h2 is closest to h5" is true only of a stretch. (3) Root age jumps up and down although the history of the population is the same everywhere. (4) Raise the recombination rate: more trees, each covering less sequence. (5) Choose the small population: trees get shallow and there are fewer of them, because the lineages meet before many recombinations can happen.

Simple explanation with examples: why one family tree is not enough

Think of your own genome. Your pedigree has one shape, but your left half of chromosome 1 may come from your grandmother and the right half from your grandfather. Ask "who is my ancestor?" and the honest answer is "for which position?". Two cousins may share a grandmother on one stretch and share nothing recent on the next stretch.

The same is true in a population of voles. Take six chromosomes and write down their closest relatives at three places:

position 12 kb 47 kb 83 kb h1 closest h2 h2 h5 h3 closest h4 h6 h6

Each column is one local tree, seen from two tips (these rows are an illustration, not output of the simulation). Reading a table like this as "one tree for the species" would throw away most of what the genome records. Every local tree is a separate, though not independent, sample of the population's history. That is why many positions together say more than one gene tree.

Markers that do not recombine, such as mitochondrial DNA, are the exception: they have a single tree (s07).

From many trees to one graph

All these local trees can be laid on top of each other. Shared branches fall on the same line, and at each recombination one line splits in two going back in time, because one chromosome had two parents. This network is the ARG: an ancestral recombination graph encodes all local trees along the genome for a given sample. Going backwards in time, lineages merge at coalescence events and split at recombination events. Hudson described this process and Griffiths and Marjoram later gave the graph its name.

Tree sequences: the same object as two tables

Writing every local tree separately would repeat almost everything, because neighbouring trees are nearly identical. A tree sequence (or succinct tree sequence) avoids this. A node table lists each ancestor once with its age. An edge table lists each parent-child branch once, with the stretch of sequence over which that branch exists. To get the tree at a position, take the rows that cover it. The format also has tables for sites and mutations, so the variants of a data set can be kept on the trees.

The tools for this format are msprime, which simulates, and tskit, which stores and analyses. This is the same kind of toy in a few lines of Python:

import msprime
ts = msprime.sim_ancestry(samples=3, population_size=1000, sequence_length=100_000,
                          recombination_rate=1e-8, random_seed=5)
print(ts.num_trees, ts.num_nodes, ts.num_edges)   # one line of counts
for tree in ts.trees():                           # walk along the sequence
    print(tree.interval.left, tree.interval.right, tree.tmrca(0, 1))

Is a tree sequence an ARG? Words differ between authors. A full ARG holds the events themselves: every recombination and when it happened. A basic tree sequence holds their outcome, the local trees and which parts they share, and it does not say when a recombination happened. It can be extended to hold those events too. Relate gives a series of trees without shared nodes, and whether such a series is truly an ARG is, as one review puts it, a matter of definition.

One object, many questions

Once the local trees are there, questions that used to need separate methods become questions asked of one object:

Population size through time
The rate of coalescence at a time is inversely proportional to Ne at that time. So the density of coalescences in the ARG, slice by slice through time, gives Ne(t). A pair of haplotypes along the genome is the view PSMC has (lesson 4); an ARG holds this for every pair at once.
Split times
Compare coalescence within groups and between groups. Two populations separated at some time, with no gene flow since, cannot have a between-group ancestor younger than that time. Relate estimates split times this way, with cross-coalescence rates.
Selection
Lineages carrying a favoured allele coalesce unusually quickly, so the tree around that allele has a dense burst of young coalescences. Balancing selection works the other way and leaves unusually old ancestors. s06 covers these tests.
Read itAsk the same history two questions. This uses the history chosen above, with all 15 pairs of haplotypes at all positions. Change the history in the first figure and this one follows.

What to notice. (1) In "Two populations", no purple bar lies to the left of the dashed line: the youngest between-group age is always older than the true split, usually by a few hundred generations. In the other histories the two colours overlap, because the groups mean nothing. (2) Press New history several times: the estimate of N swings widely, because 100 kb holds few independent trees. A genome has far more. (3) The pale red band in the first figure marks the lowest stretch of mean pair age. There is one in every history, including those with no selection at all. A dip alone is not a sweep: a scan must ask how unusual it is against the rest of the genome.

Nobody observes an ARG. Everything above used a known history. Real data give only the variants, and they say little about any one tree. In humans the mutation and recombination rates are similar, so on average each mutation that could inform a tree comes with a recombination that changes it. Of the recombination events that change the graph, only a small proportion can be detected from data, and even then their timing and breakpoints are approximate. A count of recombinations made from sequences is often far below the true number. An ARG in a file is therefore an estimate. Treating one inferred ARG as the truth gives false certainty, and it can be biased if the true history differs from what the method assumed. s03 compares the tools with this in mind.

03ARG tools: Relate, tsinfer, ARG-Needle, SINGER, ARGweaver

An ARG (s02) cannot be observed. It has to be inferred, and five programs do this in very different ways. This section says what each one reads, whether it needs phased haplotypes and known ancestral alleles, how many genomes it can take, and what comes out. The largest difference is at the end: some tools return one best answer, and others return a set of answers whose spread shows what the data cannot decide.

New words for this section

Phased
Each of an animal's two chromosome copies is known along its length (0|1 in a VCF, not 0/1; see lesson 4). All five programs work on single chromosome copies, called haplotypes. Only ARGweaver can work without phase, by averaging over the possibilities.
Ancestral allele
The base the common ancestor carried at a variable site. The other allele is derived. Deciding which is which is called polarising a site. It usually needs an estimated ancestral genome or one or more outgroup species.
Genetic map
A table of how recombination rate varies along a chromosome, usually in centimorgans (cM). 1 cM is a 1 % chance of a crossover per generation.
Tree sequence
A compact file of all local trees in which shared nodes and branches are stored once. It is the .trees format of the tskit library.
Threading
Adding one more haplotype to a partly built ARG by deciding, along the genome, where its lineage joins the existing trees. ARG-Needle, SINGER and ARGweaver all build ARGs this way.
Point estimate
One answer: one tree, one age. Its opposite here is a posterior sample: many answers drawn with a probability that reflects how well each fits the data and the model. Drawing them step by step is called MCMC (Markov chain Monte Carlo).

Simple explanation with examples: two ways to build an ARG

Copying. Relate and tsinfer ask, for each haplotype: "which others does this one look like here?" They use the Li and Stephens model, which describes a haplotype as a mosaic copied from other haplotypes, and a change of source as a recombination. Relate turns the copying into a tree at each position. tsinfer first builds likely ancestral haplotypes, then lets each one copy from older ones. Neither step dates anything: times are estimated afterwards from the mutations on each branch.

Threading. ARG-Needle, SINGER and ARGweaver start from a small ARG and add haplotypes one at a time, like adding a new specimen to a pedigree you have already drawn:

haplotype e, walking along the chromosome: positions 1-40 kb joins the branch above b, about 3,000 generations ago positions 40-55 kb joins the branch above d, about 9,000 generations ago <- a recombination at 40 kb positions 55-90 kb joins the branch above b again

ARG-Needle takes one good choice at each place and moves on. SINGER and ARGweaver draw the choice at random according to its probability, then remove pieces of the ARG and thread them again many times. That repetition is the MCMC, and it is why they are slower.

Relate

Relate (Speidel et al. 2019) first builds a tree at each position from the copying probabilities, then maps mutations onto the branches and estimates branch lengths with MCMC. Its add-on script alternates between estimating population size and re-estimating branch lengths, so size history comes out of the same run. Averaging coalescence rates within and between groups gives each population's size and cross-coalescence rate (the same idea as in lesson 4), and a further module tests for selection (s06). The authors expect it to "work equally well in other recombining species". Ancestral alleles must be set before the run, but a wrong one is not fatal: if a SNP fits the tree only with its alleles swapped, Relate flips it and records this in the is_flipped column of .mut.

# Relate v1.2.4. Filenames are given without extensions where the docs say so.
PATH_TO_RELATE/bin/RelateFileFormats --mode ConvertFromVcf \
    --haps data.haps --sample data.sample -i data_phased

# keep biallelic SNPs, flip so that 0 = ancestral, apply a mask of reliable bases
PATH_TO_RELATE/scripts/PrepareInputFiles/PrepareInputFiles.sh \
    --haps data.haps --sample data.sample \
    --ancestor ancestor.fa --mask mask.fa -o input

# -N is the size in HAPLOTYPES: twice the number of individuals
PATH_TO_RELATE/bin/Relate --mode All -m 1.25e-8 -N 30000 \
    --haps input.haps --sample input.sample --map genetic_map.txt \
    --dist input.dist --seed 1 -o run            # run.anc, run.mut

# size history: re-estimates branch lengths too. Set --years_per_gen: the default is 28, a human value
PATH_TO_RELATE/scripts/EstimatePopulationSize/EstimatePopulationSize.sh \
    -i run -m 1.25e-8 --poplabels data.poplabels --years_per_gen 1 --seed 1 -o popsize

# sampled branch lengths for one region; the topology stays fixed
PATH_TO_RELATE/scripts/SampleBranchLengths/SampleBranchLengths.sh \
    -i popsize -o region -m 1.25e-8 --coal popsize.coal --format a \
    --num_samples 100 --first_bp 100000 --last_bp 200000 --seed 1

The mutation rate here is the human value from the docs' example; use your species' rate per base per generation (lesson 4). In .mut, is_not_mapping = 1 means the SNP "could not be mapped to a unique branch". In the 1000 Genomes run, 86 % of SNPs mapped uniquely. RelateFileFormats --mode ConvertToTreeSequence writes a tskit file, but the docs warn that this gives no compression and loses how long branches persist.

tsinfer and tsdate

tsinfer (Kelleher et al. 2019) infers ancestral haplotypes from shared mutations, ordering them by allele frequency, and then finds a copying path for each ancestor and each sample. Its docs are clear about what comes out: "Internal node times are allele frequencies, not years or generations", and branch-length statistics raise an error until you date the result. tsdate (Wohns et al. 2022) does this from the mutations on each branch and works on any valid tree sequence. Its default method needs a mutation rate but no population size.

Where the data cannot settle a node, tsinfer leaves a polytomy: a node with more than two children. Its authors describe polytomies as a way of showing uncertainty, and the toy below shows why an honest "cannot say" can be worth more than a forced choice. tsinfer needs a known ancestral state for each site it infers from. Sites without one, as well as singletons and multiallelic sites, stay in the output with mutations placed by parsimony. Performance is "degraded" by genotyping error and "in particular" by phasing error.

# tsinfer 0.5.1, tsdate 0.2 (Python). Shell first: VCF to VCF Zarr. AA in the VCF becomes variant_AA.
#   vcf2zarr convert data.vcf.gz data.vcz
import tsinfer, tsdate

vdata = tsinfer.VariantData("data.vcz", ancestral_state="variant_AA")
ts = tsinfer.infer(vdata, num_threads=4)      # topology only

ts = tsdate.preprocess_ts(ts)                 # advised for real data
dated = tsdate.date(ts, mutation_rate=1e-8)   # per base per generation
dated.dump("data.dated.trees")

This is the interface of the released version. The development version (0.5.2) moves to a tsinfer run config.toml command, so check which version you installed. The tsinfer docs ran 5,000 simulated diploids in about 18 minutes and about 600 MiB. A "tsinfer-sparse" variant for array data appears in the ARG-Needle paper and is named, with ARG-Needle, as array-capable in the published review of Nielsen, Vaughn & Deng; its earlier author manuscript names only ARG-Needle. We could not find the variant in the tsinfer documentation (s04).

ARG-Needle, and what ASMC does in it

ARG-Needle (Zhang et al. 2023) was built for very large samples, including array data. Each new haplotype goes through three steps:

  1. Hashing. For each stretch of genome, find a short list of candidate close relatives by hashing genotypes. This avoids comparing it with everyone.
  2. ASMC. Estimate the age of the common ancestor (TMRCA) between the new haplotype and each candidate, along the genome.
  3. Threading. Attach the new haplotype to the closest candidate at each position, at the age ASMC gave.

ASMC (Palamara et al. 2018) is a hidden Markov model related to PSMC (lesson 4), but its input is a pair of haplotypes and it reports the TMRCA along the genome rather than a size history. It was designed so that the sparse, non-random SNPs of an array can be used: it models how those SNPs were chosen, which is the "A" (ascertained) in its name. So in ARG-Needle, ASMC supplies all the time information and the hashing only decides whom to compare. Three consequences follow from this.

  • The 300 floor. ASMC's model "requires at least 300 haploid samples", or 150 diploids. With fewer you get the CSFS requires 300, where CSFS is the conditional site frequency spectrum.
  • A built-in demography. ASMC needs a precomputed .decodingQuantities.gz file holding the demographic model, mutation rate and allele frequencies, and the final ARG normalization (which adjusts node times to match a stated size history) uses a demography too. The default for both is a European human model. For another species you must supply your own, computed with the PrepareDecoding tool from a size history you already have, for example from PSMC. Population sizes in these files are haploid.
  • ASMC-clust. The same package can run ASMC on all pairs and cluster the pairwise ages into a tree at each site (--asmc_clust 1). This scales with the square of the sample size, so ARG-Needle itself is the one for big samples.
# arg-needle 1.1.0 (pip install arg-needle). Requires 300 or more haplotypes.
arg_needle --hap_gz data.haps.gz --map data.map --mode sequence \
    --asmc_decoding_file my.decodingQuantities.gz \
    --normalize_demography my.demo \
    --out data                                   # data.argn

arg2tskit --arg_path data.argn --ts_path data.trees

The map has four columns (chromosome, SNP name, genetic position in cM, physical position in bp), and both positions must increase strictly. The manual warns that the parameters were "optimized for use with SNP data, so it may be slow when applied to sequencing data". ASMC's paper adds that pairs across individuals need "haplotypes phased with extremely high accuracy". The SINGER paper notes that ARG-Needle "does not map mutations to branches"; arg-needle-lib has its own functions for mutations on an ARG. Array data get their own section (s04).

SINGER

SINGER (Deng, Nielsen & Song 2025) threads haplotypes in two cheaper steps, first which branch and then what time, and explores the space of ARGs with MCMC that changes both topology and times. Its HMMs assume a constant size, but a rescaling step then stretches and shrinks time so that mutation counts match branch lengths. In the authors' simulations this made it robust to size changes, and it needs no size history from you. The limits come from the paper and README: phased, contemporary, whole-genome data; polymorphic sites with missing data are excluded; and the model assumes infinite sites, "which may not be applicable to cross-species data". The authors say directly that good phasing is "often challenging, especially for ancient DNA and non-model organisms".

# SINGER beta. -vcf and -output take PREFIXES: -vcf chr1 reads chr1.vcf (the most common bug, says the README).
# Choose m first, then Ne from pi = 4 * Ne * m. Here -Ne is DIPLOID. Use -polar 0.99 for polarised data.
singer_master -m 1.25e-8 -Ne 10000 -ratio 1 -polar 0.99 \
    -vcf chr1 -output chr1_w0 -start 0 -end 5e6 -n 100 -thin 20

# long chromosomes: run windows of about 5 Mb, then join with merge_ARG.py
python merge_ARG.py --file_table sub_file_table --output merged_ARG

# each sampled ARG is 4 text files (nodes, branches, muts, recombs): make .trees files
convert_to_tskit -input chr1_w0 -output chr1_w0 -start 0 -end 100 -step 1

# check convergence before trusting anything
python compute_traces.py -prefix chr1_w0 -m 1.25e-8 \
    -start_index 0 -end_index 100 -output_filename chr1_w0.traces

Defaults from the README: -n 100 samples, -thin 20 iterations between them, -ratio (recombination over mutation rate) 1, -polar 0.5 for unpolarised data. The README advises: "only using one single sample is NOT ideal". Regions with little data, such as centromeres, cause the second most common failure. Skip them: windows need not be adjacent. Branch lengths are in generations. The software still calls itself a beta version.

ARGweaver and ARGweaver-D

ARGweaver (Rasmussen et al. 2014) showed that sampling ARGs at genome scale is possible. It works in fixed, discrete time steps, and its sites model allows the same site to mutate more than once (Jukes-Cantor). The manual asks for no ancestral allele. It accepts unphased data through --unphased, which "will integrate over phasings"; the SINGER README agrees that ARGweaver "has better support" for missing and unphased data. ARGweaver-D (Hubisz et al. 2020) adds a user-defined demographic model with population splits and migration, and can give the probability that a region was introgressed. Its manual was last updated in 2013, and the last commit is from 2023.

# ARGweaver (README example values, human-like). -c 10 compresses the alignment into 10-bp blocks.
# -N is diploid. -n is iterations (default 1000); a sample is written every 10 (--sample-step).
arg-sample -s data.sites -N 10000 -r 1.6e-8 -m 1.8e-8 \
    --ntimes 20 --maxtime 200e3 -c 10 -n 100 -o run/out

# TMRCA along the region, across the sampled ARGs
arg-extract-tmrca run/out.%d.smc.gz > run.tmrca.txt

Time spent before the chain settles must be discarded (burn-in). The ARGweaver paper used 500 burn-in iterations in simulations, then 1,000 sampling iterations keeping every tenth, and for its 54 human genomes 2,000 iterations per block of about 2 Mb. Those figures are one study's settings, not defaults.

One best tree or a set of trees

Why would a best tree not be enough? Nielsen and colleagues give the reason: in humans the mutation and recombination rates are "fairly similar, meaning that, on average, every time a new mutation occurs that might provide information about the tree structure, a new recombination event will also occur". So any one local tree is supported by very few SNPs. A method that returns one tree still draws it just as cleanly. Treating that tree as true, the review warns, induces "false certainty in the results of any analysis".

The tools fall into three groups (Table 1 of the review):

  • One ARG: tsinfer and ARG-Needle. tsdate does add a variance to each node time, but on a fixed topology.
  • One topology, sampled times: Relate, through SampleBranchLengths.
  • Sampled topologies and times: SINGER and ARGweaver.

The difference shows in a measurable way. If a 90 % interval is honest, it should contain the truth 90 % of the time. On simulations in the SINGER paper, it did so 85 % of the time for SINGER, 54 % for ARGweaver and 44 % for Relate, for pairwise coalescence times. The authors attribute Relate's low figure to sampling times under a fixed topology. That is the authors' own benchmark, and the review, which shares authors with it, also places SINGER first on accuracy. Sampling is not free either: the sampled tools are limited to hundreds of genomes at most.

Which one to try

Our reading of the documents above, not a rule from any of them.

  • Tens of phased genomes and a question about one region: SINGER, because you need the intervals.
  • Few genomes without reliable phase, or a known demographic model and a question about introgression: ARGweaver or ARGweaver-D, on short regions.
  • Hundreds to a few thousand phased genomes and a question about size, splits or selection: Relate. Add SampleBranchLengths for loci you plan to interpret.
  • Very large samples, or one compact file for many analyses: tsinfer + tsdate.
  • Array data or biobank scale, with at least 300 haplotypes: ARG-Needle, after reading s04.

One or two genomes per species belong with the methods of lesson 4. What these tools then give you is the subject of the next sections: s05 for recent shared segments, s06 for selection, and s08 for what happened when a real cohort was run through this kind of pipeline.

04What SNP arrays can and cannot do

Many large cohorts were not sequenced. They were genotyped on a SNP array (also called a SNP chip): a slide that reads a fixed list of a few hundred thousand to a few million positions where people are already known to differ. That is cheap and fast, and it is enough for many questions. But the list was chosen in advance, and the choice leaves a mark on every number computed from it. This section shows the mark with a toy, explains why the methods of lesson 4 cannot be run on array data, and names the four methods that were built for it.

An array reads a chosen list, not the genome

SNP
A single position where individuals carry different letters, for example A in some and G in others. Each version is an allele.
Minor allele frequency (MAF)
The share of chromosomes that carry the rarer of the two alleles. A variant seen once among 100 chromosomes has a MAF of 1 %.
Ascertainment
The way the sites on the list were found and chosen. Typically a small group of individuals was sequenced first (the discovery panel), and variants seen there, often those common enough to be useful, went onto the array. Albrechtsen et al. (2010) write that SNP chip data suffer "from ascertainment biases caused by the SNP discovery process in which a small number of individuals from selected populations are used as discovery panels", and that these biases "will distort measures of human diversity" (abstract only; see notes).

A variant that is rare, or that exists only in a population the panel did not include, is unlikely to be on the list. So the array sees a sample of variation that is tilted toward common variants. This is our inference from the cause the papers state; the exact shape depends on the chip. Everything computed from allele frequencies then shifts: diversity, Tajima's D, the shape of the frequency spectrum.

Simple explanation with examples: why choosing the sites in advance changes the answer

Imagine counting how many bird species live in a forest, but only from a checklist written by someone who walked one path on one day. The list holds every bird that is common on that path. Your count will show many common birds, almost no rare ones, and nothing that lives only off the path. The forest is the same; the checklist decides what you can see.

A SNP array is that checklist. Most variable positions in a population are rare: in the toy below, with constant population size, about one in five variable positions has its rarer allele on only one chromosome out of a hundred, and about one in three on three or fewer. A chip made from a small panel contains few of them. The people on the chip look more similar in their rare variants than they are, and a population history read from "how many rare variants are there" will be wrong. In the toy, a chip with a MAF cut-off of 5 % keeps about six in ten of all variable sites but none of the rarest ones, and the sites it keeps tell a different story from the whole.

Quick settings

Why the PSMC family is blocked on arrays

The input of PSMC is described in lesson 4, s02: for every 100-base bin of the genome, whether it contains a heterozygous site. The PSMC README puts it as "the i-th character in the output sequence indicates whether there is at least one heterozygote in the bin". The method reads history from the gaps between differences, so every position must have been looked at. An array looks at a short list chosen in advance, with long stretches between probes that nobody read. A bin with no heterozygous probe may mean "no difference" or "not looked at", and the list itself is biased toward common variants. Our conclusion from these facts, not a statement from the tool's manual: PSMC and the other single-genome methods of lesson 4 need sequence-level data, and an array cannot be fed to them as a cheaper version. They are blocked, not merely less accurate.

What was built for arrays is a small group of methods that model the ascertainment, or do not need the rare variants, or look at something other than variant density.

The four, one by one

ASMC
A hidden Markov model of the kind used in PSMC, but run on pairs of chromosomes and made fast. Its authors say it "can operate either on pairs of unphased chromosomes within a single diploid individual, or on pairs of phased chromosomes across individuals", and that the cross-individual mode "requires the availability of phased haplotypes, which may not be easy to obtain" (Palamara et al. 2018). ARG-Needle
Builds the genealogy by threading haplotypes into it, with ASMC deciding where to attach them ("ASMC is run over pairs of samples"). tsinfer, sparse mode
tsinfer chooses a subset of sites for inference, "excluding by default any sites that are fixed, singletons, or where the ancestral state is unknown" (tsinfer manual), and needs the ancestral allele at every site it uses. We could not verify the details of the sparse mode in the manual; the review calls it "a variation of tsinfer".
GONE
Does not build a genealogy. It models how fast linkage disequilibrium falls with the genetic distance between two sites, which depends on the effective population size in each generation back, and uses a genetic algorithm to find the history that best explains the observed LD. Santiago et al. (2020): the method works "with the highest resolution within a hundred generations before sampling" and "can be applied from large samples to samples of fewer than ten individuals". The repository changelog gives limits of 100,000 SNPs per chromosome and 1,800 individuals. Because it reads recent history from LD, not from counts of rare variants, the rare-variant loss matters less to it than to a method that counts variants. That last point is our inference: we did not find ascertainment discussed in the part of the paper we read.
What can go wrong with GONE. It assumes a population that stayed isolated for a while. Novo et al. (2023, Genet Sel Evol 55:86) tested it by simulation and found that its estimates "may be substantially biased when there has been a recent mixture of populations that were previously separated for a long period of time", and also under low continued migration or frequent chromosomal inversions (abstract only). A cohort made of several regions and ancestries is the case that paper warns about. Run a population-structure analysis first and give GONE homogeneous groups.

What an array cannot do, in practice

  • Heterozygosity, nucleotide diversity, Tajima's D and the frequency spectrum computed straight from array genotypes describe the chip as much as the population (the toy above).
  • Rare variants. They are not on the list. Only methods that infer them from linkage (ARG-Needle) bring some back.
  • Variants private to a population that the discovery panel did not include.
  • Dating from branch lengths of Y chromosome or mitochondrial DNA from chip probes alone; see s07.
In one of our projects, data from an Asian human cohort came from an array, so only these methods were options at first; the questions about population size and split times wait for whole genomes (s08).

05Recent history from IBD segments

Lesson 4 read deep history from one genome. For the last few dozen generations that signal is too faint, but with many genomes there is a sharper one: stretches of chromosome that two animals share because both inherited them, unbroken, from a recent common ancestor. Such a stretch is an IBD segment. Every generation back to the ancestor gives recombination one more chance to cut the stretch, so long segments mean a recent ancestor and short ones an old ancestor. Counting the segments of each length in a whole sample therefore records the population size over roughly the last 100 generations (the method is IBDNe, part 3 below).

Words used here. A haplotype is one chromosome copy. cM (centimorgan) is the map length that counts here, not megabases. A meiosis is one parent-to-child step. Ne is the effective population size of lesson 4.

1. Segment length tells the age of the ancestor

If two haplotypes share a common ancestor g generations back, there are g meioses on the path down each side, so 2g chances to break the stretch. Browning and Browning (2015) assume crossovers at each meiosis with rate 1/100 per cM (Haldane's model). The lengths of segments listed from an analysis are then exponentially distributed with rate 2g/100 per cM:

expected IBD length = 100 / (2g) cM = 50 / g cM

This is the same rule as the ROH age table in lesson 3, section 9, where it dates runs inside one animal. Here the two copies belong to two animals, but the path is the same: 2g meioses. Two warnings, both shown by the toy below. First, one segment varies as much as the mean (for an exponential the standard deviation equals the mean), so you cannot date one segment, only a whole distribution. Second, which segments you look at matters. The paper states that segments picked from a list have the mean above, but segments picked because they cover one chosen position are longer on average, because long segments are more likely to cover it. For that case the paper gives the length as Erlang (an exponential on each side of the position, rate g/50 each), whose mean is 100/g cM, twice as long.

Simple explanation with examples: why a recent ancestor leaves long pieces

Think of the chromosome as a long ribbon copied from parent to child. At every hand-over a few random cuts are made and pieces from the two parental ribbons are re-glued. Two cousins may both hold a long piece of the same great-grandparent ribbon if only a few hand-overs happened. After thirty hand-overs on each side the ribbon has been cut so often that only short scraps are still identical.

Rule of thumb from the formula: 5 generations back gives pieces of about 10 cM; 50 generations back gives pieces of about 1 cM. IBD tools ask for a genetic map in cM because the cM-to-megabase ratio differs between species and along chromosomes.

2. A population of size Ne gives a whole mixture of ages

Two haplotypes drawn from a population do not have one ancestor age. In a population of constant size, the chance that they find their common ancestor in any one generation back is about 1/(2Ne), so a recent ancestor is likely only when Ne is small. The IBDNe paper writes the chance of a common ancestor exactly g generations back as P(g) = 1/(2N[g]) × the product of (1 − 1/(2N[g'])) over the earlier generations g'. Each generation may have its own N, and this is what lets the method follow a changing size. The expected number of segments of a given length is the sum over generations of those exponentials. Small Ne gives many long segments; large Ne gives few, almost all short.

4. The assumptions, in plain words

  • One randomly mating population. The estimated N stands for an idealised random-mating population (the paper's definition of effective size). A sample from several groups, or an admixed or structured one, breaks the model: pairs from different groups share ancestors much further back than pairs inside one group. The paper does not recommend its IBD detector IBDseq for samples with several continental ancestries, including admixed populations, and says haplotype-based detectors such as Refined IBD are robust to admixture (“data not shown”). That concerns finding segments; it does not make a single Ne(t) curve valid for a mixed sample.
  • A random sample of the population. The paper says the method appears robust to small deviations; close relatives are removed by default (filtersamples).
  • Accurate phase and a map in cM. The hap-ibd paper notes that haplotype-based methods can break one long segment into shorter ones when phasing errors fall inside it, which removes long segments and shifts the curve.
  • Recent time only. With the default length cut IBDNe reaches about 300 generations back. Older history belongs to the methods of lesson 4; they are not repeated here.

5. Why imputed haplotypes can inflate IBD

When genotypes are imputed from a reference panel, each animal's haplotype is built as a patchwork of stretches copied from panel haplotypes. Two animals imputed from the same panel can receive the same panel stretch, not because they share a recent ancestor but because both were filled in from it. Such stretches are identical by construction and look like IBD. This is our inference from how imputation works: we did not find a published source that states it for IBD counts (see notes), so test it on your data instead of assuming it.

The test is simple. Compare the average number of segments per pair of haplotypes with what a model like the toy in part 2 predicts for a population of that size. If the observed count is many times higher, the data contain segments the population does not.

In our projects. On array data from an Asian human cohort, an IBD run on the imputed genotypes gave many times more segments per pair than the size of the population allowed, and we discarded it. The same analysis on only the SNPs that were actually typed gave a plausible count, though the later Ne(t) fit still failed (part 6). Numbers are left out of this page on purpose.

6. How to read the fit checks

IBDNe gives you the .ne file with 95 % bootstrap intervals (bootstrapped over chromosomes, 80 times by default), the .boot file with every bootstrap curve, and the exclusion files. We found no single pass-or-fail fit number in the program page or the paper. The checks below are the ones the paper's text and figures support, plus one of our own:

  1. Do the bootstrap curves agree? Wide or crossing bands mean the data do not pin the curve down.
  2. Is the curve smooth? The paper notes that the estimate can oscillate around the true value, and that inferring changes in growth rate could be dangerous because they may reflect artefactual oscillation. Do not read single wiggles.
  3. Is it biologically possible? A fall of several orders of magnitude in ten generations is not a demographic history. This is our judgement, not a rule from the papers.
  4. Segments per pair and the log-histogram of lengths (our check, not an IBDNe output): compare observed counts with those expected from the estimated N. They should agree across the length range with no systematic bend.

06Selection from genealogies

A genealogy along the chromosome (s02) is also a record of selection. Where a favoured variant spread quickly, the local trees of that population are unusually shallow: its copies found a common ancestor recently. This section shows how to read that shape, how one published scan turns it into a number, how a per-mutation test works, and why a peak in such a scan is a place to look and not yet a finding.

What a sweep does to a local tree

Selective sweep
A favoured new variant spreads until the whole population carries it. Schweinsberg & Durrett (2005): "When a beneficial mutation occurs in a population, the new, favored allele may spread to the entire population. This process is known as a selective sweep."
Hitchhiking
Neutral sites near the favoured variant are carried along with it, because they sit on the same piece of chromosome. The term goes back to Maynard Smith & Haigh (1974), as cited in the paper above.
Escape
A lineage at a neutral site escapes when recombination during the sweep moved it off the chromosome that carried the favoured variant. It then traces back to a different ancestor.
Selection coefficient (s)
The fitness advantage of the favoured variant: with s = 0.02, its carriers leave about 2 % more offspring.

Go backwards in time at a neutral site close to the favoured variant. Every sampled lineage that did not escape runs back into the one chromosome where the mutation arose, so they all coalesce within the short time the sweep took. The local tree has many lineages joining at nearly one date, and that date is recent. Far from the variant, lineages escape and the tree is ordinary. Hejase et al. (2022) describe what they saw in inferred trees of human loci as "a burst of recent coalescence among the derived lineages", and in southern capuchino seedeaters (genus Sporophila) as "a burst of coalescence events, corresponding to unusually large and young clades".

How far the effect reaches can be estimated with a simple rule. Schweinsberg & Durrett treat each lineage as a coin: it is caught by the sweep with chance p, independently of the others.

p (caught by the sweep) = exp( − r × ln(2N) / s ) r = chance of recombination between the two sites, per generation

Stronger selection gives recombination less time to act, so the affected stretch is wider. With a recombination rate of 10−8 per base per generation and N = 5,000, half of the lineages are caught 150 kb from the variant at s = 0.02, and 750 kb away at s = 0.1 (our arithmetic). Treat it as a guide only: the authors themselves say that this first approximation "is not very accurate for realistic values of N". We use it because it is short enough to read.

Simple explanation with examples: one mutation, one shallow tree

Imagine a vole population and a new variant that protects against a parasite. After many generations nearly every vole carries it, and all those copies descend from the one vole in which it arose. A site right beside the variant came along with it, so at that site all voles share that one ancestor, a short time ago. A site far along the chromosome has had many chances to recombine onto other backgrounds, so its lineages go their separate ways into the deep past.

far from the variant beside the variant (ordinary tree) (shallow, many lineages meet at once) ┌────┴─────┐ ┌──┴──┐ ┌──┴──┐ │ ┌─┴─┐ │ ┌─┴─┐ ┌─┬─┬─┬─┬─┬─┐ recent a b c d e f a b c d e f g

A tree cannot be seen directly: it is inferred from the data (s03). And "shallow" has to be judged against something, which is what the next part is about.

From tree shape to a number: Tpooled / Twithin

Deng, Nielsen & Song (2025), in the SINGER paper, ran this scan on 200 genomes of the 1000 Genomes Project: five African populations (GWD, YRI, ESN, LWK, MSL) with 40 genomes each. For every 1-kb window they took two averages from the inferred genealogies:

Twithin
The average pairwise coalescence time among genomes of one population. Two copies with a coalescence time T differ by about 2μT per base, so this is a diversity measure read from trees (lesson 4, s01).
Tpooled
The same average over all pairs in the combined sample of all five populations.
scan value of a window for one population = Tpooled / Twithin high value = this population is unusually shallow here

In their words, they "computed the ratio of the ARG-based diversity estimate for the combined sample to that for each of the five populations; reductions in local diversity would show up as peaks when these ratios are plotted along the genome". The ratio goes this way round, pooled over within, so a drop in one population appears as a peak. It sits above 1 even in ordinary windows, because the pooled sample contains pairs from different populations, which coalesce later. Their line for a peak is "the genome-wide 99.99% quantile" of the ratio (Figure 5 legend). A second panel of that figure shows Twithin of each population around each peak, and that panel matters, as the toy will show.

Why use trees for this, and not count differences in the sequence? Because a 1-kb window holds very few variants: the authors note that "SNP-based diversity can be noisy at fine scales", and report that SINGER gave more accurate fine-scale diversity than Relate and tsinfer + tsdate. Inference errors do not vanish. SINGER samples many genealogies, and even so, in their simulations its 90 % credible interval covered the true pairwise coalescence time in 85 % of cases (Relate 44 %, ARGweaver 54 %).

Among the peaks they highlight MITF (reduced diversity in GWD; related to skin), SPCS3 (reduced in YRI; an immune-related protein believed to affect flavivirus production) and SCN9A (a sodium channel involved in pain perception). Then comes the sentence to keep:

The authors' own caution. "These examples illustrate the utility of SINGER for exploratory evolutionary analysis, but additional studies are needed to investigate if, and how, selection is acting in these loci."

  • A quantile is not a test. The line cuts off a fixed share of windows, whatever happened in the genome. A human genome holds about 3 million 1-kb windows, so a 99.99 % line passes about 300 of them per population even if none was selected (our arithmetic). The published list is a list of the most extreme windows.
  • Age matters. The caught lineages meet at the date of the sweep, so the older the sweep, the deeper the tree. Once that date is not far from ordinary coalescence times, nothing stands out. In the toy a sweep 3,500 generations old is almost invisible at this line.
  • The top window is not the target. In the toy with s = 0.1 the highest window lay a median of about 90 kb from the true site, because the whole plateau is equally shallow. A peak gives a region, and the gene names in a figure are the genes that overlap that region.
  • A ratio can rise without any sweep. Where every population has a small local size, Twithin falls further than Tpooled, because the pooled pairs still have to wait for the population split. The ratio rises in all populations at once, which is why the per-population Twithin panel is needed. In the toy this effect is weaker than a sweep: with the local size cut by 85 % at the centre, no excess shows at the 99.99 % line, but at the 99 % line the central 300 kb has about five times as many windows above the line as chance predicts, in all three populations alike. This is our reading of the toy, not a claim of the paper. Something of this kind can happen in real genomes: background selection, the loss of neutral variation through "selection against the steady input of deleterious mutations" (Stephan 2019), is called "pervasive" in the SINGER paper, and Stephan writes that such processes "reduce levels of genetic diversity and are hard to distinguish".

Testing one mutation: the Relate selection test

The scan above asks about windows. Speidel et al. (2019) ask about a single mutation, using the tree at that site: did the lineage carrying the mutation spread faster than the lineages competing with it? Their test is "based on estimating the speed of spread of a particular lineage (carrying a particular mutation), relative to other 'competing' lineages, over some chosen time range". To test the whole lifetime of a mutation they "condition on the number of lineages present when it first arises, and use as a test statistic the number of present-day carriers". This gives a real p-value, not a quantile, with stated limits: the null distribution holds "assuming no population stratification" (no hidden substructure in the sample), and the authors report reduced power at rarer mutations. In 20 human populations of the 1000 Genomes Project they found 35 stringent signals (p < 5 × 10−8 in each of three or more groups), including the LCT region in Europeans, and they warn that "interpretation of our findings regarding natural selection requires some care".

The test ships with Relate as a module. This is the example from the manual (PATH_TO_RELATE is your installation; -m is the mutation rate):

The column when_mutation_has_freq2 holds the value for the lifetime of the mutation. Going further, CLUES (Stern et al. 2019) estimates a selection coefficient and a frequency curve through time from local trees; the SINGER paper notes that it "performs better with a sample of local trees", which is one reason for sampling trees at all.

07Y chromosome and mitochondrial DNA as records

Two pieces of the genome are not shuffled between generations. The mitochondrial genome travels from mother to child, and the Y chromosome from father to son. Each therefore carries a single family tree of the whole population, and a view of the past that belongs to one sex only. Comparing the two, or either with the rest of the genome, is the classical way to ask whether females and males had different histories.

One block, one tree

mtDNA
The mitogenome is passed on through eggs. Every child of a mother carries her mtDNA, but only her daughters pass it on. It has no partner to swap pieces with (lesson 2, section 11 describes how to assemble it and why its nuclear copies, NUMTs, are a trap).
Y chromosome
The male-specific part of the Y is passed from father to son only. In mammals it does not recombine with the X over most of its length (lesson 2, section 12 explains how sex chromosomes look in an assembly). In birds and other species with ZW sex determination the mother-to-daughter W chromosome plays the Y's role; the Z and W are reversed relative to X and Y, but the idea is the same (Wilson Sayres 2018).
One tree each
Because nothing is shuffled, every position in the block shares one genealogy. Mutations that arrive on a branch stay together, which is what makes haplogroups possible: a haplogroup is a named branch of that one tree, defined by the mutations on it.
A single draw
One tree is one random outcome of the family-tree process. The autosomes hold thousands of independent trees. A Y or mtDNA result is therefore one noisy sample of history, however many bases were read. Wilson Sayres (2018) puts it the same way: each of these regions "adds only a single linked marker to the analysis".

Simple explanation with examples: why a haplogroup is a branch, not a label

Take a grandmother whose mtDNA has one new mutation, call it M1. All her daughters and granddaughters through daughters carry M1, and so do her sons, but her sons' children do not. Years later a great-granddaughter picks up a second mutation, M2. Now the family has two nested groups:

grandmother carries M1 ├─ daughter A carries M1 │ └─ granddaughter carries M1 + M2 └─ daughter B carries M1

"Has M1" names the larger group, "has M1 and M2" a smaller group inside it. A haplogroup name is the shorthand for such a nested group. Deeper names mean more mutations were needed to place the person, so a call that rests on one last mutation is more fragile than the broad group above it.

Effective size: why each marker is small

The effective population size (Ne) says how fast diversity is lost by chance. Under the textbook assumptions, the autosomes, the X, the Y and mtDNA differ only in how many copies of each exist in the population. Wilson Sayres (2018) states the assumptions: constant population size, random mating, equal contributions of the two sexes (the effective number of males equals that of females) and a Poisson distribution of offspring number. Under them, X diversity is expected to be three-quarters of autosomal diversity and Y and mtDNA one-quarter, "because there are three X chromosomes for every four autosomes" and one Y or mtDNA copy for every four (Wilson Sayres et al., on human Y diversity).

Most real populations break the equal-sex assumption. A few males may father most offspring while many females breed. When the numbers of breeding males (Nm) and females (Nf) differ, the autosomal effective size is, as given in a university population-genetics lecture (Carr, Memorial University):

Ne = 4 Nm Nf / (Nm + Nf)

With equal numbers this gives Nm + Nf, the total. With one male and many females Ne approaches 4, however many females there are. Here Nm and Nf are the numbers that actually leave offspring, not the census counts; unequal success among breeders of the same sex lowers them further. The same lecture derives the formula assuming a Poisson-like spread of offspring within each sex; it does not attribute it to Wright (1931), whose paper we did not open.

Our derivation, which is not from a source: count gene copies. An autosome has two copies per individual, so 2Ne in the autosomal units above. mtDNA has one copy per breeding female (Nf), and the Y one copy per breeding male (Nm). Then diversity of Y or mtDNA relative to the autosomes is the ratio of these counts. For equal sexes it gives the cited one-quarter. We checked the algebra by a simulation (below).

What to take away. A skew in breeding numbers does not shrink both markers: it helps one and hurts the other. Few breeding males shrink the Y, while mtDNA relative to the autosomes rises above one-quarter. This is the logic behind using Y and mtDNA together: a Y-to-mtDNA diversity ratio far from one fits unequal numbers of breeding males and females, but selection on the Y can produce a similar pattern (below).

Reading a skyline: Bayesian skyline plots in BEAST2

With a tree for one marker, the standard tool for past size is the Bayesian skyline plot (Drummond et al. 2005), a method for estimating past population dynamics from a sample of sequences "without dependence on a prespecified parametric model of demographic history". It is run in BEAST2, which "samples from the posterior distribution of trees (or networks) and parameters" (Bouckaert et al. 2019). In the BEAST2 skyline tutorial the coalescent skyline "divides the time between the present and the root of the tree" into segments, each with its own effective size, and the x-axis is in years once the clock rate scales branch lengths from substitutions per site into calendar time.

  • Assumption of one mixed population. The tutorial states that the skylines "assume that the population is well-mixed" and "assume that there is no significant population structure", and that results "will be biased" otherwise. Karmin et al. (2015) warn of the same trap: structure combined with unbalanced sampling can lead the skyline plot to infer false declines under constant size.
  • Needs a rate. Without a mutation rate the answer is in substitutions, not years (below).
  • Sequences from all sites. It uses the observed differences between sequences; an unbiased sample of sites is needed (last section).
  • For methods that read the same question from the autosomes of one genome (PSMC and relatives), see lesson 4; they are not repeated here.

Sex-biased demography: reading Y against mtDNA

Karmin et al. (2015) sequenced 456 Y chromosomes (299 new) and made Bayesian skyline plots for Y and for mtDNA of a subset of 320 individuals. The two curves rose together 40 000 to 60 000 years ago, but differed: the mtDNA estimates were consistently more than twice those of the Y, and the Y plot "suggested a reduction at around 8–4 kya" when the female effective size was "up to 17-fold higher than the male". In their abstract: "In contrast to demographic reconstructions based on mtDNA, we infer a second strong bottleneck in Y-chromosome lineages dating to the last 10 ky."

Their reading: the Y bottleneck is not limited to a few haplotypes, so selection is "not a likely explanation", and they hypothesise that cultural change raised the variance in male reproductive success, since it coincides with the spread of Neolithic cultures. That is the paper's hypothesis, not a measurement. A different study that compared autosomes, X, Y and mtDNA in the same men (Wilson Sayres et al., read as the preprint) wrote that models of extreme differences in reproductive success between males and females "are insufficient as the sole explanation" for the low Y diversity, and that natural selection "appears to be acting to reduce diversity on the Y". The two papers ask different questions and do not contradict each other, but both show that Y alone cannot say which cause acted. Karmin et al. add that most methods using the recombining autosomes are underpowered to see changes within the past few thousand years, so the autosomes cannot yet confirm or refute it.

  • A Y-to-mtDNA contrast is a hypothesis generator. It cannot by itself say whether the cause was fewer breeding males, unequal migration of the sexes, selection on the Y or population structure.
  • The calculator above shows only the neutral breeding-numbers part.
  • For animals, the same logic holds with the roles set by the sex-determination system (Wilson Sayres 2018), and the genomes are those you assemble in lesson 2.

Why array markers cannot be dated

A genotyping array reads a fixed list of positions that were already known to vary. Albrechtsen et al. (2010) explain in their abstract that chip data "suffer from ascertainment biases caused by the SNP discovery process in which a small number of individuals from selected populations are used as discovery panels", that these biases "will distort measures of human diversity", and that correcting them is "difficult to generalize among studies". (We read the abstract only.) See section 4 on what arrays can and cannot do.

Our reasoning about dating, not a quotation: a molecular clock counts the new mutations on each branch. Probes were chosen from variants already seen in earlier samples, so mutations private to your own samples are mostly missing, and branch lengths carry little time information. Which branches are long or short depends on whose DNA picked the probes. The array can still tell you which named branch a person sits on (the tools above say how they handle arrays), but not when branches split. A skyline needs sequence of all sites in the region, which any whole-genome read set provides for free: its mitogenome and, for males, its Y.

Check before using. The 1/4 ratios, the formula and the calculator are neutral-model baselines. Real data are often far from them for reasons other than breeding sex ratio (selection on the Y, population structure, one noisy tree). Treat a result as a pointer to a hypothesis.

Story 1. A genealogy that only showed the pipeline works

The goal was an ARG for one chromosome, built with ARG-Needle, a tool made for SNP-array data (s03). The pipeline ran. We still call it a demonstration and not a result, for two reasons.

  • The sample was too small for the tool. The ARG-Needle manual says its hidden Markov model "requires at least 300 haploid samples to build the conditional site frequency spectrum". An earlier, smaller attempt on our data stopped with an error about exactly this step. That agrees with the manual; it is one observation, not a new finding. Three hundred haplotypes is only 150 people, so the floor is easy to miss when you count people.
  • The demography was not ours. The run used a population-size history supplied with the tool, made for another population. The manual lets you give your own (it only asks that sizes are haploid). A genealogy dated with another population's history carries dates that belong to the wrong population.

What we could say: the software installs, reads our files and finishes. What we did not say: anything about the age of shared ancestors or about population size. The next step is the same pipeline on more samples with a demography fitted to the data, not a longer run.

Story 2. IBD segments and a size curve that failed its checks

The plan was a recent population-size curve from IBD segments: detect them with hap-IBD (Zhou et al. 2020), then fit IBDNe (Browning & Browning 2015). Two attempts failed in two different ways.

  • Attempt 1, imputed data. The number of segments per pair of people came out many times above what we expected, an obvious red flag before any curve was fitted. Our reading, which we have not proved against a published test, is this: imputed haplotypes are assembled from stretches of the same reference panel, so two people imputed from the same reference haplotype can look identical by descent without being related. Segments counted this way describe the panel as much as the cohort. We discarded this attempt.
  • Attempt 2, typed SNPs only. The segment count per pair was now sensible. IBDNe's fit to the observed segment lengths was, however, far worse than in its own bootstrap replicates, and the curve fell steeply within a few generations. A fall that steep, with a poor fit, is not a history. We think the cause is a broken assumption: IBDNe's effective size is defined against "an idealized random mating population", and the paper says "the sampled individuals are assumed to be a random sample from the population of interest". Our cohort was a clinical, admixed sample from several regions. The authors also warn that a fitted curve can show artefactual oscillation that looks like changes in growth rate. Their abstract says that array data inform size from around 4 to around 50 generations ago.

What we could say: nothing about the cohort's population size or split times. What would change that: a sample from one region and one ancestry group (the IBDNe page describes ancestry-specific input), and whole-genome data, which the paper says reach over 200 generations back.

Story 3. Selection candidates that came from a reference file

A scan of population differentiation produced strong candidate regions, and a genealogy study of selection was planned around them (s06). The candidates were then compared with an independent call set: a newer, high-coverage whole-genome resequencing of the same reference samples (Byrska-Bishop et al. 2022). The strong candidates came from the older reference file and did not replicate in the newer call set. We concluded they were artefacts of the older file, not signals in the people. We do not know the exact mechanism for each region, and the page does not claim one.

The lesson is a rule. A signal that appears in one data set and one call set is a hypothesis. Differentiation scores are built from allele frequencies, and allele frequencies depend on every upstream step: the reference build, the mapping, the caller and the filters (lesson 4 shows the same chain for heterozygosity). A genealogy-based test of selection inherits whatever was wrong with the list of loci you gave it.
