07How much to trust a branch

A tree method always returns a tree, even from poor data. It does not say which of its branches the data really support. This section is about the numbers written on branches: what the bootstrap, the ultrafast bootstrap, SH-aLRT and the transfer bootstrap each count, and what none of them tells you. Read it after maximum likelihood: the faster methods below are built on likelihood trees.

The question, and why we cannot ask it directly

Suppose the tree says that species D and E (letters only, not the species of our projects in earlier lessons) are each other's closest relatives. The honest question is: if I could collect a fresh, equally large set of data from the same animals, would I get D and E together again? I cannot collect fresh data. So the bootstrap does the next best thing: it treats the data I have as a stand-in for all the data I could have collected, and draws new data sets from it.

The bootstrap, step by step (Felsenstein 1985)

The data are an alignment: one row per species, one column per position. Felsenstein argued that the proper way to resample is to keep all the species and to draw the columns with replacement, assuming the columns evolved independently. In his words, the bootstrap "involves resampling points from one's own data, with replacement, to create a series of bootstrap samples of the same size as the original data."

  1. Draw as many columns as the alignment has, one at a time, each time from all original columns. With replacement means a column can come up twice or more, and some never come up.
  2. Build a tree from the new alignment, with exactly the same method you used for the real one.
  3. Repeat hundreds or thousands of times. Each new alignment, with its tree, is called a replicate.
  4. Count. For every branch of your real tree, write down the percentage of replicates whose tree contains the same branch. That percentage is the bootstrap support. To compare branches between trees we describe each branch by the split it makes: the two groups of species it separates.

Things to notice. (1) In one resample about two thirds of the original columns are present; the rest of the 30 draws are repeats. The exact figure is worked out below. (2) A branch held up by many columns pointing the same way (here AB | CDEF) comes back almost every time. A branch held up by few columns comes back less often, and rival splits appear now and then. (3) The tree from the original data contains CF | ABDE, but after a thousand resamples only about 60 % of them agree: the data barely support it. In this simulation we know the truth (C is closer to D and E than F is), so that branch is wrong. Real data never tell you this, which is why a low number is a warning and a high number is not a proof.

Why about 63 % of the columns appear

An alignment has n columns. In one draw a given column is not picked with probability 1 − 1/n. It is missed in all n draws with probability (1 − 1/n)n, which for large n approaches 1/e ≈ 0.368. So the expected share of original columns that appear at least once is 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632. For 30 columns the exact value is 1 − (29/30)30 = 0.638. The readout under the toy shows your own average next to this figure. This is ordinary probability, not a claim from a paper. It explains why a replicate is a thinner, noisier copy of the data, and why a branch supported by only one or two columns is easily lost.

What the number means, and what it does not

The suggested reading. Felsenstein proposed that "if a group shows up 95% of the time or more, the evidence for it is taken to be statistically significant." That is the origin of the popular 95 % rule.

A later check. Hillis and Bull (1993) tested bootstrap values on simulated data with a known true tree. In their abstract (we read the abstract only), bootstrap proportions are "biased but highly conservative" estimates of how likely a clade is to be real: under their conditions (equal rates of change, symmetric trees, few changes on each internal branch) a value of 70 % or more usually corresponded to a probability of 95 % or more that the clade is real. They also warn that under very high rates of change, or very unequal rates between lineages, values above 50 % can overestimate accuracy.

So the number is best read as a measure of repeatability: how stable the answer is when the columns are reshuffled and reweighted. It is not a probability that the branch is true, because:

  • It only sees random sampling error. If the method or the model is wrong in a consistent way, every replicate is wrong in the same way and the support can be high for a false branch. The long-branch attraction of the parsimony section is the standard example. A bootstrap cannot fix a biased method.
  • It assumes independent columns. Neighbouring sites in a gene, or the three sites of a codon, are not independent. The bootstrap treats them as if they were (Felsenstein states this as his assumption).
  • It depends on the alignment. A branch can be stable over columns and still come from a poor alignment.
  • It judges one branch at a time. A tree whose every branch has 70 % support is not 70 % right as a whole.

Ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot)

The ordinary bootstrap needs a full tree search on every replicate, which becomes slow for large data. Minh, Nguyen and von Haeseler (2013) built UFBoot to compute the support of groups in likelihood trees much faster. By their abstract it combines the "resampling estimated log-likelihood method" with a "simple but effective collection scheme of candidate trees", and adds a stopping rule that decides when the support values have converged. They report a median speed-up of 3.1 to 10.2 times over the RAxML rapid bootstrap on real data. In their simulations the values "appear to be relatively unbiased compared with the conservative standard bootstrap", which they say "provides a more direct interpretation of the bootstrap support".

That last point is the catch. A UFBoot percentage and a standard bootstrap percentage are not the same kind of number. The IQ-TREE FAQ says so directly: UFBoot has "a different meaning than the normal (more conservative) bootstrap supports", and "you should not compare BS% with UFBoot% directly".

Threshold, as the program's authors give it. The IQ-TREE FAQ: "For UFBoot, you should only start to rely on a branch if its support is ≥ 95%". The UFBoot2 paper (Hoang et al. 2018) gives the same figure for data with "no or mild" model violations. Under severe violations UFBoot2 "overestimated the branch supports". For that case the paper recommends an extra nearest-neighbour-interchange step on each replicate, which IQ-TREE switches on with -bnni. That is the authors' recommendation, not a guarantee: it reduces the risk of over-estimating, it does not repair a wrong model.

SH-aLRT

SH-aLRT (Guindon et al. 2010) is not a count of resampled trees. It is a test, based on likelihood ratios, of how well the data support one internal branch; the abstract calls it "a nonparametric, Shimodaira-Hasegawa-like procedure" that extends the earlier approximate likelihood-ratio test. The calculation itself is worked through, from the IQ-TREE source code, in section 12. IQ-TREE prints it next to UFBoot as "SH-aLRT support (%) / ultrafast bootstrap support (%)", for example 92.5/98. Its FAQ gives a joint rule: "One would be more confident if a clade has its SH-aLRT ≥ 80% and UFboot ≥ 95%."

Comparing whole trees: KH, SH and AU

Support values judge one branch. Sometimes the question is about a whole tree: a published tree, or a group you expected, is not in your best tree. Is it significantly worse, or just slightly worse? Three tests answer this from the per-column likelihoods. The IQ-TREE manual sums up their history: the KH test "was designed to test 2 trees and thus has no correction for multiple testing"; the SH test "fixes this problem", but "becomes too conservative (i.e., rejecting fewer trees than expected) when testing many trees"; and the AU test (Shimodaira 2002) addresses that. The abstract of that paper warns that "comparing many trees at the same time ... often leads to overconfidence in the wrong trees". A tree with p below 0.05 is rejected, and IQ-TREE marks it with a minus sign. The file TREES_FILE holds the trees to compare, one per line. The manual recommends at least 1,000 draws, here called RELL replicates. You can run all three tests on a small alignment in section 12, under "Comparing whole trees".

iqtree3 -s ALN_FILE -z TREES_FILE -n 0 -zb 10000 -au

Transfer bootstrap (TBE)

The classical (Felsenstein) bootstrap proportion, FBP, counts a replicate only if it contains the identical split. Lemoine et al. (2018) point out that for large trees this is harsh: "A replicated branch must match a reference branch exactly", and a difference of just one taxon is enough to make a nearly identical branch count as absent. As a result the FBP "tends to yield very low supports, especially on deep branches".

Their alternative, the transfer bootstrap expectation (TBE), measures how many taxa you would have to move ("transfer") from one side of the branch to the other to make a branch of the replicate tree identical to the reference branch. The formula in the paper is TBE(b) = 1 − (average transfer index) / (p − 1), where p is the number of taxa on the smaller side of branch b. It runs from 0 to 1, and the authors write that "TBE supports are easily interpreted as fractions of unstable taxa".

Worked example of the formula (ours, not from the paper) Reference branch: {1,2,3,4,5} | the other taxa, so p = 5. In every replicate taxon 5 wanders off, but 1-4 stay together. FBP: the split is never identical -> 0 % Transfer index: moving 1 taxon repairs it -> 1 TBE = 1 - 1/(5-1) = 0.75 -> 75 %

What the authors say about reading it. It "should not be interpreted as the confidence level of some statistical test" and "cannot be interpreted as the probability for the branch b to belong to the true phylogeny". On a cut-off: "our results suggest that 70% is a reasonable threshold to start with", but "it is better for users to interpret the TBE values depending on the data". TBE is a different quantity from FBP and from UFBoot, so a TBE of 80 % and an FBP of 80 % do not mean the same.

Running it

In IQ-TREE the options are -B (ultrafast bootstrap replicates) and -alrt (SH-aLRT replicates). The tutorial gives 1000 as the recommended minimum for each, and says -bnni was added in version 1.6 to reduce over-estimated UFBoot support when the model is badly violated.

# version 3 is called iqtree3; version 2 is called iqtree2
iqtree3 -s alignment.fasta -B 1000 -alrt 1000 -bnni

The IQ-TREE tutorial describes the support numbers as SH-aLRT support (%) / ultrafast bootstrap support (%), for example 92.5/98. Always say which kind of number you show, and which threshold you used.

Simple explanation with examples: why shuffle the columns?

Imagine the alignment as a deck of 30 cards, one per column, and the tree as the story the whole deck tells. To test how much the story depends on particular cards, draw 30 cards from the deck, putting each back after you look at it, and read the story again. If the story barely changes whichever cards you happen to draw, many cards say the same thing: the branch is stable. If a branch appears only when one particular card is drawn, it rested on that card.

Example: if 10 columns support AB | CDEF and none support a rival, almost every draw of 30 contains at least one of the 10 columns, so the split comes back almost every time. If only 2 columns support a split, a draw can easily miss both, and the split vanishes from many replicates.

Simple explanation with examples: 95 % of what?

"This branch has 95 % support" is easy to read as "there is a 95 % chance this branch is real". It does not say that. It says: in 95 of 100 resampled data sets, the same method found this branch. If the method is biased towards a wrong answer, it will find the wrong branch in 95 of 100 resampled data sets too. Think of a bathroom scale that reads 2 kg too heavy: weighing yourself 100 times gives the same number 100 times, the repeatability is perfect, and the number is wrong.
