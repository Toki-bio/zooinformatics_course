11Choosing and reporting

Sections 01 to 10 gave you the methods one by one. This last section puts them together. It has four parts: a helper that goes from your data and your question to a method, a list of what a methods paragraph must say, the usual mistakes, and the file formats and programs for drawing the result. Every recommendation is tagged. A source tag means a manual or paper we opened says it, and the sources are listed at the end. An advice tag means it is our own advice, which you may argue with.

Simple explanation with examples: why the answer depends on the question

Think of choosing a vehicle. A bicycle is fine for the shop round the corner, a lorry is needed for a house move, and neither goes to another continent. A quick tree from one gene is the bicycle. A tree of many genes with checks of how much they disagree is the lorry. Divergence times need a different kind of vehicle again, because you must add a clock and something that tells the clock how old a branch is, such as a fossil.

Example: you sequenced one mitochondrial gene from 30 frogs and want to see which ones group together. A first look is enough, and the helper says so. Now you want to publish the species tree of those frogs. One gene is the history of one piece of DNA, and the helper tells you why that may not be the history of the species (s10).

What a methods paragraph must state

A reader should be able to repeat your tree from your paragraph and your files. The list below follows the steps of an analysis. Kapli, Yang and Telford (2020) name the major steps as finding orthologous genes, multiple sequence alignment, choice of substitution model and the inference method, and say that understanding the sources of error at each step is essential source: Kapli 2020, abstract only. Orthologous genes are copies that diverged at a speciation, not at a gene duplication (s02). The items themselves are our advice, except where tagged.

Common mistakes

Reading the tree as a ladder
Omland, Cook and Crisp call it the "primitive lineage fallacy": assuming that a species-poor lineage that looks "early branching" or "basal" is ancestral source: Omland 2008, abstract only. Present-day species on a tree are all the same age. The order of tips on the page means nothing, because any node can be rotated (try it in the drawing tool below).
Calling 100 % "proof"
IQ-TREE's FAQ warns that for phylogenomic data UFBoot and even the standard bootstrap "will tend to be 100%", and recommends concordance factors for any phylogenomic analysis source: IQ-TREE FAQ. The gene concordance factor is the percentage of decisive gene trees that contain the branch, and the site concordance factor the percentage of decisive sites that support it source: IQ-TREE concordance factors. A branch can be strongly supported by the pooled data and still be contradicted by many genes.
Using the wrong support scale
Do not set UFBoot beside the standard bootstrap as if they were the same measure source: IQ-TREE FAQ. The FAQ says to start relying on a branch at UFBoot of at least 95 %, and to be more confident when SH-aLRT is at least 80 % as well, but it adds that these recommendations only apply to single gene trees source: IQ-TREE FAQ.
Trusting a Bayesian run that has not converged
The MrBayes manual calls an average standard deviation of split frequencies below 0.01 "very good indication of convergence", and values between 0.01 and 0.05 possibly adequate for some purposes. It says an effective sample size (ESS) below 100 suggests poor mixing, and that PSRF should be close to 1.0 source: MrBayes manual.
One gene taken for the species tree
A single locus is the history of that piece of DNA (s10). Say "gene tree" in the text unless you have dealt with disagreement between loci. advice
Branch lengths read as time
On a tree from sequences, a branch length is an amount of change, not years, unless you did a dating analysis (s09). advice
No root, or an unstated root
Most programs give an unrooted tree, and the drawing program chooses some root to show it. A rooted-looking figure must say how it was rooted. The Newick description itself says that for an unrooted tree the convention is to root it arbitrarily and report the result source: Newick standard.

File formats

A tree file is plain text. Two formats cover nearly every program.

Newick
Interior nodes are written as matched parentheses. Inside them are the descendants, separated by commas. The tree ends with a semicolon. A branch length comes after a node, after a colon. A name may be any printable characters except blanks, colons, semicolons, parentheses and square brackets source: Newick standard. Trees "can be multifurcating at any level" (a node may have more than two children).
Nexus
A file made of separate blocks. Each block holds one kind of data, such as taxa, characters or trees, so that one file can carry the alignment and the trees together source: Maddison et al. 1997, abstract only. Inside a trees block the trees are usually written in Newick.
# Newick: the first two examples are on the Newick standard page
((A,B),(C,D));                          # shape only
(B:6.0,(A:5.0,C:3.0,E:4.0):5.0,D:11.0); # with branch lengths, and a node with three children
((A,B)95,(C,D)88);                      # numbers after a bracket: usually support values

# Nexus: the same idea inside a block
#NEXUS
BEGIN TREES;
  TREE example = ((A:1,B:1):1,(C:1,D:1):1);
END;

The third Newick line is a common habit, not part of the standard we read: programs often write support values in the place where an interior node's name would go. Such a number belongs to the branch above that node, so if you re-root the tree in a program that does not know this, the numbers can end up on the wrong branches. Check your support values after re-rooting. advice The Nexus block is our own small example of the layout, not copied from the paper.

Simple explanation with examples: what a rotation is

A tree is like a hanging mobile. Each node is a joint where two or more arms hang. Spin any joint and the arms swap places, but nothing is cut or tied differently. ((A,B),(C,D)); and ((D,C),(B,A)); are the same tree, drawn twice. In both, A and B are sisters, and C and D are sisters.

So do not say "A is close to C" because their tips are drawn next to each other. Find the node where the paths from A and from C meet. The nearer that node is to the tips, the closer the relatives.

Drawing trees

The programs below read a tree file, and you can rotate nodes, change the root, show branch lengths and support values, and save a picture. Choose by how you work.

FigTree
"Designed as a graphical viewer of phylogenetic trees and as a program for producing publication-ready figures", and made to show trees summarised by BEAST. Its README says it has been superseded by PearTree source: FigTree README.
iTOL
A web tool for "the display, annotation and management of phylogenetic and other trees". It reads Newick, Nexus, PhyloXML and Jplace, and you add annotation files by dragging them onto the tree source: iTOL help.
ggtree
An R package that extends the ggplot2 plotting system, made for "visualization and annotation of phylogenetic trees and other tree-like structures" source: Bioconductor page, version 4.2.1.

For any figure we advise: show a scale bar if branch lengths mean something, say in the legend which support values you show, state the root, and keep the tree file you drew from. advice
