06Maximum likelihood

Parsimony counts changes. Maximum likelihood asks a different question: if this tree and this model of evolution were true, how probable would our alignment be? The tree that makes the alignment most probable wins. This section works through that calculation by hand on four sequences, then shows how programs do the same thing on thousands.

What a likelihood is

The likelihood of a tree is the probability of the data given that tree. Here the data are the aligned sequences. The "tree" means its shape (the topology) and its branch lengths, together with a model of substitution from section 03. Posada and Crandall (2021) write it as P(S | T, M): the probability of the sequences S, given tree T and model M.

Read that direction carefully. A likelihood is the chance of the data if the tree is right. It is not the chance that the tree is right. Getting from one to the other needs extra assumptions, which is what section 08 is about.

Felsenstein's 1981 method makes two simplifying assumptions, and most programs still make them: sites change independently of each other, and branches change independently of each other. Posada and Crandall call these "unrealistic", but they are what makes the calculation possible. Because sites are independent, the likelihood of the alignment is the likelihood of each column multiplied together.

The numbers become very small very quickly, so programs add up the natural logarithms of the column likelihoods instead of multiplying. This sum is the log-likelihood, written ln L. It is negative, and a value closer to zero is better. RAxML-NG's own tutorial shows a real example of −5708.92 for one alignment.

Simple explanation with examples: likelihood with a coin

You toss a coin 10 times and get 7 heads. You want to know the coin's chance of heads, h.

If h = 0.5, the probability of exactly this sequence of tosses is 0.57 × 0.53 = 0.00098. If h = 0.7, it is 0.77 × 0.33 = 0.00222. So h = 0.7 is the better explanation: it has the higher likelihood. No other value of h does better, so 0.7 is the maximum likelihood estimate.

Nothing here says "h is probably 0.7". It says only that, among the values of h, 0.7 makes what we saw most probable. Tree inference works the same way. The tree plays the part of h, and the alignment plays the part of the tosses.

The likelihood of one site

Take one column of an alignment with four sequences, P, Q, R and S. (These letters avoid confusion with the bases A, C, G, T.) Four sequences have three possible unrooted trees (section 01). Take the tree that joins P with Q and R with S. It has two internal nodes, u and v, which stand for ancestors we never saw.

If we knew the ancestors' bases, the probability of the column would be a simple product: the chance of the base at the start, times the chance of what happened along each of the five branches. We do not know them, so we must add up every possibility. Each of the two nodes could be any of four bases, which gives 4 × 4 = 16 products:

L = ∑u ∑v πv × P(v→u, m) × P(u→P's base, tP) × P(u→Q's base, tQ) × P(v→R's base, tR) × P(v→S's base, tS)

Here πv is the chance of that base at the starting node. P(x→y, t) is the chance that base x has become base y after a branch of length t. Branch lengths are in expected substitutions per site, as in section 03. Under JC69 every base has π = ¼, and the branch chances have a simple form:

same base: ¼ + ¾ e−4t/3    one particular different base: ¼ − ¼ e−4t/3

Sixteen terms is easy. The trouble is growth. Posada and Crandall give the count: for 12 sequences, a rooted tree has 11 internal nodes, so 411 = 4,194,304 products of 23 terms each, for every site.

Felsenstein pruning

Felsenstein's answer was to work from the tips towards the root, one node at a time. Posada and Crandall describe it as computing "in terms of conditional likelihoods, starting from the tips". The conditional likelihood of a node for a base is the probability of everything seen above that node, given that the node had that base. Each node holds four such numbers, one per base.

  1. Tips. A tip has 1 for the base we see and 0 for the other three.
  2. Each internal node, once its neighbours are done. For each base the node might have had: for each neighbour, sum over that neighbour's four bases the chance of the branch change times the neighbour's own number. Then multiply the neighbours' results together.
  3. Finish at the root. Weight the root's four numbers by π and add them up.

This works because a node's four numbers already summarise everything above it, so nothing above it needs to be looked at again. The work now grows in step with the number of nodes, not as a power of four. The method is known as Felsenstein pruning. It gives exactly the same answer as the full sum; it only avoids repeating work. The figure below checks this for whatever you set.

The root in the figure is at node v, but that choice does not matter. Felsenstein showed that for a reversible model the likelihood is the same wherever the root is placed; he called this the pulley principle. So the method gives an unrooted tree, and the root must come from elsewhere, such as an outgroup (section 01).

Real analyses add one more layer. With gamma rates (section 03), each site's likelihood is calculated once for each rate category, and the results are averaged using the category weights. The pruning itself does not change.

Branch lengths are estimated too

A topology alone has no likelihood. Its branch lengths must be chosen first, and the fair choice is the set that gives that topology its highest likelihood. Finding that set is branch length optimisation.

The smallest case is two sequences joined by one branch, length d. With n sites and k differences under JC69, the log-likelihood has only two kinds of column:

ln L(d) = n ln ¼ + (n − k) ln(¼ + ¾ e−4d/3) + k ln(¼ − ¼ e−4d/3)

The value of d at the top of this curve is the maximum likelihood distance. In this one case there is a formula for it, and it is the JC69 correction you have already met in section 03: d = −¾ ln(1 − 4p/3), with p = k/n. So the correction was a likelihood estimate all along.

A larger tree has no such formula, so programs climb numerically. Felsenstein's method improves one branch at a time while the others stay fixed, then goes round again until nothing improves. RAxML-NG's default for this step is a Newton-Raphson method (--blopt nr_fast), which is a way of climbing a curve using its slope and bend.

Comparing the three trees

Now everything can be put together. For an alignment of four sequences, take each of the three topologies, optimise its five branch lengths, and compare the best ln L each one can reach. This is a complete maximum likelihood analysis, because with four sequences every tree can be tried.

Two things differ from parsimony (section 05). First, every column counts. IQ-TREE's FAQ says that constant sites "are important for the correct estimation of the branch lengths", and should not be thrown away. Delete the constant columns from the example and watch the branch lengths grow. Second, the size of the answer is meaningful. Branch lengths come out of the same calculation as the score, and a change on a long branch costs less than a change on a short one.

The ln L values above are only comparable with each other because the alignment and model are the same. Our reading: never compare ln L between different alignments. Within one alignment, a lead of a few log units from a handful of sites is not yet a statement of confidence; that needs section 07.

One warning from building this figure. Even on a single fixed tree, a short alignment can have more than one peak for its branch lengths. On the Conflict example, some starting points stopped about 2 log units below the best value, because a change could be placed on either of two sister tips. The page therefore starts from five points and keeps the best. Real programs face the same risk, which is one reason for the repeated runs below.

Searching for the tree

With four sequences we simply tried all three trees. With 50 sequences that is impossible (section 01). Finding the best tree is what computer scientists call NP-hard, which means, in plain terms, that no shortcut is known that would guarantee it for large data sets. So, as Nguyen et al. (2015) put it, "one has to rely on search heuristics". A heuristic is a good method with no guarantee.

The basic search is hill climbing. Start from a quick tree, try small changes, keep any change that raises the likelihood, and stop when nothing nearby is better. Two kinds of small change are common:

  • Nearest neighbour interchange (NNI) "swaps two subtrees across an internal branch". Each internal branch offers two such swaps, so a tree of n sequences has 2(n − 3) NNI neighbours (Nguyen et al. 2015). With n = 4 that is 2: from any of the three topologies in the figure above, one NNI reaches either of the other two.
  • Subtree pruning and regrafting (SPR) cuts off a subtree and attaches it to another branch. It reaches many more trees in one move. RAxML-NG's search is built on SPR moves, and its radius limits how far away a subtree may be reinserted.

Hill climbing has a known weakness: it can stop on a lower peak, a local optimum, because every nearby tree is worse even though a better tree exists further away. Nguyen et al. note that this is worse when a move reaches few trees, which is why SPR often beats NNI in plain hill climbing.

The two main programs escape in different ways. IQ-TREE keeps a small set of good trees, randomly disturbs one of them with a few NNIs, and climbs again. It stops when the best tree has not improved after 100 such attempts. RAxML-NG runs complete SPR searches from several different starting trees and keeps the best result. Its tutorial shows why: on one data set, 15 of 20 searches ended near ln L = −9974.67, and 5 ended near −9980.60 on a different tree.

Neither result is guaranteed to be the true best tree. Nguyen et al. found that both programs "still finish sometimes in local optima", and advise repeated runs. Compare the final ln L across runs. If they differ, keep the highest and be more careful with that data set.

Choosing the model

Likelihood also tells us which model to use. Section 03 explained the idea: richer models always fit at least as well, so the fit must be charged for its parameters. These are the formulas used in the IQ-TREE source code, where k is the number of free parameters and n is the number of alignment sites:

AIC = −2 ln L + 2k    BIC = −2 ln L + k ln n

A lower score is better. In IQ-TREE's report, k counts the branches as well as the model parameters, so a tree of 10 sequences starts at 17.

Simple explanation with examples: AIC and BIC with invented numbers

An invented alignment of 10 sequences and 1,000 sites, so ln n = 6.91 and the tree has 17 branches. HKY adds 4 free parameters and GTR adds 8.

model ln L k AIC BIC JC69 -5200.0 17 10434.0 10517.4 HKY -5120.0 21 10282.0 10385.1 <- best BIC GTR -5115.0 25 10280.0 10402.7 <- best AIC

GTR has the highest ln L, as a richer model must. BIC says its extra 5 log units do not pay for 4 more parameters. AIC, with its lighter charge, just prefers it. The criteria can disagree, so report which one you used.

ModelFinder in IQ-TREE computes these scores for many models on one quick tree, a parsimony tree, and keeps the model with the lowest BIC. RAxML-NG 2 has a similar tool called MOOSE, also using BIC by default. In the ModelFinder paper, a BIC difference above 10 is treated as strong evidence against the higher score. Because the default compares models on a single tree, the paper notes that a poor starting tree can lead to a suboptimal model. The -mtree option does a full tree search for each model, at a large cost in time.

IQ-TREE

IQ-TREE 3 (Wong et al. 2026) is the current major version; the latest release when this was written was 3.1.4. The program itself is called iqtree3, while much of the documentation still writes iqtree, and version 1 used some different option names. Commands to adapt:

# the simplest run: choose the model with ModelFinder, then search for the tree
iqtree3 -s alignment.fasta -T 4 --seed 1 --prefix run1

# the same, with a model you chose yourself
iqtree3 -s alignment.fasta -m GTR+G --seed 1 --prefix run2

# no tree search: the likelihood of one fixed tree
iqtree3 -s alignment.fasta -te mytree.nwk -m GTR+G --prefix fixed

RAxML-NG

RAxML-NG (Kozlov et al. 2019) is a rewrite of the older RAxML. The latest release when this was written was 2.0.4. Version 2 changed some behaviour, and the wiki's tutorial was written for version 0.8, so we checked the defaults below in the 2.0.4 source code. Unlike IQ-TREE, it stops with an error if you give no --model.

# check the alignment first; the tutorial says most failed runs are format errors
raxml-ng --check --msa alignment.fasta --model GTR+G --prefix T1

# tree search under a model you chose
raxml-ng --msa alignment.fasta --model GTR+G --prefix T2 --threads 2 --seed 2

# choose the model with MOOSE, then search (new in version 2)
raxml-ng --msa alignment.fasta --model DNA --prefix T3 --seed 2

# set the number of starting trees yourself
raxml-ng --msa alignment.fasta --model GTR+G --tree pars{25},rand{25} --prefix T4 --seed 2

# no tree search: the likelihood of one fixed tree
raxml-ng --evaluate --msa alignment.fasta --model GTR+G --tree mytree.nwk --prefix E1

The best tree is in .raxml.bestTree, its fitted model in .raxml.bestModel, one tree per starting tree in .raxml.mlTrees, and everything printed on screen in .raxml.log. The tutorial's check for a run is to read the "Final LogLikelihood" line and to compare the trees with --rfdist.

The two programs are not rivals with a clear winner. Nguyen et al. found higher likelihoods with IQ-TREE on most of their benchmark, and still recommended using more than one program. Kozlov et al. found that RAxML-NG was generally faster and scored higher on alignments with many sequences, while IQ-TREE's results varied much less between runs. Both of these are the authors' tests of their own software, on older versions.

Simple explanation with examples: what a maximum likelihood run does, in order
  1. Read the alignment and merge identical columns, since they have the same likelihood.
  2. Choose a model, by you or by BIC.
  3. Build quick starting trees.
  4. For a tree: fit the branch lengths and model values, using pruning to get ln L at each try.
  5. Rearrange the tree with NNI or SPR moves, and keep what improves ln L.
  6. Stop by the program's rule, and report the best tree found.

Step 4 sits inside step 5, so pruning runs a very large number of times. That is why likelihood is slow compared with distance methods, and why these programs spend so much effort on making it fast.
