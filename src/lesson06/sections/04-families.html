04Families, subfamilies and consensus sequences

A search (section 02) gives you thousands or millions of copies of one SINE. They are not all alike. Some differences were made once, in an active "source" copy, and passed on to all its offspring; most were made later, in each copy on its own. This section is about telling those two kinds of difference apart: how copies are grouped into subfamilies, how one sequence, the consensus, is made to stand for each group, and which lab tools do it.

Family, subfamily, consensus

The definitions our lab works with are written in the SINEderella manual (section 6.1.6):

Family
A set of SINE copies made of the same parts (for example the same head, body and tail, section 01), so that they still align along their whole length.
Subfamily
A lineage inside a family, defined by a shared, diagnostic pattern of small indels or single-base differences that all its copies carry: in effect a synapomorphy. Not by the private mutations each copy collected after it was inserted: those are noise on top of the subfamily signal.
Diagnostic position
An alignment column where (almost) all copies of one group carry the same base or gap and (almost) no copy outside the group does.
Consensus
One sequence that stands for a group of copies: at each column of their alignment, the base most copies have. Private mutations are scattered, so they are outvoted; the shared pattern survives. A good consensus approximates the sequence of the source gene that made the copies.

A direct consequence, stated in the same manual section: average pairwise identity is the wrong measure for finding subfamilies. Two old copies of one subfamily can differ at 20 % of positions, all private; two young copies of sister subfamilies may differ only at their two or three diagnostic positions. Identity measures the private decay, which is exactly the part the definition calls noise. The figure below lets you see this happen.

Published SINE subfamilies show the kinds of features that diagnose them. In the rodent SINEs B2, Dip and Ves the subfamilies are "reliably distinguished by relatively long indels" (Vassetzky et al. 2021). In the shrew-mole SINE Urop, subfamilies b and c share a 24-nucleotide tandem duplication that subfamily a lacks (Kosushkin et al. 2026). The SINE Can splits into older a1 and a2 and younger b1 and b2, of which b2 (and less so b1) is still active (Kosushkin et al. 2022).

Simple explanation with examples: scribes, slips and a family tree of manuscripts

Think of a medieval text copied by hand. A scribe copies a manuscript and introduces a few slips. Other scribes copy his copy, so every later copy in that line carries his slips, plus slips of their own. Philologists group surviving manuscripts by the slips they share, not by how many slips each has: a manuscript full of its own errors can still belong, without doubt, to a line marked by two shared errors.

SINE copies work the same way. A source copy in the genome mutates, keeps being copied, and all its offspring carry its changes. Those shared changes are the diagnostic positions. Each copy then decays on its own, a little more every million years. A consensus is like reconstructing the lost exemplar of one line: take many copies of that line, and at each position keep the reading most of them agree on.

Example: 8 copies of subfamily A and 7 of subfamily B. At position 12 all A copies read G and all B copies read A; at position 57 all A copies read T and all B copies read C. Everywhere else the copies differ by scattered single changes. Positions 12 and 57 together are the diagnostic pattern: they split the copies the same way, and each split position backs up the other.

Divergence of each copy from its own group consensus

The model. A random 72-bp source sequence with one CpG (columns 41-42). Lineage A keeps it; lineage B differs from A at two columns; lineage C differs from B at two further columns. 8, 7 and 5 copies. Each copy then gets its own random mutations at a rate drawn between 3 % and 16 % (old and young copies in every lineage). The C of its CpG turns into T, and the G into A, each with a probability six times that rate (at most 90 %). To keep the pattern clean, the toy spares the four diagnostic columns from private mutations; in real copies decay hits them too, which is why real tools allow a few exceptions. Rows are shuffled. Consensus rule: the most common base of the group, upper case when more than half of the group has it, lower case when fewer, n on a tie (the lower-case convention follows SubFam). Backing check (toy rule): another column backs a column you picked when, in every class of at least 3 copies, the copies share one base there, a different base for each class, with at most one exception. Diagnostic columns in the identity grouping: one base fills at least 90 % of the group and at most 2 % of the other copies (the definition ViewAlign's peel uses; one exception allowed each side here because the groups are tiny). Identity grouping: p-distance (share of differing columns) between every pair, average-linkage tree (UPGMA), cut into 3 groups. Real alignments add indels, truncated copies and thousands of copies.

What the toy shows. (The diagnostic columns are 12, 28, 57 and 64; "New copies" changes the copies, not these columns.) Columns 12 and 57 split the copies in exactly the same way and back each other up: a shared pattern, one subfamily boundary. Columns 28 and 64 do the same one level down. Picking 12 and 28 together gives three groups, and every one of them is a true lineage; note that the middle lineage has no column of its own: it is recognised by a combination (the "new" base at 12, the "old" base at 28). A column with one or two odd letters, a private mutation, gives groups too small to call. The CpG (columns 41-42) is the trap: many copies differ there, in every lineage, because the CpG decays fast everywhere, so the bar is tall but no other column backs the split. It sorts copies by age, not by descent. Grouping by overall identity fails in its own way: the deepest branches of the identity tree are the most decayed single copies, so a cut into three groups usually splits off two old copies and leaves the lineages mixed. Look at the divergence plot after a good split: each copy is a little closer to its own group consensus (filled dot) than to the consensus of all 20 (ring); the difference is the shared pattern.

Building a consensus

The basic rule is a vote in each column of a multiple alignment. The details decide the result:

  • Majority or plurality. A majority call needs more than half of the sequences; a plurality call only needs the base to be the most common one, above some threshold. With old copies no base may reach half, so tools use a lower threshold.
  • Are gaps counted? If gaps stay in the denominator ("a base must be in at least 50 % of all sequences"), a column that most copies lack is not called. This trims ragged ends and rare insertions. If gaps are left out, the consensus gets longer and denser.
  • Threshold as a fraction, never a count. The same count means different things in groups of 20 and 2,000.
  • Run the final call once. Our lab notes record that re-aligning copies to a consensus and calling again, round after round, made one family's consensus creep outward from 203 to 252 to 269 bp: each round pulls in a little flank.

SINE_consensus: a bootstrap consensus for one group

SINE_consensus (lab tool, public) builds the consensus of one group of copies that you have already chosen. sine_consensus.sh works in rounds: it draws a random subsample of 100 copies, aligns them with MAFFT, calls a mini-consensus, adds it to the mini-consensuses of earlier rounds, aligns those and calls the consensus of the consensuses. It stops when that consensus changes at fewer than 1 % of positions between rounds (at least 5, at most 50 rounds). If the rounds keep swinging, an "anchor phase" (after round 3) mixes copies of the best consensus so far into each subsample, up to half of it, so that one unlucky sample cannot flip the result.

The call itself, as the script implements it (version of March 2026): a column is skipped when fewer than 30 % of sequences have a base there; otherwise the most common base is called if it is in at least 50 % of all sequences, gaps included; ties are not called. The README's option table still describes an older rule (gaps left out of the denominator); the script's own help (-h) is the current one. A second script, sine_pairwise_consensus.sh, aligns every copy one by one to a reference consensus (MAFFT with a high gap-opening penalty, --op 5) and votes position by position; the README recommends it for families with thousands of copies.

# one consensus from the copies of one subfamily (FASTA, at least 3 sequences)
# -k keeps the log and a "trace" alignment of the mini-consensus of every round
bash sine_consensus.sh -k dip_group1.fa          # writes dip_group1_consensus.fasta

# the same rule, stricter about ragged ends: base in >= 70 % of all copies
bash sine_consensus.sh -t 70 dip_group1.fa

# pairwise version for a large family, 8 alignment jobs in parallel
bash sine_pairwise_consensus.sh -j 8 -k dip_all.fa
The CpG caveat

In vertebrates most CpG sites are methylated, and a methylated C decays to T many times faster than other sites mutate (lesson 3, section 02). A CG in the source gene becomes TG in some copies, CA in others (the same change on the other strand), and TA where both have happened. A vote is taken column by column: once more than half of the copies have lost the C, the first column is called T; once more than half have lost the G, the second is called A. In an old family the consensus loses its CpGs, and copies measured against it look less diverged than they are. SINE_consensus and SubFam use plain votes. RepeatModeler's consensus builder (MultAln.pm, "Correct for missed CpG calls") scores a CG hypothesis higher when the copies show TG or CA; its own comment notes that the setting seemed to overestimate CpGs in very old elements. What to do by hand: at every TG or CA of your consensus, look at the column pair; a mix of CG, TG and CA there points to a lost CpG. Tick "CpG-aware consensus" in the toy to see the difference.

SubFam: thousands of copies as an alignment of consensus sequences

Twenty copies fit on a screen; 30,000 do not, and no one can see the shared pattern in them. SubFam (lab tool, public; version 1.5.1) represents a large set of sequences as a short alignment of consensus sequences. Its README: 40 rows for 2,000 copies, each row the consensus of a group of closely related copies, "the private mutations of individual copies are averaged away, and the variants a group shares stay visible".

  1. Order
    All copies are put in an order where similar copies sit next to each other. The order comes from a tree built on k-mer counts (k = 6, weighted Jaccard distance, UPGMA), the same tree ViewAlign uses for "Reorder by similarity". No alignment is made in this step. Option -m uses MAFFT's guide tree instead (mafft --retree 0 --reorder, the method of SubFam 1.0).
  2. Chunk
    The ordered list is cut into blocks of about N = 50 neighbours (-n); nothing is dropped.
  3. Consensus per chunk
    Each chunk is aligned with MAFFT (in parallel) and gets a plurality consensus: a base is called when at least 0.36 × chunk size of the sequences support it (-p; 18 of 50, the value SINEderella has used since its first version). Bases supported by half the chunk or fewer are written in lower case.
  4. Align the consensuses
    The chunk consensuses are aligned with MAFFT L-INS-i (an accurate, slow mode) up to 300 rows, a faster setting up to 1,000, MAFFT's automatic mode beyond. Result: PREFIX.aln.fasta.

Why chunks, not clusters at an identity threshold? Standard clustering tools (CD-HIT, VSEARCH, MMseqs2) need a cut-off such as "90 % identical". For repeat families no cut-off fits all: a young family is split into hundreds of tiny clusters at 95 % and lumped together at 80 %, and in an old family any useful cut-off leaves most copies alone. SubFam only asks which copies are each other's nearest neighbours and averages 50 of them. On the README's simulated families (8 subfamilies, 2,000 copies, decayed by 3, 8 or 15 %), SubFam recovered 8, 8 and 7 of 8 subfamilies with 40 rows; each VSEARCH threshold worked only for one age. These are simulations from the tool's own benchmark, not a test on real SINEs.

What SubFam does not do. It does not decide what the subfamilies are. A chunk can fall inside a subfamily (redundant rows) or straddle two (mixed rows), and a subfamily needs roughly N copies to get a row of its own; use a smaller -n for rare ones. Grouping the rows is the next step (below).

# 30,000 sampled copies, chunks of 50, 16 threads, outputs named dip.*
./SubFam.sh -n 50 -t 16 -o subfam_out -x dip dip_sample30k.fa

# dip.aln.fasta    the chunk consensuses, aligned: open this in ViewAlign
# dip.chunks.tsv   which copy built which consensus row
# dip.chunkstats.tsv  per row: how tight the chunk was (agreement, identity, raggedness)
# copies on both strands? add -r ; rare subfamilies? try -n 20
In our projects

SINEderella hands SubFam a random sample of 30,000 copies, which gives about 600 rows. Our de novo runs on species A and its close relative made such alignments (600 rows each, after the known SINE family was masked; section 03). Lab notes on the SINEs of the stick insect Timema (550 chunks, unpublished) found that two neighbouring chunks belonged to the same subfamily 92 % of the time (0.916), against 31 % (0.313) for two chunks picked at random: the chunk order really is a similarity order.

Finding the subfamilies: peeling

The SubFam alignment of a few hundred rows is where the subfamily call is made. In the lab this has been done by eye: open the alignment, find a block of rows that share a pattern of columns no other rows have, set it aside, and repeat on the rest until only noise is left. This is called peeling. Each peeled block becomes a candidate subfamily; its copies (from chunks.tsv, or SubFam's -e option) go to SINE_consensus.

Automatic grouping is a first pass, not the answer. The SINEderella manual records a test of its earlier automatic clusterer against nine subfamilies called by hand: it found a true two-way split but merged the nine into two large groups. Our lab's rule is that subfamily decisions are made by a person, on alignments. One more caution when you draw the result: subfamily consensuses are not species. An active source gene can give rise to a new subfamily while still active itself, so ancestor and descendant coexist. Our lab draws consensus relationships as a clustering (for example UPGMA on p-distance), not as a phylogeny.

Viewing large alignments in ViewAlign

ViewAlign runs in the browser, needs no installation and opens FASTA, Clustal, MSF, Stockholm and other formats. For SINE work the features that matter are:

  • Size. Full and Block views draw only the visible window above about 100,000 residues; the Canvas view switches on by itself above 5 million. An alignment with more than 500 sequences and more than 3,000 columns asks before loading. A SubFam alignment of 600 rows × 300 columns is small for it.
  • Reorder by similarity sorts rows by the k-mer tree (k = 6 by default), so blocks of similar rows become visible.
  • Consensus line and group consensus (Consensus menu), the Clustering panel above, and in-browser MAFFT to realign a selected block.
  • Sharing by link. An alignment on the web opens with https://toki-bio.github.io/MSA-viewer/?url=<address of the FASTA>&title=<title>. The README links a starter example of 40 SINE copies in 4 groups: try Group by k-mer with both methods on it.

SINEderella: the whole workflow

SINEderella (lab tool, public) is the orchestrator: one command that runs the search, extraction, assignment and report for a genome and a bank of consensus sequences. Its stated aim (docs/AIM.md): the subject is interspersed repeats, copies with unique flanks; the output is (1) the list of SINE loci in the genome and (2) an HTML page with links to alignments, conclusions on alignment parts, copy numbers, leak and divergence from consensus, produced by the workflow itself, on every run, without manual checking. Copies whose flanks are shared with other copies (segmental duplications, units of tandem arrays) are not the subject: they are never shown in alignments, at most counted.

  1. Clean the inputs
    Genome and consensus FASTA headers and line endings are normalised; the bank is checked for reverse-complement duplicates; an assembly check runs (it never blocks).
  2. Step 1: search and extract
    Each consensus is searched with sear (section 02): a hit must cover at least 80 % of the consensus length at 65 % similarity or more (the README's table still says 0.9; the manual and code use 0.8). Hits are merged, SINE-derived satellites and tandem arrays are taken out (section 06), copies are extracted, and a random sample of up to 30,000 goes through SubFam (chunks of 50).
  3. You: review the SubFam alignment
    Not automatic. When no good subfamily set exists yet, you peel the chunk-consensus alignment, build one consensus per subfamily with SINE_consensus, and re-run with the new bank (SINEderella --add adds consensuses to an existing run). For rare subfamilies, step1c_deplete.sh runs SubFam with chunks of 20 on the copies the bank explains worst.
  4. Step 2: assignment ("asSINEment")
    Every copy is compared with every consensus by ssearch36 (Smith-Waterman, section 02), 10 times. Because ssearch36 shuffles sequences for its statistics, scores wobble slightly between runs. A copy is assigned only if the same consensus wins all 10 times, and its bitscore is at least 0.45 × the bitscore of that subfamily's N-th best copy (N = 10, or fewer for a small subfamily).
  5. Step 3: per-copy measures
    sim_ratio = the copy's score against its consensus ÷ the consensus scored against itself (1.0 = identical). LEAK = the runner-up consensus reached at least 90 % of the winner's score: the copy sits between two subfamilies. CONFLICT = search hits of different consensuses overlap at this locus. Unassigned copies get a soft label from their best search hit; nothing is discarded.
  6. Step 4: plots
    Per subfamily: a histogram of divergence (100 − % identity of each copy to the consensus it was searched with), and the base frequencies at every consensus position.
  7. Flank checks and verdicts
    For each subfamily, flank windows are widened until pairs of copies are no more alike there than random genome pairs; for each copy, its flanks are compared with those of the other copies of its family. Every copy gets a verdict: SINE (assigned, flanks tested and unique), grey (flanks could not be tested) or not SINE (not assigned, or flanks shared). "A test that could not be done is not a pass."
  8. Report and plates
    An HTML report (results/report.html) with the subfamily table, divergence and links to "plates": alignments of the top 100 and a random 100 SINE loci per subfamily, with 50 bp of left and 70 bp of right flank, viewable in ViewAlign.

What a subfamily is in SINEderella. In practice, one consensus in the bank. Every consensus competes with every other as an equal: there is no family level yet. The manual (section 6.1.6) describes a two-stage design, families first, then subfamilies within each family, and marks it "not yet implemented". So the subfamilies a run reports are only as good as the bank you gave it, which is why the review step matters.

# full run: genome + bank of consensus sequences; creates run_YYYYMMDD_HHMMSS/
THREADS=32 SINEderella genome.fa dip_bank.fa

# add newly built subfamily consensuses to that run (re-assigns what they touch)
SINEderella --add new_subfams.fa --run run_20261031_120000

# drop a family from the run and re-assign its copies
SINEderella --exclude FamX_b2 --run run_20261031_120000
In our projects

In two rodent genomes (species A and a close relative) a bank of five consensuses of one SINE family, a SINEBase consensus and four published subfamilies, found more than a million loci per genome. About five in six were firmly assigned (10 of 10 votes), and one subfamily took most of them, in both species. Lab notes on the voting itself (on Timema SINEs, unpublished): three identical runs over 20,000 copies changed the winning consensus of 17 copies, which is exactly the instability the 10-of-10 rule filters out.

SINEplot: a map of copies between subfamilies

SINEplot (lab tool, public) draws every copy as a dot in a two-dimensional map. Input: ssearch36 scores of all copies against all subfamily consensuses (field 12, the bitscore). The subfamily centres are placed by MDS from how well the consensuses match each other; each copy is placed between the centres, pulled towards each in proportion to its score. A copy is assigned to its best subfamily; its confidence is its best score ÷ that consensus's score against itself, and its conflict ratio is second-best ÷ best score. In conflict mode the copies are marked solid (ratio below 0.50), moderate (0.50-0.85) or ambiguous (0.85 and above). It shows the same thing as SINEderella's LEAK flag, as a picture: copies floating between two centres. Despite the repository's one-line description, the plot is of scores, not a divergence histogram; the per-subfamily divergence histograms come from SINEderella's step 4.

# consensus names must be short (under 15 characters); copies are everything else
cat subfamilies.fa copies.fa > all_sines.fa
ssearch36 -m 8 -T 4 all_sines.fa all_sines.fa > scores.txt   # -m 8: tab-separated table
python SINEplot.py scores.txt -o sine_map.html -t "SINE subfamilies"

A classic alternative: COSEG

COSEG (version 0.2.4, 2024; distributed on the RepeatMasker website of the Institute for Systems Biology) identifies repeat subfamilies automatically from significant co-segregating mutations: mutation sites that occur together in the same copies more often than chance would allow, which is the "shared diagnostic pattern" idea put into statistics. Its download page credits Alkes Price for the original code. Price, Eskin and Pevzner (2004) described a method to identify and statistically validate subfamilies and applied it to human Alu, reporting 213 Alu subfamilies where RepBase Update then listed 31. We have not run COSEG on the SINEs of this lesson and describe it only from its download page and the 2004 paper's abstract.
