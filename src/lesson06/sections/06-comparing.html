06Comparing samples: differential methylation

Once every CpG of a genome has a methylation level (s03), the next question is usually a comparison: does this region differ between males and females, between two tissues, two populations, young and old animals, or the mother's and the father's copy? This section explains what the comparison tools compute, why the number of animals matters more than the number of reads, how a statistically "significant" difference can be biologically trivial, which hidden differences between samples (tissue, age, sex) can fake a result, and what epigenetic age clocks can and cannot do with the few animals a zoologist usually has.

In our projects. The lab has no methylation data to compare yet: none of our genomes has modification calls (s02), and the species A genome is a single male. Everything below therefore uses toy data and the tools' own examples. For population questions in the species A project we already concluded that more individuals had to be sequenced; the same holds for any methylation comparison (our inference, explained below).

The words

Methylation level
At one CpG in one sample: reads that call the C methylated divided by all reads with a call there (modkit's percent modified, s03). Over a region: the same ratio with the calls of all CpGs in it added up.
DML / DMR
A differentially methylated locus is a single CpG whose level differs between the groups; a differentially methylated region is a stretch of neighbouring CpGs that differ together. Regions are what biologists usually report, because neighbouring CpGs tend to share their state and one CpG alone is noisy.
Effect size
How big the difference is, in a unit you can interpret: here, level in group A minus level in group B (0.80 − 0.60 = 0.20, "20 percentage points").
Significance
How sure you can be that a difference is not chance. A p-value is the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if there were no true difference. Small p means "unlikely to be chance", not "large" or "important".
Biological replicate
Another animal of the same group. Not another read, not another library of the same animal (that is a technical replicate). Only biological replicates tell you how much animals of one group differ among themselves.
False discovery rate (FDR)
Among everything you call "different", the share that is not. Testing millions of CpGs at p < 0.05 gives tens of thousands of chance hits, so p-values are corrected, usually with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.
Simple explanation with examples: why 100 reads from one animal are not 100 animals

Imagine you want to know whether mice from the mountains are heavier than those from the lowlands. You catch one mouse from each place and weigh each of them a hundred times. The hundred weighings tell you very precisely what these two mice weigh. They tell you nothing about how much mice vary within a place, so you cannot say whether the difference belongs to the places or to the two individuals.

Reads are the weighings. At one CpG, 30 reads of an animal measure that animal's methylation level very well. If the level of this CpG differs naturally between animals (it often does, by tissue, age, sex, genotype, or for no known reason), a test that pools the reads of the group treats animal-to-animal differences as if they were group differences:

group A, 1 animal: 24 of 30 reads methylated = 0.80 group B, 1 animal: 15 of 30 reads methylated = 0.50 pooled-read test: p ≈ 0.015 "significant" but: the same CpG in three more animals of group A reads 0.55, 0.62, 0.71

With several animals per group, the test compares the spread between groups with the spread within groups, and the false signal disappears. The toy below lets you try this.

The workflow

  1. One table per sample
    For each animal (or each parental copy, s04): a bedMethyl table of CpG counts from the same pipeline, the same reference and the same basecaller model (s03, s07).
    modkit pileup · bgzip · tabix
  2. Look before testing
    Coverage per sample, overall level per sample, a clustering or principal-component plot of all samples: an outlier animal or a batch effect shows up here, not in a DMR list.
    modkit modbam summary · R / Python
  3. Explore: one sample against another
    Region scores, single-site scores and segmentation into "same" and "different" blocks. Quick and needs no replicates; a ranking, not a statistical answer about groups.
    modkit dmr pair · modkit dmr multi
  4. Test groups of animals
    A model of counts with animal-to-animal variation, other factors (sex, age, tissue) in the design, corrected for testing many CpGs.
    DSS · dmrseq · methylKit · metilene
  5. Filter by effect size and look at the data
    Keep DMRs that are both well supported and large enough to matter; plot the per-animal levels of each one you report.
    IGV · your own plots (s07)

1. modkit dmr: comparing bedMethyl tables

modkit dmr (modkit v0.6.4) compares bedMethyl tables made by modkit pileup. Its documentation states the purpose plainly: "to enable exploratory data analysis of methylation patterns", with scores that are "simple and interpretable". It has two subcommands and three uses:

  • dmr pair with -r regions.bed: one sample against another over regions you supply (CpG islands, promoters, repeat families from s05).
  • dmr multi: every pair among several named samples, regions only; with --ref-sample each sample is compared only with one reference sample. Samples given the same name are combined.
  • dmr pair without -r: single CpGs, optionally joined into segments by a two-state HMM (--segment). This is the mode for the two parental copies of one animal (s04).
# input: bgzip-compressed, tabix-indexed bedMethyl per sample (modkit 0.6.4 docs)
modkit pileup A1.bam - --cpg --ref asm.fa -t 16 --log-filepath A1.pileup.log | bgzip -c > A1.bed.gz
tabix -p bed A1.bed.gz

# 1) regions, one sample against another
modkit dmr pair -a A1.bed.gz -b B1.bed.gz -r regions.bed --ref asm.fa --base C \
  --header -t 16 -o A1_vs_B1.regions.bed --log-filepath dmr.log

# 2) all pairs of named samples over the same regions (-o is a directory here)
modkit dmr multi -s A1.bed.gz A1 -s A2.bed.gz A2 -s B1.bed.gz B1 -s B2.bed.gz B2 \
  -r regions.bed --ref asm.fa --base C --header -t 16 -o dmr_multi/

# 3) single CpGs, replicates repeated with -a / -b, plus HMM segments
modkit dmr pair -a A1.bed.gz -a A2.bed.gz -b B1.bed.gz -b B2.bed.gz --ref asm.fa --base C \
  --header --segment segments.bed -t 16 -o sites.bed --log-filepath dmr.log

--base C picks which modified base is compared (all modifications of C, for example 5mC and 5hmC, unless --single-code names one). --min-valid-coverage (default 0) drops sites with fewer valid calls. Use --header and read columns by name: the column order differs between region, single-site and segment output, and the docs' own example table has one more column than their schema list.

Segmentation. The HMM has two states, "Different" and "Same". Its emission comes from the score above (probability of "Same" = e−score). The probability of staying in "Different" is highest (--diff-stay, default 0.9) when the next CpG is adjacent and decays to the prior probability of a differential site (--dmr-prior, default 0.1) once the next CpG is --decay-distance (default 500 bp) away; sites more than --max-gap-size (default 5,000 bp) apart are not joined. The default "coarse" mode joins neighbours eagerly and can include unchanged CpGs in a "different" block; --fine-grained gives shorter segments.

Replicates in modkit are pooled reads, not a test across animals. With repeated -a/-b, the help text of --cap-coverages says that three replicates of 30× "would normally" give a total coverage of 90; dmr multi combines samples with the same name. The source code (modkit 0.6.4, single-site DMR) confirms it: the counts of all replicates of a condition are added up before the score, the MAP-based p-value and Cohen's h are computed, so the test has no term for how much animals of one group differ from each other. It also reports "balanced" values (each replicate first scaled to the same coverage, then added up) and, with equal group sizes, per-replicate-pair p-values and effect sizes, which show whether every pair agrees. Use modkit dmr to explore and rank; for a claim about groups of animals, use a method that models replicates.

2. Statistical tests across animals: DSS and others

DSS (Bioconductor, release 2.60.0) was written for bisulfite sequencing, but it needs only, per sample and CpG, the number of reads (N) and the number of methylated reads (X), which a bedMethyl table holds in columns 10 (Nvalid_cov) and 12 (Nmod). It models counts with a beta-binomial distribution, estimates the animal-to-animal spread (the dispersion) of each CpG while borrowing strength across all CpGs (a shrinkage estimator from a Bayesian hierarchical model; Feng et al. 2014), and tests each CpG with a Wald test.

# bedMethyl -> DSS input (chr, 1-based pos, N, X); check that column 4 is "m" in your file
zcat A1.bed.gz | awk 'BEGIN{OFS="\t"; print "chr","pos","N","X"} $4=="m" {print $1,$2+1,$10,$12}' > A1.dss.txt
# R, DSS 2.60 (function defaults read from the DSS source)
library(DSS)
ids  <- c("A1","A2","A3","B1","B2","B3")
dat  <- lapply(ids, function(s) read.table(paste0(s, ".dss.txt"), header = TRUE))
BSobj <- makeBSseqData(dat, ids)

# two groups; smoothing borrows information from CpGs within smoothing.span (default 500 bp)
dml  <- DMLtest(BSobj, group1 = c("A1","A2","A3"), group2 = c("B1","B2","B3"), smoothing = TRUE)
dmrs <- callDMR(dml, delta = 0.1, p.threshold = 0.01)   # defaults: delta 0, p.threshold 1e-5, minlen 50, minCG 3, dis.merge 100, pct.sig 0.5

# other factors in the design (Park & Wu 2016): test group while accounting for sex and age
design <- data.frame(group = c("A","A","A","B","B","B"), sex = c("F","M","F","M","F","M"), age = c(1,2,3,1,2,3))
fit  <- DMLfit.multiFactor(BSobj, design = design, formula = ~ group + sex + age)
test <- DMLtest.multiFactor(fit, coef = "groupB")
dmrs2 <- callDMR(test, p.threshold = 0.01)              # delta is not allowed with multifactor results

With delta = 0.1, callDMR scores each CpG by the posterior probability that the true difference exceeds 0.1 rather than by its p-value. A DMR must be longer than minlen bp, contain more than minCG CpGs, and at least pct.sig of its CpGs must pass. The vignette says it is very difficult to choose a natural and rigorous threshold for DMRs and recommends trying several. (In the source, dis.merge is lowered to minlen when it is larger, so with the defaults DMRs closer than 50 bp are merged.) The six-animal design above is a toy: with six animals and four terms (intercept, group, sex, age) only two degrees of freedom remain.

One animal per group. DMLtest refuses to run with one sample in a group unless smoothing = TRUE (or equal.disp = TRUE). The method for this case (Wu et al. 2015) uses neighbouring CpGs as "pseudo-replicates" to estimate dispersion. That makes the test possible, but it still cannot tell a difference between groups from a difference between the two individuals.

These tools were developed for bisulfite data. With long reads the counts come from a modification caller with its own error rate, and calls below a confidence threshold are dropped by modkit pileup (s03). The statistics still apply to the counts, but a difference in calling quality between samples (another basecaller model, much lower read quality) can look like a methylation difference.

Try it

Two groups of animals, 2,000 CpGs: what does a test find?

A toy genome of 200 regions with 10 CpGs each. Each CpG has a baseline level (most around 0.8, some near 0.1, as in a mammalian genome). In 20 regions (the true DMRs) group B differs from group A by the true difference. Every animal has its own level in every region, scattered around its group's level by the animal-to-animal variation (a standard deviation); reads per CpG follow a Poisson distribution around the coverage, and each read is methylated with the animal's probability. The test is either a pooled-read test (all reads of a group added up, two-proportion z-test; this is what a read-count test without replicates does) or a per-animal test (a moderated t-test on each animal's level; it borrows strength across units as DSS does for its dispersions, but it is a simple stand-in, not DSS itself). Both are corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg at FDR 5 %. The plot is a volcano plot: each dot is one tested region (or CpG), placed by its observed difference (left-right) and its strength of evidence, −log10 of the p-value (up; 2 means p = 0.01, 6 means p = 0.000001).

Test
Unit tested

What to notice. (1) Set animal-to-animal variation to 0: the pooled-read test is then fine, and more coverage helps it. (2) Put the variation back to 0.05 and switch to pooled reads: false positives appear, and raising coverage makes it worse, because more reads make every chance difference between a few animals look certain. (3) With the per-animal test, one animal per group cannot be tested at all, two gives little power, and adding animals helps much more than adding coverage beyond about 10×. (4) Regions find true DMRs that single CpGs miss, because the reads of 10 CpGs add up. (5) Set the variation to 0.02, the true difference to 0.05, 10 animals and 60× coverage: a difference of 5 points becomes "significant" in most true DMRs; the effect-size filter then decides whether you care. (6) Raise the variation to 0.10 and watch how many animals you need for the same power.

3. Effect size and significance are different questions

A p-value mixes two things: how large the difference is and how much data you have. With deep coverage and many CpGs per region, a difference of 1 to 2 percentage points becomes "highly significant"; with few reads, a difference of 30 points may not be. Report both, and decide in advance what size of difference you consider biologically meaningful (DSS's delta, modkit's Cohen's h lower bound, or a plain filter on the difference). 4. Hidden differences between samples (confounders)

A confounder is a difference between your groups other than the one you are studying, which also changes methylation. Every DMR list should be read against this list:

  • Tissue and cell composition. Each cell type has its own methylation profile. A tissue is a mixture, and if the mixture differs between groups (more white blood cells in sick animals, more fat in a liver sample), the levels differ without any cell changing. In human blood, cell composition changes with age and explains much of the variation in methylation, and it is tangled with age at the CpG level (Jaffe & Irizarry 2014). Compare the same tissue, sampled the same way.
  • Age. Many CpGs change with age; that is what epigenetic clocks use (below). If one population was sampled as juveniles and the other as adults, age-related CpGs appear as population DMRs.
  • Sex. In female mammals one X is inactivated and methylated on many promoters (s01, s04). Unbalanced sexes give X-linked "DMRs" and dilute others; analyse sex chromosomes separately (lesson 2, s12), or put sex in the design.
  • Genotype. A SNP that destroys a CpG in some animals removes the site in them (the read shows TG or CA, not CG). In a pooled comparison this looks like a loss of methylation; in a phased animal it shows up as an allele-specific difference (s04).
  • Batch and platform. Different flow cells, basecaller models, read quality or DNA extraction between groups. Sequence the groups mixed, not group after group, and record the basecaller and model for every sample (s07).

If a confounder cannot be avoided, put it in the model (DSS DMLfit.multiFactor), which needs more animals than terms. If it is perfectly tied to the groups (all population A animals are adults, all population B juveniles), no statistics can separate the two.

5. Epigenetic clocks: estimating age from methylation

An epigenetic clock predicts an animal's age from the methylation levels of a set of CpGs. It is built, not discovered: you need many animals of known age, measured at the same CpGs, and a regression that picks the useful sites.

  1. Training animals of known age
    Animals whose age is known independently (marked at birth, captive records, tooth layers). The age error of these animals sets a floor on the clock's accuracy.
  2. Methylation at many CpGs
    An array, targeted sequencing or whole-genome data. The mammalian methylation array used for cross-species clocks measures up to about 36,000 CpGs per species, chosen because their sequence is conserved across many mammals (Arneson et al. 2022).
  3. Elastic net regression
    A penalised regression of (transformed) age on all CpG levels; most coefficients become zero, leaving tens to hundreds of CpGs. Horvath's human pan-tissue clock (2013) used glmnet with alpha 0.5, lambda by cross-validation, and kept 353 CpGs.
  4. Test on animals not used for training
    Report the median absolute error in years and the correlation with true age. With few animals, leave-one-out cross-validation is often the only option.

Scale of the published examples: Horvath's clock was developed using about 8,000 human array samples of 51 tissues and cell types, with a median error of 3.6 years in test data (correlation 0.96). The pan-mammalian clocks of the Mammalian Methylation Consortium used 11,754 arrays from 185 species and 59 tissues (Lu et al. 2023). A review of wildlife clock design (Newediuk et al. 2025) notes that wildlife clocks "often rely on datasets with no more than a few dozen samples", that inaccurate training ages bias any estimate of age acceleration, that clocks are most accurate when the training animals resemble the animals to be aged (tissue, sex, population, age range), and that leave-one-out cross-validation estimated test error as well as a separate hold-out set in their polar bear data. A systematic review and meta-analysis across more than 40 species (Le Clercq et al. 2023) found methylation-based age estimates more accurate than telomere length.
