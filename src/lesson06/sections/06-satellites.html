06Satellite DNA: monomers, arrays and higher-order repeats

A SINE copy sits alone, between two stretches of unique DNA (05). Satellite DNA is the opposite: one short sequence copied head to tail, hundreds or millions of times, so that each copy's neighbours are more copies of itself. This section covers the vocabulary, how satellites are found (in reads and in assemblies), how the lab's CLsat workflow handles them, and why a satellite's copies must be turned to a common starting point before you compare them. It matters for a SINE lesson for two reasons: satellites can be built from pieces of SINEs, and a SINE search that does not recognise tandem arrays counts array units as if they were separate insertions.

Words you need

Tandem repeat
Copies of a sequence lying directly next to each other, all in the same orientation: ACGTTACGTTACGTT…. The opposite of interspersed repeats such as SINEs, whose copies are scattered (01).
Monomer (unit)
The one sequence that is repeated. Its length is the period. Monomers range from a few base pairs to several thousand. Usage of the size names varies between papers; roughly: microsatellites have units of a few bp, minisatellites tens of bp, and satellites proper usually have units of about 100 bp or more in long arrays.
Array
One uninterrupted run of monomers at one place in the genome. An array can be a few hundred bp or several megabases. The same satellite usually has many arrays, on many chromosomes.
Higher-order repeat (HOR)
A block of several different monomer variants (call them a, b, c) that is itself repeated as a unit: abc abc abc. Neighbouring monomers differ, but every a is almost identical to every other a. The classic case is human alpha satellite.
Centromeric satellite
A satellite in the centromere. In humans the centromere is defined by proteins, not by sequence: it is the part of an alpha-satellite HOR array bound by the kinetochore protein CENP-A.
Pericentromeric satellite
A satellite in the regions flanking the centromere (the pericentromere), part of the densely packed, gene-poor heterochromatin. Satellites can also be subtelomeric: near chromosome ends.
Satellite library
The set of different satellite families present in one genome. Related species share much of the library but differ in which families are abundant (next heading).

Rapid turnover and the library hypothesis

Satellites change fast. Fry and Salser (1977), working on kangaroo-rat satellites, proposed that related species carry a common library of satellite sequences, and that each species amplifies some of them and lets others shrink; this gives each species its own satellite profile. Ugarković and Plohl (2002) review the evidence: satellite families behave as independent units whose copy number and sequence both change, and in some groups the profile changes a lot in a short evolutionary time. Within one family, copies tend to become similar to each other across the genome (concerted evolution).

Melters et al. (2013) searched 282 species for high-copy tandem repeats and assumed the most abundant one is the centromere repeat, which held for most species with known centromeres. Such repeats were found in almost all animal and plant genomes, but their monomers varied widely in sequence and length (with long PacBio reads they found monomers up to 1,419 bp), and showed little sequence conservation beyond about 50 million years of divergence. In the complete human genome (T2T-CHM13), centromeric and pericentromeric repeats make up 6.2 % (189.9 Mb); each chromosome has one active alpha-satellite HOR array (HOR arrays tend to be large and homogeneous, often thousands of nearly identical HOR units), flanked by pericentromeric regions that can hold smaller arrays of diverged alpha-satellite monomers, other satellites (such as HSat2 and HSat3) and transposable elements (Altemose et al. 2022).

Satellites made from SINEs

Vassetzky, Kosushkin and Ryskov (2023) found tandem repeats built from parts of SINEs of three families (Squam1, Squam2, Squam3) in 56 squamate reptile genomes, and called them SINE-derived satellites (sSats). A locus counted as an sSat when it held more than four such tandem copies within 100 bp of each other. Units were roughly 70-290 nt (67 nt for the Squam3-derived sSat3 of snakes) and always shorter than the SINE they came from; each covers a piece of the SINE, for example the 3' part of the head with the B box plus part of the body. Snake sSats were similar in structure and copy number; geckos had a highly diverse set; agamids had none. The sSats are likely subtelomeric. The authors propose that the huge number of SINE copies raises the chance that one piece is duplicated in tandem (by unequal recombination) and then amplified: a "nonrandom process of satellite genesis originating from repetitive SINEs".

In our projects (unpublished lab notes, bat SINEs). In the horseshoe bat Rhinolophus sinicus, a search with the SINE consensus MEG-RS hit long arrays whose repeating unit is 2,155 bp (GC 67.6 %); only 107 bp of that unit resemble the SINE. The same bat has a satellite with a 140-bp monomer repeated 58 times that is built from the head and body of one of its tRNA-derived SINE families. Both would inflate SINE copy numbers if every array unit were counted as an insertion. This is one reason our SINE workflow keeps only copies with unique flanks and flags arrays (07).

Reading a dot plot

A dot plot compares a sequence with itself: position along the sequence on both axes, and a dot wherever a short word at position x is identical to the word at position y. The main diagonal is always full (every word matches itself). A tandem array adds parallel diagonals: a line one monomer length away from the main diagonal, another two monomer lengths away, and so on, filling a square block. In an HOR array the lines one HOR length apart are much stronger than the lines one monomer apart, because only whole HOR units are nearly identical.

Try it

A toy array in a dot plot

The toy builds random flanks, then an array of about 600 bp in the middle. You choose the monomer length and the HOR period (how many different monomer variants make one HOR unit; 1 = plain array, no HOR). The variants all descend from one random monomer and differ from it by the "variant divergence"; then every copy gets fresh point mutations. The dot plot marks every place where a word of the chosen length is identical in both positions. Below it, the offset profile shows, for each shift d, the share of array bases that equal the base d positions further on. Scaled down: real satellite monomers are usually 100 bp or more, and real arrays much longer.

Things to try: set the HOR period to 1 and the diagonals are evenly strong, one per monomer. Set it to 3 and raise the variant divergence: the lines one and two monomers away fade, every third line stays strong, and the tallest profile bar moves from the monomer length to the HOR length. Raise "mutations per copy" until the diagonals break into dashes; a shorter word recovers them but also fills the flanks with chance dots. This is how a dot plot (or a tool built on the same idea) separates the monomer from the HOR: both periods are real, at different levels.

Simple explanation with examples: monomer, array, HOR

Think of beads on a string. A monomer is one bead; an array is one string of beads. If all beads are the same colour, the string repeats every bead. If the beads come in a pattern red-green-blue, red-green-blue, the string repeats every three beads: that block of three is a higher-order repeat. Every red bead looks like every other red bead, but red and green differ.

A tool that asks "what is the repeating unit?" can therefore give two right answers: one bead (the monomer, about 171 bp for human alpha satellite) or one red-green-blue block (the HOR, several monomers long). Tandem-repeat finders often report the same array at several periods; it is up to you to say which level you mean.

On a dot plot, a single unique sequence shows only the main diagonal. A plain array shows a square full of evenly spaced diagonal lines. An HOR array shows the same square, but every third (or fourth, …) line is darker.

Finding satellites in reads

Long, nearly identical arrays are exactly what assemblers handle worst: reads from different places in the array look the same, so the assembly collapses or breaks them (07). Methods that work on unassembled reads avoid the problem.

RepeatExplorer2 and TAREAN
RepeatExplorer2 (Novák, Neumann and Macas 2020) clusters a small sample of short reads by similarity: each read is a point in a graph, reads are joined when they overlap, and groups of densely connected reads become clusters, each one repeat family. It runs on a Galaxy web server (repeatexplorer-elixir.cerit-sc.cz) or from the command line (GitHub kavonrtep/repex_tarean, also as a Docker image, tag 2.3.8). Its tandem-repeat part, TAREAN (Novák et al. 2017), recognises satellites by the shape of the cluster graph: reads from a tandem array overlap all the way round the monomer, so the graph closes into a circle; the circular shape is what flags a cluster as a putative satellite. TAREAN then rebuilds the monomer consensus from the most frequent k-mers in the cluster's reads. Because the reads are a random sample of the genome, the share of reads in a cluster estimates that repeat's share of the genome, without an assembly. The 2020 protocol also covers comparing several species and choosing satellite probes for FISH.
k-mer counting
The general idea: count every k-mer in the reads (lesson 01). Satellite k-mers occur thousands of times more often than single-copy k-mers, so they sit far out in the high-count tail of the k-mer spectrum. Chaining the most frequent k-mers that overlap each other spells out the monomer. TAREAN uses this step for its consensus; TRASH (below) uses repeated-k-mer density to find arrays in an assembly.

Finding satellites in assemblies

On an assembly, tandem-repeat finders find arrays without knowing the unit: TRF (a genome-wide table of arrays, units up to 2,000 bp), TRASH (monomers and higher-order structure of centromeric arrays) and TideCluster (groups arrays from the whole genome into families and builds consensus units with TAREAN). Their options and outputs are in lesson 03, section 04; a toy TRF is there too. Two points from that section matter here: TRF can report the same array at several periods (the monomer and multiples of it, as in the dot plot above), and an assembly only holds the arrays it managed to assemble.

The lab's CLsat workflow

The CLsat workflow (public repository Toki-bio/CLsat_workflow) is the code behind the lab's study of minisatellite-scale satellites (CLsat families) in Darevskia lizards; the manuscript is under revision. It is being turned into a reusable tool, working name satpipe, with one configuration file and 21 stages. It starts from whole assemblies and ends with a catalogue of array loci, each with a family, boundaries and orthology between species, plus a web viewer (CLsat-viewer). The stages, as the repository's documentation and scripts describe them:

  1. Find tandem repeats in every genome
    TRF on each whole assembly with 2 5 7 80 10 50 2000 -h (units up to 2,000 bp).
  2. Keep candidate satellite motifs
    From TRF's table, keep arrays with a period of 80-300 bp and at least 10 copies; each gives one consensus motif. The tool version adds a check, on by default, that cuts a motif that is really two or more units back to one unit (TRF can report an array at a multiple of its unit, for example period 292 for a 146-bp unit); the published run did not have this step.
  3. Drop motifs made of simple repeats
    TRF is run again on the motifs themselves; a motif with more than 50 bp covered by an internal simple repeat is set aside.
  4. Rotate every motif to a canonical start
    A monomer is a circle cut open at an arbitrary point, so the same unit can be reported starting anywhere. Each motif is rotated to its alphabetically smallest rotation, so identical monomers become identical strings. By default only the given strand is used; the repository records that one locus reported on the other strand never clustered for this reason and was added by hand.
  5. Cluster within each species, then across species
    VSEARCH --cluster_fast --id 0.90 per species; each cluster is aligned with MAFFT, rotated to a common start column and realigned; then all species are pooled with the known family consensuses placed first, so each known family anchors its own cluster.
  6. Assign clusters to families: a human decision
    Clusters were assigned to families by inspecting a UPGMA tree of the cluster centres. The repository stores this as a curated table, not as a computed result, and the pipeline stops and asks for it.
  7. Grow each seed into a whole array
    Each family member found by TRF is a seed. From each seed, the array is followed outward window by window (3 monomers per window) with ssearch36 against a query made of three tandem copies of the consensus, so any starting phase aligns. A window counts when it scores at least 50 bits with at least 0.67 of it aligned. Very long arrays switch to big jumps and then pinpoint the end.
  8. One locus universe, re-scored against every family
    Expanded intervals of all families are merged; every locus is scanned again with all family consensuses and given to the best one. A difference of 20 bits marks a contradiction, 15 an ambiguous call.
  9. Orthology from flanks
    600 bp of DNA on each side of each array (never inside another array), compared all against all with ssearch36; a pair needs at least 200 bp aligned at 80 % identity. All-against-all because an array can sit in opposite orientation in two assemblies, so a left flank can match a right flank. Same logic as SINE orthology (05).
  10. Subfamilies, array ends and a common frame
    The first two monomers of an array are aligned to doubled subfamily consensuses (two copies end to end), so the alignment works whatever the phase; the phase of the terminal monomer is read the same way. The last stage rotates every consensus the run writes into the frame of one anchor family.
satpipe check my.yaml                 # config, input files, external tools
satpipe run my.yaml --to 08_cross     # stop after cross-species clustering; inspect cross/cluster_report.tsv
                                      #   and write the cluster -> family table by hand
satpipe run my.yaml                   # run again: everything after that is automatic, resumable
satpipe viewer runs/my_run            # build the viewer pages for this run

The commands are from the repository README. It lists the outside programs needed: TRF, VSEARCH, MAFFT, FASTA (ssearch36), samtools and bedtools (to cut sequences and intervals out of genomes), GNU parallel (to run jobs side by side) and gawk (a text-processing language; the scripts need this version of awk). Every parameter left out of the configuration file takes the value of the published Darevskia run.

A common frame before comparing

Because a monomer is a circle, two copies of the same unit cut at different points look unrelated when aligned end to end: the alignment splits into two blocks. Before families are compared, their consensuses are rotated into one shared starting point (a frame), and monomers are then cut out of the arrays in that frame. Two working rules in the lab: rotate the circular consensus, then re-cut the copies with it, rather than rotating copies already cut from an array; and use a common frame only for families that are similar. For unrelated families "no common frame" exists; satpipe's anchor setting has an independent option for exactly this case, where each family keeps its own frame.
