01What a SINE is

A SINE is a short piece of DNA that cannot copy itself, yet sits in the genome in hundreds of thousands of copies. This section follows one SINE through its life: how it is read into RNA, how a partner element writes it back into a new place, what marks the insertion leaves, and why the copies of one family slowly drift apart. That life cycle is what makes SINEs useful to a zoologist.

Lesson 3, section 02 introduced the SINE as one class of transposable elements and showed its head-body-tail plan. Here we go one level deeper, because the rest of this lesson depends on it.

Definition

SINE stands for short interspersed element. SINEBase, the curated catalogue of SINE families (Vassetzky & Kramerov 2013), defines SINEs as relatively short (under 700 bp; its working criteria below allow up to 1 kb) non-autonomous retroposons: they encode no protein of their own. They are read into RNA by the cell's RNA polymerase III from a promoter inside the element, and written back into DNA by the reverse transcriptase of a partner LINE. Most SINEs are 150-300 bp long (Vassetzky et al. 2021).

SINEBase uses three working criteria to accept a sequence as a SINE:

  1. it is short (under 1 kb) and interspersed, not part of a tandem array;
  2. it has at least 100 copies in the genome (genomes poor in repeats, such as Arabidopsis thaliana, excepted);
  3. its 5′ part is at least 60 % identical over at least 60 nt to a tRNA, 5S rRNA or 7SL RNA gene.

Anatomy of a SINE copy

A SINE is built from modules. SINEBase: the 5′ head comes from a small RNA made by pol III; the body is of unknown origin or comes from the partner LINE; the 3′ tail is a run of simple, often degenerate repeats. Every genomic copy is also flanked by two short target site duplications, which are not part of the SINE but a scar of its insertion. Head
The 5′ part, descended from one of three small pol III RNAs. tRNA is the ancestor of most SINE families. 7SL RNA (the RNA of the signal recognition particle) gave rise to primate Alu and rodent B1, after a central deletion of the 7SL sequence; outside supraprimates such SINEs were known only from hagfish, until our lab found Urop in a shrew mole (Kosushkin et al. 2026, below). 5S rRNA gave rise to several SINE families of fish, lizards, rodents, bats and butterflies (Vassetzky et al. 2021).
Boxes A and B
The internal pol III promoter of a tRNA or 7SL head: two 11-bp boxes spaced by 30-40 bp (Vassetzky et al. 2021). Because the promoter lies inside the element, every copy carries its own promoter wherever it lands. A 5S rRNA gene has a different internal promoter (box A, an intermediate element and box C), so a 5S-derived head has no B box.
Body
Of unknown origin, or taken from the 3′ end of the partner LINE, so that the SINE RNA "looks like" LINE RNA to the LINE's enzyme. Some bodies share a core domain with distant SINE families (SINEBase).
3′ end and tail
Most mammalian SINEs end in an A-rich tail, like the 3′ end of L1; the L1 enzyme needs an A-rich RNA end to start copying (Vassetzky et al. 2021; Dewannieux et al. 2003 showed that the poly(A) of Alu is essential for its mobility). In "T+" SINEs (B2, Dip, Ves and nine other mammalian families) the tail is preceded by AATAAA polyadenylation signals and a short pol III terminator, TCTTT in B2 or TATTT in Dip and Ves. Elsewhere, for example in the eel, the SINE's 3′ end is a copy of its partner LINE's 3′ end, and that shared sequence is what the LINE enzyme recognises (Kajikawa & Okada 2002).
Target site duplication (TSD)
A short stretch of the host DNA that appears on both sides of the copy. It is made during insertion (next figure), so it belongs to the locus, not to the SINE family.

Life cycle: copy and paste through RNA

  1. Transcription.
    Pol III reads a genomic SINE copy into RNA, starting from the promoter in the head. In T+ SINEs it stops at the terminator; in SINEs without one (Alu, B1, ID) the A-run is read too and transcription stops further downstream at random runs of T (Vassetzky et al. 2021). Only pol III transcripts serve as templates for new copies; a SINE read as part of a gene intron by RNA polymerase II does not.
  2. Recognition by a partner LINE.
    The SINE RNA borrows the protein made by an active LINE: in mammals L1, which has an endonuclease and a reverse transcriptase. Marked Alu copies moved in human cells only when L1 was present, and 100-1,000 times more often than control messenger RNAs (Dewannieux et al. 2003).
  3. Target-primed reverse transcription (TPRT).
    The endonuclease nicks one DNA strand at the target. The free 3′ end of the nicked strand is used as the primer to reverse-transcribe the RNA, and only after that is the second strand cut (Luan et al. 1993, shown for the R2 element of the silk moth and proposed for LINEs and SINEs). In mammals the first nick falls mostly at a TTAAAA-like site; the second, on the other strand, at a variable distance, preferably within 15-16 bp (Jurka 1997).
  4. Repair: the TSD appears.
    The two nicks are staggered, so after the new DNA is joined in and the gaps are filled, the stretch between the nicks exists twice, once on each side of the new copy. TSDs of varying lengths flanked the new Alu insertions in the cell experiment (Dewannieux et al. 2003).

Families, subfamilies and why copies drift apart

All copies that share one origin and the same set of modules form a SINE family (SINEBase; the tail may differ). Within a family, not every copy is a source of new copies. The master gene model (Deininger et al. 1992) says that a family is amplified from a very small group of active "master" copies, while the great majority behave like dead pseudogenes. When a master copy itself changes and keeps making copies, all its offspring share the change: they form a subfamily. Published subfamilies are often "reliably distinguished by relatively long indels" (indels), as in B2, Dip and Ves (Vassetzky et al. 2021). Section 04 shows how we find them.

A new copy is, at birth, the same sequence as the master RNA it was copied from. After that it is just a piece of host DNA: it mutates at the neutral rate (lesson 3, s02), its TSD copies mutate independently of each other, and its poly(A) tail shortens over host generations (Vassetzky et al. 2021). Young copies are therefore nearly identical to each other and to the consensus; old ones are diverged. Genomic copies of old SINE families can differ by 35 % or more.

Simple explanation with examples: a rubber stamp that needs someone else's hand

Picture a rubber stamp (the SINE) with its own ink pad built in (the promoter in its head). It can make a print (an RNA), but it has no hand to press the print onto paper. A LINE is the hand: it makes the cut and presses the copy into the genome. The SINE borrows that hand by looking, at its tail end, like the LINE's own RNA.

Where the hand presses, it tears the paper in two slightly offset places. When the tear is mended around the new print, the few letters between the tears are written twice, once on each side: the target site duplication.

Each print then fades on its own. Prints made last year are crisp and almost the same; prints made long ago are smudged differently. Put a dozen old prints on top of each other and read the letter most of them show at each place: you get the original stamp back. That is the consensus.

Why SINEs are good markers

A SINE insertion at one particular site of the genome is a character that is either present or absent. Shedlock & Okada (2000) credit "the irreversible, independent nature of their insertion": each insertion happens on its own at its own site, so the same family inserting at exactly the same site in two lineages is very unlikely; and once in place, a copy is not removed precisely. An insertion shared by two species at the orthologous site is therefore a shared derived character. Homoplasy is rare (Kosushkin et al. 2023).

"Rare" is not "never". Ray et al. (2006) went through all published primate Alu studies and found very low levels of the three confounding events: incomplete lineage sorting, parallel insertions at the same site, and precise excision. They titled the paper "SINEs of a nearly perfect character". That is why studies use many loci and look for conflicts, not a single insertion. Section 05 shows how presence and absence are scored on whole genomes.

Pitfalls when you judge a candidate

  • Boxes A and B are evidence, not proof. SINEBase found that pol III promoter searches, even with position frequency matrices, give many false positives and misses. The test that counts is similarity to a real tRNA, 5S or 7SL gene (60 % over 60 nt).
  • A TSD is a bonus fact, not proof. TSDs fade with age (B2e above: 23 %), and other transposable elements make them too. Decide from the alignment of many copies.
  • Not detected is not absent. Standard RNA-gene finders are tuned to keep SINEs out of tRNA gene lists, so they miss decayed heads (see the bat example below). Write "no tRNA head detected at this threshold", never "not tRNA-derived".
  • An RNA pseudogene is not a SINE. A reverse-transcribed copy of a cellular tRNA or 5S rRNA is a pseudogene. SINEs usually carry a body, share substitutions with their own source copy rather than with the cellular RNA gene, and greatly outnumber RNA pseudogenes (SINEBase).
  • Results are family-specific. Lengths, box positions, TSD lengths and the share of copies with TSDs differ between families. A number measured on one family (for example Tal of moles or B2 of mice) does not describe SINEs in general.
In our projects

A rodent SINE family. Species A, a rodent, and a close relative with a published chromosome-level genome share one tRNA-derived SINE family that has a published consensus and published subfamilies. The consensus sequences we searched their genomes with are about 200 to 270 bp long. Section 02 shows how their copies were found.

A 7SL SINE in a mole. In the gracile shrew mole Uropsilus gracilis we found about 280,000 copies of Urop, a dimeric 7SL-derived SINE absent from five other talpids (moles). Like Alu, it is a dimer of two 7SL-derived monomers, and the deletion in its 7SL part has the same boundaries as in Alu; it has three subfamilies (a-c), and the youngest, Urop_c, has an excess of very long pure poly(A) tails (Kosushkin et al. 2026, BMC Biology).

Heads the standard tools miss. In SINE families we found in the bat Rhinolophus sinicus, every unit's head matched a tRNA-Ile gene at 73-93 % identity over 66-75 nt, far above shuffled-sequence controls; yet the Rfam models missed four of these heads and tRNAscan-SE missed two (lab notes, unpublished).
