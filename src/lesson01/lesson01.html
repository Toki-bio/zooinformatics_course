Field guide · before you run an assembler

Pre-Assembly Read Profiling

Before assembling a genome, find out what you are dealing with. Raw short and long reads answer six questions:

  • Are the reads good enough? How many bases you have, how long and how accurate the reads are, and whether they still contain adapter sequence.
  • How big is the genome? The length of one chromosome set in bases. This is the length the assembly should reach.
  • How heterozygous is it? The share of positions where the chromosome copy from the mother differs from the copy from the father.
  • What is the ploidy? How many chromosome sets each cell carries: two in a diploid, three in a triploid, four in a tetraploid.
  • How repetitive is it? The share of the genome made of sequences present in many copies, such as transposons and satellite DNA.
  • Is anything foreign mixed in? DNA from bacteria, parasites, gut contents or the people who handled the sample, and the abundant mitochondrial DNA.

Each answer changes which assembler you choose and how you set it. Laboratory measurements of DNA amount and chromosome number give an independent check.

Key ideas

The page uses a small set of terms. They are defined here once, with a picture of the central idea: counting k-mers.

Read
The sequencer's readout of one library molecule (genomic DNA broken into fragments, adapters attached, often copied by PCR). It is an imperfect copy of one genomic location and carries sequencing errors. A paired-end fragment gives two reads; a HiFi read is the consensus of many passes over one molecule.
Short reads
Illumina (or MGI/DNBSEQ, Element) reads, usually 150 bases, with about 0.1–0.5 % errors. Usually paired-end: two reads (files R1 and R2) from the two ends of the same fragment.
Long reads
PacBio HiFi: 15–25 kb, about 99.9 % accurate. Oxford Nanopore (ONT): from a few kb to over 100 kb; accuracy depends on the pore version and on the basecaller (the software that turns the electrical signal into bases), and is about 99 % or better with the current R10.4.1 pore.
bp, kb, Mb, Gb
Base pairs; thousands, millions and billions of bases.
Coverage (depth)
How many reads cover each position on average: total sequenced bases ÷ genome size. 30 Gb of reads for a 1 Gb genome is 30× coverage.
Base quality (Phred Q)
The sequencer's confidence in a base: Q = −10 · log10(error probability). Q20 = 1 error in 100 bases, Q30 = 1 in 1,000, Q40 = 1 in 10,000.
k-mer
A substring of fixed length k taken from a read (figure, middle). Counting k-mers needs no reference genome, which makes it the standard way to profile a genome from raw reads.
k-mer spectrum
The histogram of k-mer counts: how many different k-mers were seen once, twice, three times and so on (figure, right). Its peaks reveal coverage, genome size, heterozygosity and repeats.
Haplotype
The sequence of one chromosome set. A diploid has two, one from each parent.
Heterozygosity
The share of positions where the haplotypes differ. Humans: about 0.1 %. Many wild species: 0.5–2 % or more.
Ploidy
The number of chromosome sets per cell.
Repeats
Sequences present in many copies: transposable elements, and satellite DNA (a short unit repeated head to tail many times). Repeats are the main reason assemblies break.
Genome size, C-value
Genome size is the length of one chromosome set in bases. The C-value is DNA mass per nucleus: 1C is the DNA of an unreplicated gametic nucleus, 2C that of an unreplicated body-cell nucleus, whatever the ploidy. In a diploid, 1C equals one chromosome set. In a tetraploid, 1C holds two sets, and the size of one set is the monoploid size (1Cx). 1 picogram (pg) of DNA = 978 Mb.
Contig, scaffold
A contig is a continuous sequence assembled from overlapping reads, with no gaps. A scaffold is a chain of contigs placed in order, with gaps (runs of N) between them. The assembly is the full set.
N50
The length at which half of all bases sit in pieces (reads or contigs) of that length or longer. Shown step by step, with L50 and N90, in Measuring contiguity.
Model fit
Tools such as GenomeScope do not measure the genome directly. They fit an idealised curve to the k-mer spectrum and read size and heterozygosity off that curve, so a poor fit gives wrong numbers.
Why is the error spike so tall when reads are 99.8 % accurate?

Each distinct k-mer adds 1 to the height of one bar, and how often it was seen decides which bar. A real k-mer seen 35 times adds 1 to the bar at x = 35. A k-mer containing an error, seen once, adds 1 to the bar at x = 1. Nothing is lost: the 35 sightings are kept as the bar's position, and they come back in every size calculation as x · n(x) (35 × the bar height). But the bar heights count k-mers, not sightings, and every sequencing error creates up to k new k-mers that exist nowhere else.

How one error spoils k-mers. Every k-mer that overlaps the wrong base contains it, so each of them is a k-mer that exists nowhere in the genome.

Try it. Set the error rate and watch the two parts of the spectrum. Bars at 1–2 are k-mers containing an error; the bell is the k-mers copied correctly.

So the spike is many times taller than the real peak, yet holds little sequence, which is why cutting at the valley loses almost nothing. Every real spectrum has it; GenomeScope and KAT clip the y axis so the peaks stay readable. It grows with coverage (errors accumulate, distinct real k-mers do not) and with the error rate (raw ONT), and is smallest for HiFi.

Why does the peak sit at the coverage? Step by step

Two statements on this page seem to clash: "one read contains many k-mers" and "each sighting of a k-mer is one read". Both are true. They describe the same table read in two directions: along a read, and along one k-mer. Parts A–G below use a toy genome with reads of 10 bases and k = 5.

A. One read gives many k-mers. A 10-base read cut into 5-mers gives 10 − 5 + 1 = 6 k-mers, one starting at each of its first six bases. This is the "many k-mers per read" direction.

B. Now follow one k-mer. Take CGATT, which sits at genome positions 13–17. A read "contains" it only if the read covers all five of those positions. A read that covers four of them has a different 5-mer there, not CGATT.

C. For one k-mer, each read gives at most one sighting. If CGATT occurs once in the genome, a read either contains it once or not at all. So: sightings of CGATT = number of reads that start inside its 6-position window. This is the "k-mers = reads" direction. It holds for one particular k-mer, not for k-mers in general.

D. How many reads start inside the window? In this toy, reads start at random places. Real libraries come close, but GC content and PCR make some regions more covered than others. With N reads on a genome of G positions, on average N ÷ G reads start at each position. The window has L − k + 1 positions, so on average N × (L − k + 1) ÷ G reads start inside it.

E. Rewrite that as coverage. Read coverage is C = N × L ÷ G (all bases sequenced ÷ genome size). Substituting gives sightings per k-mer = C × (L − k + 1) ÷ L: the read coverage, scaled down slightly because a read gives L − k + 1 k-mers, not L.

toy: 36 reads of 10 bases on a 60-base genome read coverage C = 36 × 10 / 60 = 6× sightings per k-mer = 36 × 6 / 60 = 3.6 = 6 × (10 − 5 + 1) / 10

F. Do it for every k-mer at once. Put reads in rows and k-mer positions in columns, and mark which read contains which k-mer. Every row has exactly 6 marks (A). Each column has a random number of marks averaging 3.6 (B–E).

G. Why this gives the genome size. Rows and columns count the same marks. By rows: 36 reads × 6 = 216 sightings. By columns: 60 k-mers × 3.6 = 216. So 216 ÷ 3.6 = 60 recovers the genome length. That is the size formula used in step 4 of the workflow: total sightings ÷ sightings per k-mer (the peak).

Do repeats within a read, k-mer order or DNA strand bias the counts?

A k-mer occurring several times in one read. Counters count every occurrence, not the number of reads. That is correct: two occurrences in a read mean two places in the genome, and each place is seen ~16 times.

read: ...CACACACACA... k = 4 k-mers: CACA ACAC CACA ACAC CACA ACAC CACA → CACA ×4, ACAC ×3 in this read the genome has 4 positions starting with CACA, each seen ~16× → total ~64 → lands at 4× the peak (a repeat)

Counting "reads that contain it" instead would make this repeat look like a single-copy k-mer and break the size calculation (sightings ÷ peak).

Order (k1 then k2 in one read, k2 then k1 in another). Counting ignores neighbours: the reads become an unordered collection of k-mers. Two different orders mean two different places in the genome, and each k-mer is counted at each place. Order matters for assembly, not for the spectrum.

The two DNA strands. A read from the other strand contains the reverse complement of each k-mer, in reverse order. Canonical counting (Jellyfish -C; default in KMC and Meryl) files a k-mer and its reverse complement under one name, so both strands add to the same counter:

top strand read: ...GATTA... → GATTA bottom strand read: ...TAATC... → TAATC = reverse complement of GATTA canonical: both counted as GATTA (the alphabetically first of the pair) without -C: GATTA ~8× and TAATC ~8× as separate entries → the peak moves to half the coverage

Neighbouring k-mers share k − 1 bases and are seen by nearly the same reads, so their counts are correlated. This does not move the peak; it only means the k-mers are not independent data points.

One k-mer, one place in the genome, many reads

Ideally, one distinct k-mer corresponds to one place in the genome. It is seen once in every read that covers all k of its bases, so its count is the k-mer coverage there, a little below the read coverage. Each read contains it once; the total is many reads, not one. The other peaks of the spectrum are exactly the cases where this one-to-one link breaks.

Casek-mer ↔ place in genomeTimes seenWhere it lands
Unique sequence (the ideal)1 k-mer ↔ 1 place≈ coveragemain (homozygous) peak
Heterozygous site1 place ↔ 2 k-mers, one per parental copy≈ ½ coverage eachhet peak
Repeat with n copies1 k-mer ↔ n places≈ n × coveragerepeat peaks at 2×, 3×, …
Sequencing error1 k-mer ↔ no place; comes from one read1–2error spike

From the read's side the link runs the other way: one read holds L − k + 1 k-mers, one for each of the L − k + 1 consecutive places where a k-mer can start inside it.

Step 1Expected size and ploidy, without sequencing

Genome size and heterozygosity from k-mers are model fits (see Key ideas). They fail in two predictable ways. k-mers from high-copy repeats get lost, so the size comes out too small. And the fitted curve can lock onto the wrong peak, which doubles or halves the size. A physical measurement of the DNA in a nucleus, and a chromosome count, give independent numbers. They also give the assembly length to aim for. Look up your species or a close relative first; measure only if nothing exists.

Why k-mer size estimates go wrong: two toy examples

A k-mer estimate of genome size is one division: all k-mer sightings ÷ sightings per genome position. The second number is the coverage of the homozygous peak, the peak made by k-mers present on both chromosome copies (see step 4). Each of the two numbers can go wrong in its own way.

1. High-copy repeats: sequence disappears from the top of the division. A toy genome of 1,000 Mb, sequenced to a k-mer coverage of 35×:

part of the genome length distinct k-mers each seen k-mer estimate unique sequence 850 Mb 850 million ~35× counted low-copy repeats (2–4 copies) 50 Mb ~17 million 70–140× counted satellite: 1 kb unit × 100,000 100 Mb ~1,000 ~3.5 million× lost ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── k-mer estimate ≈ 900 Mb flow cytometry: 1,000 Mb → 10 % too small

The satellite holds 100 Mb of sequence, but because all its copies are nearly identical it yields only about a thousand distinct k-mers, each seen millions of times. Two things then drop it. Counters cap the histogram: Jellyfish histo -h and KMC -cx merge or discard everything above the cap. And GenomeScope's model describes unique sequence plus k-mers in a few copies, and GenomeScope2 ignores k-mers above its own maximum coverage setting (-m), so k-mers above any of these caps never enter the length it reports. The larger the share of satellites and young, nearly identical transposon copies, the bigger the shortfall: 10–20 % is common, and satellite-rich genomes can come out much smaller still.

2. The wrong peak: the bottom of the division is off by a factor of two. The same total of 35 billion sightings, divided by three different "homozygous peak" values:

peak taken as homozygous genome size when this happens 35× (true) 35 / 35 = 1.0 Gb correct 17.5× (the het peak) 35 / 17.5 = 2.0 Gb heterozygosity above ~1.5 %: the het peak is the tallest, and the fit takes it for the main peak 70× (2-copy repeats) 35 / 70 = 0.5 Gb the true homozygous peak is taken for the het peak: an inbred or haploid sample run as diploid, or the wrong -p

Which problem do you have? Compare the k-mer estimate with cytometry or a database C-value. A result close to ×2 or ×0.5 points to the wrong peak; a result 10–30 % too low points to repeats. Then check the coverage on its own: the expected k-mer coverage of one chromosome copy is about (sequenced bases ÷ 2C in bp) × (L − k + 1)/L. With 40 Gb of 150 bp reads and 2C = 2.0 Gb, that is 20 × 0.87 ≈ 17×. GenomeScope2 (the profiling tool of step 4) reports this per-copy value as kcov; if its kcov is near 35 or near 8 instead, the fit is on the wrong peak.

Fixes. Wrong peak: rerun GenomeScope2 with -l set to the expected per-copy coverage (17 here) and the correct -p. Repeats: raise the histogram cap (histo -h 1000000, KMC -cx), and use the cytometry value as the expected assembly size.

Simple explanation with examples

The idea. Each nucleus is stained with a dye that binds in proportion to the amount of DNA, then nuclei pass one by one through a laser. Brighter means more DNA. Because the brightness scale has no absolute units, you add nuclei of a standard with known DNA content to the same tube and read off the ratio.

standard (chicken red blood cells) 2C = 2.5 pg peak at channel 250 sample peak at channel 450 ratio 450 / 250 = 1.8 sample 2C = 1.8 × 2.5 = 4.5 pg → 1C = 2.25 pg 1C in bases = 2.25 × 978 Mb = 2.2 Gb ← this is the assembly length to aim for

Ploidy is a separate question. Cytometry tells you how much DNA there is, not how many chromosome sets. Suppose a known diploid relative has 2C = 2.3 pg and your sample has 4.5 pg. That suggests a tetraploid, but a diploid with a genome that doubled through repeats would give the same number. A chromosome count usually decides: 2n = 48 against the relative's 24 points to four sets. Chromosome fission can mimic this, so check the shapes of the chromosomes too.

Comparing with k-mers. GenomeScope reports the size of one chromosome set (the monoploid, 1x size). In a diploid that equals the 1C value. In a tetraploid, 1C holds two sets, so divide by 2 before comparing. k-mer estimates usually come out somewhat lower than cytometry, because high-copy repeats fall outside the model; a gap of 10–20 % is common, and a much larger one means the k-mer fit, or the assumed ploidy, needs checking.

Step 2Check the reads

Short reads are judged position by position along the read. First, does quality fall toward the end? Second, do reads contain adapters? These are synthetic DNA attached to the fragments for sequencing; they appear in a read when the fragment is shorter than the read. Third, how many reads are duplicates: identical reads from copies of one molecule, made by PCR or by the sequencer's imaging, rather than independent molecules. Long reads are judged by their length distribution and per-read quality, because read length decides which repeats an assembler can cross. Always report yield in bases and divide by the expected genome size: that number is your coverage, and every later threshold depends on it.

Simple explanation with examples: N50, coverage, why plot bases

N50. Sort reads from longest to shortest and add up their lengths until you pass half of all bases. The read where you cross that line is the N50.

reads (kb): 10 8 5 3 2 2 total = 30 kb, half = 15 kb running sum: 10 18 ... 18 ≥ 15 → N50 = 8 kb

Read this as: half of your sequence is in reads of 8 kb or longer (pictures, L50 and N90: Measuring contiguity). A repeat longer than most of your reads cannot be crossed, so the assembly breaks there.

Why plot bases, not read counts. 1,000 reads of 500 bp carry 0.5 Mb; a single 100 kb read carries 0.1 Mb. A count histogram is dominated by the short reads, which matter least for assembly.

Coverage. Total bases divided by genome size: 30 Gb of reads for a 1 Gb genome is 30×. For a diploid, each haplotype gets half of that, 15×.

Short-read QC tools compared: FastQC, MultiQC, fastp and others

These tools do three different jobs. Report tools look at reads and change nothing. Trimming tools write new, cleaned read files. MultiQC aggregates: it reads the reports of other tools and combines them into one page, without looking at reads itself. A usual short-read run uses one of each: FastQC on the raw reads, fastp to trim (it also writes its own before-and-after report), and MultiQC over all of it.

Trimming: when and how much

Short answer: remove adapters early and lightly; in most cases skip quality trimming; always keep the raw reads.

  1. Look at the raw reads
    FastQC and seqkit on the untouched files, so you see what the sequencer delivered.
  2. Remove adapters and polyG; keep fastp's default filters
    fastp with adapter detection on and sliding-window quality trimming left off (its default). It turns polyG trimming on automatically for NovaSeq/NextSeq data. Its default filters also drop reads with over 40 % of bases below Q15, more than 5 Ns, or fewer than 15 bases; that is fine.
  3. Use the trimmed reads for everything after that
    k-mer counting, GenomeScope, Smudgeplot, Kraken2 and assembly. Build the Meryl database from the same reads, so Merqury (step 3) checks the assembly against the data it was built from.
  4. Archive and submit the raw reads
    SRA/ENA expect the original files, not trimmed ones.

Why adapters must go, even with excellent data. Adapter sequence enters a read whenever the DNA fragment is shorter than the read, which happens in part of every library. Adapter k-mers form a small high-coverage bump in the spectrum, and after assembly they count as read k-mers missing from the assembly, which slightly lowers Merqury completeness. PolyG tails are pure artefact. Removing both costs almost no real sequence.

Why quality trimming is usually unnecessary. A sequencing error only creates rare k-mers, which land in the error wall left of the valley and are excluded anyway. Heavy trimming lowers coverage, shortens reads (which lowers k-mer coverage further, by (L − k + 1)/L), and can remove some regions more than others, such as GC-rich stretches. Assemblers and polishers handle an occasional bad base themselves. The exception is a run where quality collapses well before the read end: then trim only that tail and keep reads of at least ~50 bases.

Long reads. HiFi: remove reads that still contain adapters (HiFiAdapterFilt), because they cause assembly artefacts; do not quality-trim, since HiFi reads are already filtered to Q20 or better. ONT: recent Dorado versions trim adapters during basecalling by default (check your version and settings); filter only mildly with chopper or Filtlong (for example, drop reads under 1 kb or below Q10), and use Filtlong's target-bases option to cut excess coverage rather than to chase quality. Long, slightly noisy reads are still the ones that cross repeats.

Step 3Count k-mers

Cut every read into k-mers (see Key ideas), count how often each distinct k-mer occurs, and make the histogram: how many k-mers occur once, twice, … n times. The histogram is the input to every profiling tool in step 4. All counters give the same histogram when k, canonical counting and the count caps are set the same; they differ in speed, memory use, and what else you can do with the stored counts (the k-mer database).

Simple explanation with examples: what a k-mer histogram is

A read of length L contains L − k + 1 overlapping k-mers. With k = 4:

read: ATGCGTACG (9 bp → 9 − 4 + 1 = 6 k-mers) k-mers: ATGC TGCG GCGT CGTA GTAC TACG

Sequence a genome at 30× and each genomic k-mer turns up about 30 times (a little less, since a 150 bp read yields only 130 21-mers). A sequencing error creates up to k new k-mers that occur nowhere else, so they are seen once or twice:

true: ATGCGTACG → ATGC TGCG GCGT CGTA ... seen ~30× error: ATGCTTACG → TGCT GCTT CTTA TTAC seen 1×

The histogram is then a table such as 30 9000000: nine million different k-mers were each seen 30 times. Canonical counting (-C) merges a k-mer with its reverse complement (ATGC and GCAT), because reads come from both strands.

Choosing k

k = 21 works for most genomes up to a few Gb with short reads. Very large genomes may need k = 23–25. HiFi reads tolerate k = 31–40 (the FastK tools commonly use 40). Merqury's best_k.sh <genome size> computes the k at which a random k-mer is unlikely to occur by chance:

k = log4( G · (1 − p) / p ), p = 0.001 → G = 1 Gb: k ≈ 19.9 · G = 3 Gb: k ≈ 20.7 · G = 30 Gb: k ≈ 22.4
Simple explanation with examples: why k = 21

There are 4k possible k-mers. If that number is small compared with the genome, k-mers repeat by chance and the spectrum shows nothing.

k = 11: 4^11 ≈ 4.2 million possible 1 Gb genome → each 11-mer ~240× by chance useless k = 21: 4^21 ≈ 4.4 × 10^12 possible 1 Gb genome uses ≤ 10^9 of them almost all unique k = 41: even more unique, but each error or SNP now spoils 41 k-mers, and k-mer coverage drops

(SNP: a single-base difference between haplotypes or individuals.)

So k is the smallest value that makes chance matches rare. Larger genomes need slightly larger k; accurate HiFi reads tolerate k = 31–40.

Too small or too big k

The rule is: take the smallest k at which chance matches are rare. Merqury's best_k computes exactly that, aiming for about 1 in 1,000 k-mers having a random twin elsewhere in the genome. Each direction away from it has its own cost:

  • Too small. Unrelated places share k-mers by chance. Unique k-mers are pushed into the 2× and 3× peaks, so the model reads them as repeats and duplications; het k-mers that happen to match elsewhere make heterozygosity look lower. At the extreme there is no peak at all.
  • Too big, cost 1: lower k-mer coverage. Coverage shrinks by (L − k + 1)/L. With 150 bp reads that is 87 % at k = 21, 67 % at k = 51 and 33 % at k = 101, so the peaks slide left toward the error spike.
  • Too big, cost 2: more k-mers ruined by errors. A k-mer is error-free with probability (1 − error rate)k. At 0.3 % errors: 94 % at k = 21, 74 % at k = 101.
  • Too big, cost 3: the het peak takes over. The share of k-mers touching a heterozygous site is 1 − (1 − h)k: at 1 % heterozygosity, 19 % at k = 21 and 64 % at k = 101. The het peak becomes the tallest, and the risk of fitting the wrong peak rises.
  • What a big k buys. Repeats shorter than k stop looking like repeats. That helps assembly graphs more than profiling.

Real genomes are not random: transposons and satellites share k-mers at any k. That shows up as repeat peaks, which is biology, not a fault of k.

Use ONT reads for k-mer profiling only if they are recent R10.4.1 reads called with the SUP (super-accurate) basecalling model, or duplex reads (Q20+). Older ONT reads make the error peak swallow the spectrum. Use short reads or HiFi for profiling whenever you have them.

Step 4Read the spectrum

In a diploid genome, k-mers present on both haplotypes occur at full coverage (the homozygous peak). k-mers covering a heterozygous site (a position where the two haplotypes differ) exist on one haplotype only, so they sit at half coverage (the heterozygous peak). Sequencing errors make a wall of k-mers seen once or twice. Repeats sit at 2×, 3× … the homozygous peak. The low point between the error wall and the first real peak is the valley. GenomeScope2 fits a curve made of these peaks and reports genome size, heterozygosity, repeat fraction and error rate.

Simple explanation with examples: where each peak comes from

Take a diploid sequenced at 40×, so each parental chromosome copy is covered 20×. At one position the two copies differ:

mother: ...GGACGTTAGCAATC... father: ...GGACGTTGGCAATC... k-mer not touching the site GGACGTT on both copies → seen ~35× (homozygous peak) k-mer covering the site CGTTAGC on mother only → seen ~17× (heterozygous peak) CGTTGGC on father only → seen ~17× k-mer with a read error CGTCAGC in one read → seen 1–2× (error wall) k-mer in a 2-copy repeat present twice per copy → seen ~70× (repeat peak)

(35× rather than 40× because only 130 of 150 positions in a read start a 21-mer. The k-mers are drawn as 7-mers to fit; the counts are for k = 21.)

Why a little heterozygosity makes a big het peak. At 1 % heterozygosity, 1 base in 100 differs. Each such site touches 21 k-mers, so the share of k-mers that cover at least one het site is 1 − 0.9921 ≈ 19 %. At 2 % it is 35 %, and since every het site makes two het k-mers (one per allele), the het peak becomes taller than the homozygous peak.

Genome size by hand

G ≈ Σx ≥ valley x · n(x) / homozygous peak coverage k-mer cov = read cov · (L − k + 1) / L

Exclude the error wall left of the first valley, multiply each coverage bin by its k-mer count, and divide by the homozygous peak. For a diploid, use the homozygous peak, not the het peak. With 150 bp reads and k = 21, k-mer coverage is 87 % of read coverage.

Simple explanation with examples: the size calculation

Every position in the genome starts one k-mer, and that k-mer is seen about as many times as the homozygous peak says. So the total number of k-mer sightings, divided by the sightings per position, is the number of positions.

bin (x) distinct k-mers n(x) x · n(x) 1–4 error wall excluded (left of valley) 17 40 million 0.68 billion 35 800 million 28.0 billion 70 45 million 3.15 billion ... sum after valley ≈ 35 billion sightings genome size ≈ 35 billion / 35 (homozygous peak) = 1.0 Gb

Het k-mers still add up correctly: each het site has two alleles, each seen ~17×, which together make the 35× one position should get. That is why you divide by the homozygous peak, never the het peak. If you divide by 17 by mistake, the answer doubles to 2 Gb, which is exactly the error GenomeScope makes when its fit picks the wrong peak.

Typical shapes

Simple explanation with examples: reading a smudge

Smudgeplot looks for two k-mers that are identical except for one base, the two alleles A and B of a SNP. Let n be the coverage of one chromosome copy (here 20×). Count how often each allele is seen:

copies A seen B seen A+B (y axis) B/(A+B) (x axis) diploid AB 20 20 40 = 2n 20/40 = 1/2 triploid AAB 40 20 60 = 3n 20/60 = 1/3 tetraploid AAAB 60 20 80 = 4n 20/80 = 1/4 tetraploid AABB 40 40 80 = 4n 40/80 = 1/2

The height of a smudge tells how many copies carry the SNP in total, and its left–right position tells the allele ratio. A diploid fills the AB smudge; a triploid's brightest smudge sits at ⅓; a tetraploid lights up AAAB and AABB at 4n. A faint AABB smudge in a diploid usually means duplicated regions, not tetraploidy.

Diploid or tetraploid? How to tell them apart

The trap. AB (diploid) and AABB (tetraploid) both sit at ½ on the x axis. Only their height differs: 2n against 4n. But the y axis is measured in units of n, the coverage of one chromosome copy, and Smudgeplot estimates n from the data itself. If it estimates n twice too high, the AABB smudge is labelled "2n" and a tetraploid is reported as a diploid. Smudgeplot's own code warns that its n estimate can be wrong and should be checked against GenomeScope.

Checks, in order of strength:

  • A smudge at ¼ (AAAB). A diploid cannot produce it: with two copies the minor allele is always ½. A clear smudge at ¼ means at least four copies. Likewise, a clear smudge at ⅓ means at least three.
  • An independent value of n. Reads come from all the DNA extracted. After discounting organelle and contaminant reads, the read coverage of one chromosome copy is roughly total sequenced bases ÷ 2C (in bp), whatever the ploidy. Multiply by (L − k + 1)/L to get n on the k-mer scale that Smudgeplot uses. With 2C from flow cytometry (step 1), compare: if the brightest smudge sits at about 2 × that n, it is AB (diploid); at about 4 × n, it is AABB (tetraploid).
  • GenomeScope at both ploidies. Run -p 2 and -p 4. Prefer the fit whose k-mer coverage agrees with the n above, not just the higher model-fit percentage.
  • Chromosome count. 2n twice that of diploid relatives settles it (step 1).
80 Gb of reads; flow cytometry 2C = 4.4 pg × 978 Mb = 4.3 Gb read coverage per copy ≈ 80 / 4.3 ≈ 19× → × 0.87 → n ≈ 16× k-mer coverage per copy brightest smudge at ½, total coverage ≈ 65× → 65 / 16 ≈ 4n → AABB, tetraploid (had it been at ≈ 33× → 2n → AB, diploid)

Auto- or allotetraploid. An autotetraploid (genome doubled within one species) has four similar copies, so AAAB and AABB are both strong. An allotetraploid (two species combined) has two distinct subgenomes: differences between them are fixed, so AABB dominates and AAAB is weak (use the allotetraploid button above).

The hardest case. For assembly, a strongly diverged allotetraploid behaves like a diploid with a large genome, and it is also the hardest case to detect. When the two subgenomes of an allotetraploid differ by more than a few percent, most k-mers from one subgenome have no single-base partner in the other. Then only the variants within each subgenome form pairs, at 2n and ½, and the plot looks diploid. GenomeScope reports a "haploid" size equal to both subgenomes together, so 2C from cytometry agrees with the diploid reading and cannot expose it. Only the chromosome count against relatives, or a high BUSCO duplication rate after assembly (see the VGP section), reveals it.

Step 5Screen for foreign and organelle DNA

Contaminants inflate the size estimate and assemble into extra contigs. Organelles (mitochondria, and chloroplasts in plants) carry their own small genome in tens to thousands of copies per cell, depending on tissue and extraction. Their k-mers are seen tens to thousands of times more often than nuclear k-mers, but there are few of them, so they form a thin tail at the far right of the spectrum, not a peak. Screen the reads now, then assemble the mitogenome (mitochondrial genome) separately. That lets you remove its copies from the nuclear assembly later and recognise NUMTs (pieces of mitochondrial DNA inserted into nuclear chromosomes).

Removing contaminant reads before assembly: BBSplit

BBSplit (part of the BBTools suite) maps every read against several reference genomes at once and sends each read to the file of the reference it matches best; reads that match none go to a separate "unmatched" output. For a de novo project you give it only the contaminant genomes you expect (for example human, PhiX, a known symbiont or food organism) and keep the unmatched reads for assembly. Use it when Kraken2 or kat gcp points to a specific contaminant with a known genome.

  • Risk: removing your own reads. Conserved regions such as rDNA, mitochondrial genes and highly conserved coding genes can match a related contaminant genome. Screening a mammal against the human genome at default settings will remove real reads. Set a high identity threshold (minid=0.97 or stricter), and compare the k-mer spectrum before and after: removal should shrink the contaminant bump, not the main peaks.
  • Ambiguous reads (equally good matches to two references): ambiguous2=toss discards them, best keeps the first best hit, all writes them to every matching file. With only contaminant genomes as references, every read that maps is removed from the unmatched output, ambiguous or not; ambiguous2 only decides which contaminant file it goes to.
  • Long reads: BBSplit is built for short reads. For HiFi or ONT reads, map with minimap2 to the contaminant genomes and remove reads aligned over most of their length, or screen contigs after assembly with FCS-GX.

BBSplit in nf-core/sarek. nf-core/sarek is a Nextflow pipeline for variant calling (finding SNPs and other differences) by mapping reads to an existing reference genome of the same species. It is not an assembly pipeline. Recent releases (documented for 3.11.0) include BBSplit as an optional contamination-removal step that runs before read mapping. Enable it with --tools bbsplit and list the contaminant genomes with --bbsplit_fasta_list; --bbsplit_index reuses a prebuilt index, and --save_bbsplit_reads keeps the split FASTQ files. Reads assigned to the main reference (--fasta / --genome) continue to mapping. Typical uses are xenograft samples (removing mouse reads from human tumour tissue grown in mice) and resequencing of a species with known contaminants. The same idea, applied in sarek's way, is a reasonable pre-filter before assembly, but sarek itself needs a reference genome of your species.

Step 6Plan the assembly

Assemblers can return one primary assembly (one sequence per region) plus alternate haplotigs (contigs from the second haplotype), or two separate haplotype assemblies. Phasing means assigning each piece of sequence to the right haplotype. Two kinds of extra data help with phasing and with building whole chromosomes: Hi-C (chromatin is cross-linked inside the nucleus, cut and re-joined, so each read pair joins two pieces of DNA that touched in 3D; most such pairs come from the same chromosome and the same haplotype) and trio data (short reads from both parents, used to sort the offspring's reads by parent).

Coverage rules of thumb (total bases ÷ haploid genome size)

  • Illumina for profiling: 30–60× gives a clean GenomeScope fit; about 25× is a practical minimum for a diploid.
  • PacBio HiFi: 25–35× for a diploid hifiasm assembly; more helps phasing at high heterozygosity.
  • ONT: 30–50×, with a share of ultra-long (>100 kb) reads if you want to span long repeats. R10.4.1 SUP reads can go into hifiasm (--ont) or Dorado correct first.
  • Hi-C or trio short reads: plan them now if heterozygosity is high or you need chromosome-scale phased haplotypes.
  • VGP minimum: at least 30× HiFi and 60× Hi-C, which the VGP calls "diploid coverage".

Measuring contiguity: N50, L50 and Nx

After assembly the first question is how many pieces the genome came out in. One number cannot say everything about a list of contigs of very different lengths, and the mean length is useless: a thousand tiny contigs drag it down even when most bases sit in a few huge ones. N50 answers a better question: half of all assembled bases are in contigs at least how long? The same calculation works for reads (read N50, step 2), contigs and scaffolds.

N50
The length of the contig that crosses the halfway point of the sorted, end-to-end ruler. Half of all bases are in contigs of this length or longer.
L50
How many contigs it takes to reach that halfway point: the rank of the N50 contig. It is a count, not a length. A good assembly has a large N50 and a small L50. (A few old tools swap the two names; check the units: bases or a count.)
N25, N75, N90, Nx
The same with the line placed at 25 %, 75 %, 90 % or any x % of the bases. N25 describes the longest contigs, N90 the tail. Nx can only fall as x rises. A large gap between N50 and N90 means much of the genome sits in small pieces.
NG50, NGx
The ruler is the expected genome size (from step 1 or GenomeScope), not the assembly length. This stops an assembly from looking better by dropping its short contigs, or worse by carrying duplicated haplotigs. If the assembly holds less than x % of the genome, NGx does not exist.
auN
The area under the Nx curve (Li 2020), equal to Σ length² ÷ Σ length. One number for the whole curve; it changes smoothly when a contig is joined or broken, whereas N50 jumps.
Contig N50 vs scaffold N50
Contig N50 counts gap-free sequence; scaffold N50 counts chains of contigs joined across gaps (runs of N), for example with Hi-C. VGP's "6.C" (see the VGP route) refers to contig N50 ≥ 1 Mb and chromosome-scale scaffolds.
Simple explanation with examples: N50 by hand, and what it does not tell you
contigs (Mb), sorted: 20 14 9 7 6 5 4 3 2.5 2 1.5 1 total 75 running total: 20 34 43 50 56 61 65 68 70.5 72.5 74 75 50 % of 75 = 37.5 → first total ≥ 37.5 is 43, reached by contig 3 (9 Mb) → N50 = 9 Mb, L50 = 3 75 % of 75 = 56.25 → first total ≥ 56.25 is 61, contig 6 (5 Mb) → N75 = 5 Mb, L75 = 6 90 % of 75 = 67.5 → first total ≥ 67.5 is 68, contig 8 (3 Mb) → N90 = 3 Mb, L90 = 8

N50 does not measure correctness. Joining two contigs that do not belong together raises N50. Check accuracy separately (Merqury QV, BUSCO, Hi-C contact maps).

N50 can be gamed. Throwing away all contigs under 50 kb shortens the ruler, so the halfway point moves into longer contigs and N50 goes up, while sequence is lost. NG50 against the expected genome size does not move. Compare assemblies of one species with NG50 or with the full Nx curve.

Commands. seqkit stats -a -T asm.fa (N50, N50_num = L50; add -N 90 for N90); gfastats asm.fa 1000000000 (contig and scaffold N50/L50, and NG50 when the genome size is given); quast.py asm.fa --est-ref-size 1000000000 (N50, L50, NG50, LG50, auN and the Nx plot).

The VGP route: today's reference standard

The Vertebrate Genomes Project (VGP) builds chromosome-level (one scaffold per chromosome), haplotype-resolved (both parental copies assembled separately) reference genomes for every vertebrate order. The VGP publishes its whole pipeline as public workflows for Galaxy, a free web platform for running bioinformatics tools (Larivière et al. 2024, Nature Biotechnology). Its pre-assembly stage is a short version of this page: k-mer profiling with Meryl and GenomeScope2, read statistics, and a Mash check that the reads come from the expected species.

Is it the gold standard? For chromosome-level reference genomes of eukaryotes, yes in practice. The VGP, the Darwin Tree of Life project, the European Reference Genome Atlas (ERGA) and the Earth BioGenome Project (EBP) all use the same core: HiFi contigs with hifiasm, Hi-C phasing and scaffolding, duplicate purging, contamination screening, quality checks with k-mers and conserved genes, and manual curation (a person inspects the Hi-C contact map and fixes wrong joins and breaks). Two limits apply. The EBP accuracy target of Q40 is a floor; good HiFi assemblies now reach Q50–60. Telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assemblies, with every chromosome complete from end to end and no gaps, go further by adding ONT ultra-long reads (verkko, or hifiasm with ultra-long reads) to fill the gaps left in centromeres (the chromosome's attachment point during division, usually long satellite arrays), rDNA and other satellites. The EBP standards still describe T2T as emerging, not routine.

The VGP Galaxy workflows, in order

VGP numbers its workflows WF0–WF9; the numbers are names, not the running order.

  1. k-mer profiling WF1, WF2
    Meryl and GenomeScope2 (WF2 also profiles the parents' reads). k = 21 for mammal-size genomes.
  2. Mitogenome WF0
    Assembles the mitochondrial genome from HiFi reads and marks its genes.
  3. Contigs WF3, WF4, WF5
    hifiasm with HiFi only, with Hi-C phasing (two haplotypes, hap1 and hap2), or with trio data.
  4. Purging WF6
    purge_dups, with coverage cutoffs taken from the k-mer profile; usually not needed with trio data.
  5. Scaffolding WF8 (WF7)
    YaHS orders and joins contigs into chromosomes using Hi-C. Bionano optical maps (images of very long DNA molecules labelled at a short sequence motif) are optional.
  6. Decontamination WF9

What the pre-assembly numbers feed in the VGP pipeline

  • GenomeScope2 genome size is the expected assembly length, and the denominator for the coverage check.
  • GenomeScope2 k-mer coverage sets hifiasm's homozygous coverage (--hom-cov) and the purge_dups coverage cutoffs. A wrong peak here gives a wrongly purged assembly.
  • The Meryl database is reused by Merqury after assembly for QV (consensus quality, on the same Phred scale as base quality), k-mer completeness (share of reliable read k-mers found in the assembly) and the spectra-cn plot (the read spectrum coloured by how many times each k-mer occurs in the assembly).

The quality target: 6.C.Q40

The Earth BioGenome Project (EBP) writes its standard as three numbers. A typical high-quality target is 6.C.Q40, together with at least 90 % k-mer completeness, at least 90 % BUSCO completeness and at least 90 % of the sequence assigned to chromosomes.

Simple explanation with examples: reading "6.C.Q40"
6 contig N50 ≥ 10^6 bp = 1 Mb (7 would mean ≥ 10 Mb) C scaffold N50 is chromosome-scale: scaffolds are whole chromosomes Q40 consensus quality: at most 1 error per 10^4 bases (Q50 = 1 per 10^5)

A published butterfly assembly reported as 6.7.Q55 has contig N50 of at least 1 Mb, scaffold N50 of at least 10 Mb, and about 1 error per 300,000 bases. It meets the contig and quality parts of 6.C.Q40. The "C" also requires scaffolds to be whole chromosomes, which a 10 Mb N50 alone does not prove.

QV is computed by Merqury from k-mers: an assembly k-mer that never occurs in the reads marks an error. This is why the read k-mer database built before assembly matters afterwards.

Running it in Galaxy

Galaxy is a free web platform for running bioinformatics tools on public servers (usegalaxy.org, usegalaxy.eu, usegalaxy.org.au and others) without installing anything. Every run keeps its parameters and tool versions, so the history doubles as a methods record. The VGP pipeline and most tools on this page are available there.

FCS-GX is the clearest case for Galaxy. Run locally, it needs the ~470 GB GX database and, for normal speed, a machine with about 512 GB of RAM. On Galaxy the database is already mounted on the server, so you upload the assembly, choose the taxonomic division (a broad group such as "vertebrates" or "insects"), enter the NCBI taxonomy ID (the number NCBI assigns to each species), and run NCBI FCS GX screen and then clean, followed by NCBI FCS Adaptor. The Galaxy Training Network tutorial "Screening assembled genomes for contamination using NCBI FCS" includes a ready-made workflow. If a run fails on usegalaxy.org, switch to the newest tool version first, as the Galaxy help forum advises.

  • How to start: on a Galaxy server, open Workflows → Public workflows (or WorkflowHub, or the IWC collection of reviewed Galaxy workflows) and import "VGP" or "NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen". The Galaxy Training Network (GTN) tutorial "Using the VGP workflows to assemble a vertebrate genome with HiFi and Hi-C data" walks through trajectory B on a small yeast dataset.
  • Getting data in: upload large files by URL or FTP rather than through the browser, and pull public reads straight from SRA/ENA.
  • Limits: each account has a storage quota and queue times vary, so tens of Gb of HiFi plus Hi-C can outgrow a free account. A common split is to run the heavy steps on your own server and use Galaxy for FCS-GX and other database-heavy steps.
  • Version caution: Galaxy tool versions can lag behind the command-line releases. Record the version shown in the dataset info for your methods.
