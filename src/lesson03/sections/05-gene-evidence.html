05Evidence for genes: transcripts and proteins

A gene predictor guesses where genes are from the DNA alone. It guesses much better when you also show it evidence: pieces of messenger RNA from the animal itself (RNA-seq, long-read transcripts) and proteins of related species laid onto the new genome. This section explains what a gene looks like in the genome, how each kind of evidence is produced and aligned, and which files the gene predictors of section 06 expect.

What a gene looks like in the genome

In a mammal, a protein-coding gene is not one continuous stretch of code. It is cut into pieces that are joined only in the RNA copy.

Exon
A piece of the gene that stays in the mature messenger RNA (mRNA, the RNA copy that leaves the nucleus and is translated into protein).
Intron
A piece between two exons that is copied into RNA and then cut out. Mammal introns are often thousands of bases long, many times longer than the exons.
Splicing, splice sites
Cutting out the introns and joining the exons is called splicing. Almost every intron begins with the two bases GT (the donor site) and ends with AG (the acceptor site). Rarer types are GC-AG and AT-AC. The point where two exons meet in the mRNA is a splice junction.
CDS
Coding sequence: the part of the mRNA that is translated, from the start codon (ATG, read as methionine) to the stop codon (TAA, TAG or TGA). It is read three bases (one codon) at a time, so its length is a multiple of 3.
UTR
Untranslated region: the parts of the mRNA before the start codon (5′ UTR) and after the stop codon (3′ UTR). They are in exons but do not code for protein. The first exon is often all UTR.
Isoform
One gene can be spliced in several ways, for example with or without one exon, or with a different last exon. Each version is an isoform (or transcript). Different tissues often make different isoforms.
Simple explanation with examples: a recipe with adverts in the middle

Think of a gene as a recipe printed in a magazine, interrupted by adverts. The recipe parts are the exons; the adverts are the introns. Before cooking, the cell cuts out the adverts and glues the recipe parts together (splicing). Every advert starts with the same two letters, GT, and ends with AG, so the scissors know where to cut.

DNA ATGGCTCAGgtaagt...advert...ttttcagGTTGAACTGgtgag...advert...cctagCCTTAA mRNA ATGGCTCAGGTTGAACTGCCTTAA protein M A Q V E L P stop

The title and the notes at the end of the recipe are not cooking steps: those are the UTRs. The cooking steps from "ATG" to the stop word are the CDS. A shortened version of the recipe, with one paragraph left out, is another isoform: liver might use the full recipe, testis the short one.

Why this matters for annotation: in the genome you only see the magazine with the adverts. An RNA read that runs across a glued joint ("split read") proves exactly where an advert was cut out. A protein of a related species tells you which recipe parts should be there, but not the title page (UTR) and not which version each tissue uses.

Three kinds of evidence

  1. Short-read RNA-seq
    mRNA is turned into cDNA, broken into fragments and sequenced as 100-150 bp Illumina reads. Many reads, cheap, but each read sees only a small part of one transcript. Reads that cross an exon-exon junction show the exact intron positions.
    tools: HISAT2 or STAR (mapping), StringTie (assembly)
  2. Long-read transcripts
    PacBio Iso-Seq or nanopore cDNA sequencing reads a whole mRNA molecule in one read, from 5′ end to poly(A) tail. One read shows a complete isoform, so no assembly is needed, but there are fewer reads per gene.
    tools: lima, isoseq, pbmm2 (PacBio); pychopper, minimap2 (nanopore)
  3. Proteins of other species
    Known protein sequences (from a close relative, or from a large database such as OrthoDB) are aligned to the genome across introns. They show coding exons and their borders, need no lab work, but say nothing about UTRs or tissue-specific isoforms, and miss genes your species has and the relatives do not.
    tools: miniprot; ProtHint inside BRAKER
  4. A related annotated genome
    Whole gene models of a relative projected onto your genome through a genome alignment. This is the topic of section 07 (Liftoff, TOGA2).

Planning RNA-seq

  • Tissues. A gene leaves RNA evidence only where it is switched on. The human tissue atlas (Uhlén et al. 2015, Science 347:1260419) needed transcriptomes of 32 tissues and organs to detect more than 90 % of the protein-coding genes. For annotation, pool or sequence several different tissues of the same species rather than many replicates of one.
  • Preservation. RNA degrades fast. The ERGA pilot guidelines (v2) state that tissue in RNAlater is sufficient for RNA-seq, and the listed sequencing centres ask for total RNA with a RIN above 8 for Iso-Seq (most list 300-500 ng as the minimum).
  • Depth. There is no single agreed number of reads for annotation. Depth decides whether weakly expressed genes and rare isoforms get any split reads at all; the toy below shows this. More tissues usually help more than more reads from one tissue.
  • Stranded or unstranded. A stranded library keeps the information which DNA strand the RNA was copied from; an unstranded one loses it. Strand matters where genes overlap on opposite strands and for UTRs. The BRAKER README says strandedness is not very important for gene prediction without UTRs, because spliced reads are "artificially stranded" by their GT-AG motif, but stranded data help UTR prediction. If nobody tells you which kind you have, RSeQC infer_experiment.py measures it from mapped reads (it needs a gene model file, for a new species for example protein-based models of conserved genes).

Mapping short reads: HISAT2 or STAR

An RNA read that crosses a splice junction lies in two places on the genome, often thousands of bases apart. A normal DNA aligner (BWA, or minimap2 in genome mode) cannot place it. A spliced aligner splits the read and writes the gap as an intron. In the SAM file this is the CIGAR operator N: the HISAT2 manual's example read 90M200N10M means 90 bases match, then a 200-bp intron is skipped, then 10 more bases match. Such a read is a split read (or spliced read).

Mask repeats first (section 04) and use the soft-masked genome; HISAT2 ignores lowercase masking, as the BRAKER README notes.

# HISAT2 2.2.3 (2.2.1 is the version listed by BRAKER 3.0.8)
hisat2-build -p 16 genome.fa genome_idx            # index; about 8 GB RAM for a mammal-size genome without --ss/--exon
hisat2 -p 16 --dta -x genome_idx \
       -1 liver_R1.fq.gz -2 liver_R2.fq.gz \
       --novel-splicesite-outfile liver.junctions.txt \
  | samtools sort -@ 8 -o liver.bam
samtools index liver.bam
# stranded library: add --rna-strandness RF or FR (paired), R or F (single-end); ask the provider or measure it
  • --dta ("downstream transcriptome assembly"): HISAT2 asks for longer anchors before it accepts a new splice site, which helps StringTie. BRAKER's GeneMark-ETP runs StringTie on your BAM, so BRAKER requires --dta for HISAT2 alignments: spliced reads must carry the XS strand tag.
  • --rna-strandness: unstranded is the default. F/R for single-end, FR/RF for paired reads; the manual equates RF with TopHat's fr-firststrand.
  • Default maximum intron length 500,000 bp (--max-intronlen).
# STAR 2.7.11b: build the index (mammal genomes: at least 16 GB RAM, ideally 32 GB, says the README)
STAR --runMode genomeGenerate --runThreadN 16 \
     --genomeDir star_idx --genomeFastaFiles genome.fa
# map one tissue, per-sample 2-pass mode
STAR --runThreadN 16 --genomeDir star_idx \
     --readFilesIn liver_R1.fq.gz liver_R2.fq.gz --readFilesCommand zcat \
     --twopassMode Basic --outSAMstrandField intronMotif \
     --outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate --outFileNamePrefix liver.
  • 2-pass mode. In pass 1 STAR finds junctions; it then inserts them into the index and maps all reads again. The manual says this "does not significantly increase the number of detected novel junctions, but allows to detect more spliced reads mapping to novel junctions", which is what a new genome without annotation needs. With several tissues you can instead collect every sample's SJ.out.tab and give them all to pass 2 with --sjdbFileChrStartEnd.
  • --outSAMstrandField intronMotif adds the XS strand tag (and drops reads with inconsistent or non-canonical introns). BRAKER asks for this option when the BAM comes from STAR.
  • SJ.out.tab lists every junction with its read counts; it is the quickest way to see how much splicing evidence you have.
  • For an assembly with more than 5,000 contigs the manual recommends lowering --genomeChrBinNbits to min(18, log2(max(genome length / number of contigs, read length))). For example, a 2.5 Gb assembly in 25,000 contigs gives log2(100,000) ≈ 16.6, so 16.

Which one? Both write sorted BAM files that BRAKER accepts. HISAT2 needs less memory (the index fits on a desktop); STAR is the aligner NCBI's EGAPx pipeline uses for short RNA-seq reads. BRAKER itself runs HISAT2 when you give it FASTQ files instead of BAM.

Which exons and isoforms does your RNA-seq support?

A toy gene with six exons and three isoforms: A (all of exons 1-5) is made mostly in brain, B skips exon 3 and is made only in testis, C ends in exon 6 instead of 5 and is rare, liver only. Pick tissues and sequencing depth; reads are subsampled from the same simulated library, as when you downsample real data. Split reads (orange) are joined by a thin line across the intron; the arcs below the reads count them per junction. Turn on the protein to add a miniprot alignment of a relative's protein.

Tissues sequenced

Toy rules (what the figure computes)
  • Expression, in reads on this gene per million reads sequenced: brain A 2.0; liver A 0.8, C 0.25; testis A 0.3, B 0.9. These numbers are invented to show the effect; real genes range over several orders of magnitude.
  • Each read is 100 bases, single-end, placed uniformly along the chosen transcript (real coverage is less even). A read that covers two exons is drawn as a split read.
  • An exon is "covered" when at least one read overlaps it. An isoform is "supported" when every one of its junctions has at least the minimum number of split reads. Exon 3 skipping (junction 2→4) and the exon 4→6 junction are the only junctions unique to B and C.
  • The protein covers the coding part of isoform A (from the start codon in exon 2 to the stop codon in exon 5); it supports the junctions 2→3, 3→4 and 4→5 but never the UTR exon 1, the 1→2 junction, or the B and C isoforms.
  • Drawn reads are capped at 26 rows; counts in the arcs and the readout always include all reads.

Long reads: whole transcripts in one read

PacBio's current bulk Iso-Seq workflow (isoseq.how; isoseq 4.3.0/26.2.0, pbmm2 26.2) starts from HiFi reads, which already have predicted accuracy of at least Q20:

# 1. remove cDNA primers, orient reads 5'->3' (also splits pooled samples by their barcode sequences)
lima movieX.hifi_reads.bam primers.fasta movieX.fl.bam --isoseq --peek-guess
# 2. trim poly(A) tails, remove concatemers -> full-length non-concatemer reads (FLNC)
isoseq refine movieX.fl.NEB_5p--NEB_Clontech_3p.bam primers.fasta movieX.flnc.bam --require-polya
# 3. cluster reads of the same isoform (several SMRT cells, i.e. several PacBio sequencing runs: list FLNC files in flnc.fofn, one file name per line)
isoseq cluster2 flnc.fofn clustered.bam
# 4. spliced alignment to the genome
pbmm2 align --preset ISOSEQ --sort clustered.bam genome.fa mapped.bam
# 5. merge redundant transcripts into unique isoforms (GFF)
isoseq collapse mapped.bam collapsed.gff
  • FLNC reads are the clean input. --require-polya keeps only reads with a poly(A) tail of at least 20 bases.
  • isoseq cluster2 replaces isoseq cluster (to be deprecated). It outputs isoforms supported by at least 2 FLNC reads; add --singletons to keep the rest.
  • pbmm2 is PacBio's wrapper around minimap2 that reads and writes PacBio BAM and comes with parameter presets; ISOSEQ is the preset for transcripts (its README lists -G maximum intron length and -C non-canonical splice cost as the extra settings you may change).

Nanopore cDNA. Reads from cDNA kits come in both orientations and include primers. Pychopper (v2.7.10) finds the primers, keeps full-length reads and orients them (-k picks the kit's primers; PCS109 is the default, check that your kit is in its list). Then align with minimap2's spliced mode:

pychopper -r report.pdf -u unclassified.fq -w rescued.fq cdna_reads.fq full_length.fq
minimap2 -t 16 -ax splice genome.fa full_length.fq | samtools sort -o ont_cdna.bam      # nanopore cDNA
minimap2 -t 16 -ax splice:hq -uf genome.fa isoseq.fa | samtools sort -o isoseq.bam      # PacBio / high-quality full-length cDNA

In minimap2 (2.31) -uf tells it the reads are already in transcript orientation; -x splice otherwise tries both strands. The default maximum intron is 200 kb (-G).

Long reads in BRAKER. The standard BRAKER3 expects short reads. For long reads only, the README points to a separate container (teambraker/braker3:isoseq) with a GeneMark-ETP version hard-coded to assemble long reads, fed with a minimap2 splice:hq BAM. It warns: do not mix short and long reads in that variant, and accuracy depends heavily on long-read depth (similar to short reads with 2022 PacBio HiFi data, lower with noisier, shallower data). EGAPx (section 06) takes short and long RNA reads together.

From alignments to transcripts and ORFs

Mapped reads are not yet transcripts. A transcript assembler joins overlapping reads and their junctions into full isoform models.

# StringTie 3.0.3 (the "StringTie2" algorithm, extended)
stringtie -p 8 -o liver.gtf liver.bam                    # short reads
stringtie -p 8 -L -o isoseq.gtf isoseq.bam               # long reads
stringtie -p 8 --mix -o mix.gtf liver.bam isoseq.bam     # both: short BAM first, long BAM second
stringtie --merge -o merged.gtf liver.gtf brain.gtf testis.gtf   # one non-redundant set across tissues

Defaults worth knowing: minimum transcript length 200 bp (-m); minimum junction coverage 1 read (-j); isoforms below 1 % of the gene's most abundant isoform dropped (-f 0.01); --rf/--fr declare a stranded library. The README recommends at least 8 GB of RAM, 16 GB for large data sets.

A transcript is not yet a protein. TransDecoder finds the most likely ORF in each transcript and writes it back to genome coordinates:

# TransDecoder v6.0.0: full wrapper, genome + GTF mode
TransDecoder --genome genome.fa --gtf merged.gtf
# main output: merged.fasta.transdecoder.genome.gff3 (plus .pep, .cds, .bed on the transcripts)

By default it reports ORFs of at least 100 amino acids (-m; the wiki warns that lowering it raises false positives sharply). It scores coding potential with a model it trains on your own data, so it needs a whole transcriptome (hundreds of candidates at least), not a handful of transcripts. Version 6.0.0 introduced this TransDecoder wrapper; older tutorials call TransDecoder.LongOrfs and TransDecoder.Predict separately.

Protein evidence

For BRAKER: an OrthoDB partition. ProtHint needs many members of each protein family, not just one relative's proteome. The BRAKER team provides ready partitions; for any vertebrate take Vertebrata.fa.gz (OrthoDB v12: 5.8 GB compressed; the v11 file was 3.1 GB). You may add the proteins of a close relative to the same file.

wget https://bioinf.uni-greifswald.de/bioinf/partitioned_odb12/Vertebrata.fa.gz
gunzip Vertebrata.fa.gz                                  # the Greifswald tools need it unzipped
cat Vertebrata.fa relative_proteins.faa > proteins.fa   # optional
braker.pl --genome=genome.softmasked.fa --prot_seq=proteins.fa --bam=liver.bam,brain.bam --threads=16

miniprot: proteins straight onto the genome. miniprot (Heng Li; v0.18) aligns a protein to a whole genome, allowing introns and frameshifts. It translates the genome in all six frames and seeds on short amino-acid words, then aligns with a splice model. BUSCO 5.7+ uses it in genome mode (lesson 2, section 10), and GALBA and EGAPx use it for gene evidence.

# index once (slow and memory-hungry, so build it separately), then align
miniprot -t16 -d genome.mpi genome.fa
miniprot -Iut16 --gff -j2 genome.mpi relative_proteins.faa > relative.miniprot.gff
-I
Sets the maximum intron length from the genome size: min(max(3.6 × √genome length, 10,000), 300,000). For a 3 Gb mammal genome that is 3.6 × 54,772 ≈ 197 kb. Without -I or -G the limit is 200 kb.
-u, -t16
-u also prints proteins that found no hit (useful to count them); -t is the number of threads (default 4).
-j2
Splice model for vertebrates and insects (the default 1 is a general model). Not in the README's basic command, which uses only -Iut16 --gff.
--gff
GFF3 output with features mRNA, CDS and stop_codon; the full alignment of each hit is kept in a ##PAF line above it. Without --gff you get only the protein PAF format.

What to read in the output (attributes in the mRNA line):

FieldMeaningUse
Identity, PositiveFraction of identical and of similar (positive-scoring) amino acidsClose relatives give high values; very low identity = distant homolog or paralog.
Rank1 = best hit of this protein; more hits are often paralogs or pseudogenesUsually keep rank 1 for gene evidence.
Frameshift, StopCodonNumber of frameshifts and in-frame stop codons in the alignmentEither a pseudogene, a real loss, or an error in the assembly; see section 06 on homopolymer errors.
Donor, AcceptorShown only when the intron does not start with GT or end with AGNon-canonical introns deserve a second look.
TargetWhich part of the protein (amino-acid coordinates) this hit coversA hit covering a small part of the protein is weak evidence.

In the ##PAF line, the protein CIGAR uses M (match: 3 bases per amino acid), I/D (amino-acid gaps), F and G (frameshifts), and N, U, V for introns of phase 0, 1 and 2 (an intron that falls between codons, after the first, or after the second base of a codon). Tags fs and st count frameshifts and stops. By default a hit is reported only if it covers at least 10 % of the protein (--outc 0.1) and scores at least 99 % of that protein's best hit (--outs 0.99).

The miniprot README says it "is not optimized for mapping distant homologs". Proteins of the same family or order work best; for distant groups BRAKER's OrthoDB route is designed to tolerate unknown distance.

Pitfalls

  • Wrong genome version. RNA-seq mapped to an earlier assembly cannot be reused after scaffolding or polishing; contig names and coordinates change. Map again.
  • Missing XS tag. HISAT2 without --dta or STAR without --outSAMstrandField intronMotif: BRAKER's StringTie step needs the tag on spliced reads.
  • Contamination and organelles. RNA-seq contains many mitochondrial and ribosomal RNA reads; they map to NUMTs and repeats and create false "genes". The STAR manual suggests removing junctions in the mitochondrial genome and non-canonical junctions with few reads.
  • Expressed repeats. Active transposable elements are transcribed and spliced too. Masking repeats before prediction (section 04) keeps them from becoming gene models.
  • Absence of evidence. No reads on a gene means it is not expressed in the tissues you sampled, not that it is absent.
In our projects

We had no RNA-seq for our rodent species A (not yet published, so no name or exact numbers here). The evidence for its genes came from DNA only: our long reads, the published HiFi assembly of a close relative, and the human annotation projected onto species A by TOGA2 in the Hiller lab (most human reference genes were found, the large majority as 1:1 orthologs; section 07). This is the "only DNA" case: projection gives good coding models of conserved genes, but nothing for UTRs, tissue-specific isoforms or genes absent from the human set. Adding RNA-seq from a few tissues (or Iso-Seq) would be the step that makes a BRAKER3 or EGAPx run possible (section 06).
