03Assembling short reads

Short reads (Illumina, MGI) are 100–250 bases long, so no single read crosses a repeat that is longer than itself. Short-read assemblers therefore do not compare reads with each other. They cut every read into k-mers and join k-mers that overlap. This section shows how that works, why the contigs stop where they stop, how errors are removed, how k is chosen, how the graph fits into memory, and which tools exist today.

Words used in this section

de Bruijn graph
A network built from k-mers. Each distinct k-mer is a point (a node). An arrow (an edge) joins two k-mers when the last k − 1 bases of the first are the first k − 1 bases of the second, so they could sit next to each other in the genome. Named after the mathematician Nicolaas de Bruijn. (Some tools put the (k − 1)-mers on the nodes and the k-mers on the edges; the idea is the same.)
unitig
A run of k-mers with no choice along the way: each has exactly one way forward and one way back. The assembler writes it out as one sequence. A unitig ends where the graph branches or stops.
contig
A gap-free sequence the assembler reports. Contigs are unitigs after cleaning (errors removed, small side branches cut) and, in some tools, joined further with the help of read pairs.
scaffold
Contigs put in order and orientation and joined with runs of N (unknown bases) in between. The N-run stands for a gap whose sequence is unknown.
bubble
Two paths that split and join again a little later. Made by a heterozygous site (the two parental copies differ) or by a read error in the middle of a read.
tip
A short dead-end side branch. Usually made by a read error near the end of a read.

A de Bruijn graph with a repeat

The toy genome has three unique pieces and one repeat (purple) that occurs twice, identically. All its k-mers are put into a graph and joined into unitigs. Move k: while k is shorter than the repeat + 2, both copies give the same k-mers, the graph merges them into one purple unitig, and the genome falls apart into several contigs. Once every k-mer that touches the repeat also carries a unique base on each side, the graph becomes one line. Then add a read error or a heterozygous site.

Simple explanation with examples: why repeats cut contigs

Think of a train route written on cards, each card showing three station names in a row. You rebuild the route by laying cards so that the last two names of one card match the first two of the next. That is a de Bruijn graph with k = 3.

route: Oak – Mill – Bridge – Park – Mill – Bridge – Lake cards: Oak·Mill·Bridge Mill·Bridge·Park Bridge·Park·Mill Park·Mill·Bridge Mill·Bridge·Lake

After Mill·Bridge the next card can be "…Park" or "…Lake". The cards alone cannot tell you which comes first. A careful assembler does not guess: it stops at Mill·Bridge and reports the pieces it is sure of. So the route comes out as several short pieces, and the repeated part Mill – Bridge is written once, not twice.

With cards of four names, Oak·Mill·Bridge·Park and Park·Mill·Bridge·Lake are different cards, and the route is unique again. Longer k = longer cards = repeats up to k − 2 long are resolved. But a card is only made when a read contains all of its names, and reads have a fixed length, so k cannot grow forever (see Choosing k).

A bubble: the mother's chromosome says Park – Mill, the father's says Park – Mole – Mill. Both versions make cards, so the path splits after Park and joins again at Mill. A tip: one card with a misspelled station ("Brigde") that leads nowhere, because no other card continues from it.

What breaks a contig, and what the assembler does about it

  • Repeats longer than about k. Every copy gives the same k-mers, so the graph merges the copies and branches where they begin and end. Contigs stop at those branches. Interspersed repeats such as SINEs and LINEs, satellites and duplicated genes all do this. No setting removes the problem; only longer reads or long-range links do.
  • Heterozygous sites make bubbles. Assemblers "pop" bubbles: keep one path (often the one with more coverage) and drop the other. The result is one pseudohaploid sequence. Where the two copies differ a lot, the bubble is not recognised and both copies end up as separate contigs (a false duplication, removed later with purge_dups, section 06).
  • Read errors make tips (error near a read end) and small bubbles (error in the middle). Error k-mers are rare (seen once or twice), so tools cut low-coverage branches. ABySS, for example, removes branches shorter than k (its t parameter, default k) and, in Bloom filter mode, uses only k-mers seen at least kc times (default 2).

The k − 1 rule: how far a contig reaches into a repeat copy

Take one copy of an identical repeat, L bases long, flanked by unique DNA. The last k-mer that is still unique contains one flanking base plus the first k − 1 bases of the copy. So the contig from the left enters the copy by k − 1 bases and stops; the contig from the right does the same from the other end. The middle, L − 2(k − 1) bases, is in no unique contig. If that number is negative, the two contigs overlap inside the copy but are still not joined. The copy is resolved only when k ≥ L + 2.

In our projects: young SINEs cut the desman assemblies exactly k − 1 bases deep.
  • Galemys pyrenaicus (GCA_019455555.1) was assembled with ABySS 2.1.5 at k = 100. Of its 62,376 N-runs, 17,284 are exactly 50 N, and 64 % of those 50-N runs touch a Tal SINE (8.1 × more than random), sitting in the middle of the element. For a 268-base copy of the youngest subfamily (d4) the rule predicts 268 − 2 × 99 = 70 bases in no contig; the gaps closed later from reads were 74–109 bases. 16 % of d4 copies are gapped against 0.2 % of the older d5 copies: older copies differ from each other by mutations, so their k-mers are unique.
  • Desmana moschata: the raw SPAdes runs (final k = 99, inferred from exact 99-base overlaps at 75.5 % of contig ends, not read from a log) end inside a young Tal SINE at 50.3 % (animal R8) and 48.0 % (R9) of contig ends, a median 98 bases (= k − 1) into the element. In a toy test (SPAdes 3.15.5, --isolate, k = 77) contigs ran 75–76 bases into an identical copy from each side, and 250-base copies with 3 % divergence were fully resolved.
  • The reads are there: in Galemys, 237 of 238 gaps were closed identically by independent libraries. The gap is made by the graph, not by missing data. 26 long-read assemblies (PacBio, ONT; two Talpa, a hedgehog, 22 bats) have no N at young SINEs.

Fixing read errors before assembly

Most correctors use the k-mer spectrum from lesson 1. A k-mer from the genome is seen about as often as the k-mer coverage (tens of times). A k-mer that contains a sequencing error is seen once or a few times, because the same error rarely happens twice at the same place. So k-mers above a threshold are called trusted (or solid), the rest untrusted. A read base is changed when one substitution turns all the untrusted k-mers that cover it into trusted ones.

read ACGTTGCATGCC k = 5, k-mer coverage ≈ 30 k-mers ACGTT 31 CGTTG 29 GTTGC 33 TTGCA 1 TGCAT 1 GCATG 2 CATGC 1 ATGCC 1 five k-mers in a row are rare: they all cover the 8th base try A→G TTGCG 30 TGCGT 28 GCGTG 32 CGTGC 30 GTGCC 29 → all trusted: correct A→G

Not every pipeline wants corrected reads. The MaSuRCA manual says: "Do not do any trimming, cleaning or error correction. This will likely deteriorate the assembly", because MaSuRCA corrects reads itself. ABySS 2 has no separate read-correction step; it starts contigs only from reads made entirely of solid k-mers and trims error branches in the graph. Error correction of many hundreds of millions of read pairs needs a lot of memory (next box).

In our projects: species D died in error correction. Species D is one of our unpublished small-mammal genomes, sequenced with short reads only. The run was spades.py --careful -1 R1.fastq.gz -2 R2.fastq.gz -o spades_output -t 16 -m 256 -k 21,33,55 on downsampled reads (SPAdes 4.x, standard mode). It failed in BayesHammer (the error-correction step) with "finished abnormally, OS return value: 12". Return value 12 is the operating-system code for "out of memory"; our project report too says it was apparently out of memory. Two things in that command go against the manual: --careful is "recommended only for assembly of small genomes. We strongly recommend not to use it for large and medium-size eukaryotic genomes", and the log itself suggested --isolate. The desman SPAdes runs used --isolate.

Choosing k

Small k
Most k-mers appear in many reads, so low-coverage regions are still connected. But more of the genome is repeated at that length, so the graph has more branches and the contigs are short. In the toy above, try k = 4.
Large k
Resolves repeats up to k − 2 bases. But each read gives only L − k + 1 k-mers (lesson 1), so k-mer coverage falls, and where it falls to zero the path breaks. One read error spoils k k-mers, so with large k fewer k-mers are error-free. k can never exceed the read length.
Odd k
SPAdes and MEGAHIT require odd k. An odd-length k-mer can never be its own reverse complement, which keeps the two DNA strands apart in the graph. ABySS accepts even k (its manual uses k = 96).

There are three ways to deal with the trade-off:

  1. Several k in one run (multi-k)
    Assemble with a small k, then use those contigs together with the reads to build the graph again at a larger k, and so on. The small k bridges thin coverage; the large k resolves repeats. SPAdes picks k from the read length: 21, 33, 55 for 100-base reads; 21, 33, 55, 77 for 150-base reads; 21, 33, 55, 77, 99, 127 for 250-base reads. Its manual: "We strongly recommend not to change -k parameter unless you are clearly aware about the effect"; all values must be odd and below 128. MEGAHIT's default list is 21, 29, 39, 59, 79, 99, 119, 141, and it drops values longer than the longest read + 20. Platanus-allee starts at k = 32 and grows in steps of 20 up to 0.5 × read length.
  2. Predict one best k (KmerGenie)
    For single-k assemblers (ABySS, Minia, SOAPdenovo2). KmerGenie builds the k-mer histogram for many k, predicts for each how many distinct genomic k-mers the data hold, and returns the k with the most. Its own manual says: "There is no need to use Kmergenie for a multi-k assembler, like SPAdes."
  3. Try several k and compare
    The ABySS manual calls this "standard practice" and gives a starting point: for 2×150 reads at 40× coverage, k is often 70–90; for 2×250 reads, 110–140. Compare the assemblies with N50 and BUSCO (sections 08, 10), not only N50.

Fitting the graph into memory: the Bloom filter

A mammal genome gives roughly as many distinct genomic k-mers as it has bases (billions), plus many error k-mers. Storing each k-mer and its count in a hash table takes at least 8 bytes per k-mer (k = 31 at 2 bits per base) plus the count and the table's empty slots. A Bloom filter stores a set in a few bits per element. It is a row of m bits, all 0 at the start, and h hash functions. To insert a k-mer, compute its h hash values and set those h bits to 1. To ask "is this k-mer in the set?", look at the same h bits: if any is 0, the k-mer is certainly absent; if all are 1, it is probably present. "Probably", because other k-mers may have set those bits by chance: a false positive. The filter never gives false negatives, and it does not store the k-mers themselves.

The de Bruijn graph does not need its edges stored: from any k-mer the assembler tries the four possible next k-mers (add A, C, G or T) and asks the Bloom filter which exist. Minia was the first assembler to store the graph this way; ABySS 2 does it too, and so do the correctors Lighter and BFC.

false-positive rate = (1 − (1 − 1/m)hn)h ≈ (1 − e−hn/m)h m = bits, h = hash functions, n = distinct k-mers inserted

This is equation 1 of the ABySS 2 paper. The source code of ABySS 2.3.10 estimates the same rate in a simpler way: the share of bits that are 1, raised to the power h (if 30 % of bits are 1 and h = 4, a random absent k-mer hits four 1-bits with probability 0.34 ≈ 0.8 %).

Grey = correctly "absent", red = false positive ("present" although it was never inserted). Make the filter smaller and watch false positives appear. Type any 5-mer to see which bits it uses.

With h fixed, the false-positive rate depends only on bits per k-mer (m / n). For each m / n there is a best h, about 0.69 × m / n; more hash functions than that fill the filter faster than they help. In a real assembler a false positive adds a k-mer that is not in the reads. ABySS 2 found that most such k-mers form a short dead-end side branch, which a look-ahead step removes; its code treats side branches of up to 5 k-mers as possible false positives.

ABySS 2 in practice. The parameter B is the whole memory budget: 8/9 of it goes to a counting Bloom filter and 1/9 to a second filter that remembers which k-mers are already in a contig. The manual's guide values: B=2G for Caenorhabditis elegans (~101 Mb), B=50G for Homo sapiens (~3.1 Gb), B=500G for Picea glauca (~20 Gb). Check the log (with v=-v): the FPR under "Counting Bloom filter stats" should be 5 % or less. Default H (hash functions) is 4. In the ABySS 2 paper the human assembly needed under 35 GB of RAM, while DISCOVAR de novo needed 618 GB and SOAPdenovo2 659 GB for the same data.

Read pairs, insert size and scaffolds

Both ends of each DNA fragment are read, so the two reads of a pair lie a known distance apart. The insert size (or fragment size) is the length of the fragment, both reads included. A normal paired-end library has fragments of about 300–600 bases, the two reads facing each other. A mate-pair (jumping) library is made by circularising long fragments, 2–10 kb or more, and reading across the junction; it needs much more DNA and lab work. The Galemys genome used both: paired-end libraries of 350 and 550 bases and mate pairs of 5 and 9 kb.

contig 1 ────────────────────────────▶ contig 2 ─────────────────────────▶ read 1 ──▶ ◀── read 2 |←────────── insert size ≈ 9,000 (from the library) ───────────→| scaffold ──────────────────────────────NNNNNNNNNN──────────────────────────────

Pairs do three jobs. (1) Inside the graph they choose between branches: if read 1 lies in the left unique contig, read 2 tells which exit from a repeat is right (SPAdes and ABySS do this; MaSuRCA merges the two reads of a pair into one longer "super-read" when a unique path joins them). (2) Scaffolding: pairs whose reads fall in two different contigs order and orient those contigs. (3) The distance between the reads gives an estimate of the gap size, written as that many N. Some tools write a fixed number of N when the size is unknown, so an N-run is not always a measured gap size (section 06). ABySS uses mate-pair libraries (mp=) only for scaffolding; they "do not contribute towards the consensus sequence".

In our projects: N-runs of fixed length and doubled SINE copies. Galemys (ABySS) has 17,284 runs of exactly 50 N, 64 % of them touching a Tal SINE. The published Desmana moschata assembly (GCA_051107935.1) was built from SPAdes 4.0 (k = 99) and MaSuRCA 4.1 contigs, then joined onto the Galemys genome with RagTag 2.1. 65,059 of its 66,963 N-runs are exactly 100 N, RagTag's placeholder for an unknown gap. At those joins the MaSuRCA contigs on both sides often each contained the whole SINE copy, so the same ~250-base copy is written twice with 100 N between: 19,250 doubled copies genome-wide. Reference-guided joining copies the neighbour's order and cannot see such overlaps.

What is used now? For a new vertebrate reference, short reads alone are no longer the standard: the reference projects use long reads and Hi-C (lesson 1, sections 04–05), and short reads are used to polish and to check quality (sections 06, 08). Short-read-only assembly is still done when only short reads can be afforded, for population or comparative work, and for many small genomes. Of the tools above, ABySS 2, MEGAHIT and MaSuRCA document mammal-size use, Platanus-allee is built for heterozygous genomes, and all four had updates in 2025–2026; SPAdes is maintained and does run on mammals (our desman, Cricetulus), but its developers wrote it for small genomes. Expect contig N50 in the tens of kilobases.

In our projects: what short reads give for a mammal. Contig N50: Desmana raw SPAdes 24.2 kb (R8) and 21.4 kb (R9), published 43.3 kb; Galemys (ABySS, with mate pairs) 64.5 kb. Long reads on other small mammals of ours: species B (Flye, short long-reads with N50 under 3 kb) a few hundred kb; species A (hifiasm) above 10 Mb. Benchmarks on the Monsoon cluster with 32× mouse reads: ABySS 5.4 h and contig N50 6.7 kb, MEGAHIT 8 h and 13.9 kb. Memory requested for Darevskia mixta (~16×): ABySS 120 GB, MEGAHIT 240 GB, SPAdes 470 GB (requests, not measured peaks).

Command lines

Syntax checked against each tool's current manual. Paths and numbers of threads/memory are examples; see section 02 for planning memory.

# SPAdes 4.x - isolate mode (recommended for eukaryote isolates at 50x or more; not combinable with --careful)
spades.py --isolate -1 R1.fq.gz -2 R2.fq.gz -o spades_out -t 32 -m 500
#   -m = memory limit in GB (default 250); SPAdes stops when it reaches it
#   k is chosen from read length (150-base reads: 21,33,55,77); set -k only if you know why (odd, < 128)
#   → spades_out/scaffolds.fasta (recommended), contigs.fasta, assembly_graph_with_scaffolds.gfa
#   headers like >NODE_3_length_237403_cov_243.207 : cov = k-mer coverage at the largest k
#   after a crash: spades.py --continue -o spades_out

# ABySS 2 - Bloom filter mode (B = total memory budget); try several k (and kc 2-4)
abyss-pe name=sp k=96 B=50G j=32 kc=3 v=-v \
         lib='pea' pea='R1.fq.gz R2.fq.gz' \
         mp='mpa' mpa='MP_R1.fq.gz MP_R2.fq.gz'          # mp only if you have mate pairs
#   → sp-unitigs.fa, sp-contigs.fa, sp-scaffolds.fa ; abyss-fac sp-scaffolds.fa for N50
#   check the log: "Counting Bloom filter stats" FPR should be ≤ 5 %
#   ABySS puts temporary files in TMPDIR (default /tmp): export TMPDIR=/big/disk first

# MEGAHIT - multi-k; -o must not exist yet
megahit -1 R1.fq.gz -2 R2.fq.gz -t 32 -m 0.5 -o megahit_out
#   -m = memory for graph building, as bytes or a fraction of the machine's RAM (default 0.9)
#   → megahit_out/final.contigs.fa (contigs ≥ 200 bases by default)

# MaSuRCA - simple mode for one paired-end library (raw, untrimmed reads); -r adds long reads (hybrid)
masurca -t 32 -i /full/path/R1.fq.gz,/full/path/R2.fq.gz
#   bigger projects: copy sr_config_example.txt, edit, then:  masurca config.txt ; ./assemble.sh
#   → CA*/primary.scf.fasta (and alternative.scf.fasta); CA.mr.*/ when long reads were given

# Platanus-allee - contigs, then phasing, then consensus (logs go to stderr)
platanus_allee assemble -f R1.fq R2.fq -t 32 -m 200 2>assemble.log
platanus_allee phase -c out_contig.fa -IP1 R1.fq R2.fq -t 32 2>phase.log
platanus_allee consensus -c out_consensusInput.fa -IP1 R1.fq R2.fq -t 32 2>consensus.log
#   → out_allPhasedScaffold.fa (two haplotypes), out_consensusScaffold.fa (pseudohaploid)

# Choosing one k for a single-k assembler
ls -1 R1.fq.gz R2.fq.gz > reads.list
kmergenie reads.list -t 16             # last line of output = best k; report histograms_report.html

Pitfalls. (1) SPAdes --careful on a mammal: the manual advises against it. (2) Memory: in our species D run the step that ran out of memory was error correction; SPAdes stops when it reaches -m, so set it to what the machine really has. (3) Do not give MaSuRCA trimmed or corrected reads. (4) ABySS reads abyss-pe variables also from the environment; abyss-pe env prints what it will use. (5) Gap N-runs from scaffolding are guesses or placeholders; check them before using gap sizes for anything.
