05Chromosome-level scaffolding with Hi-C and YaHS

An assembler gives you contigs: stretches of sequence with no gaps. It cannot tell you which contig lies next to which on a chromosome, or which way round it goes. Hi-C reads can. A Hi-C scaffolder such as YaHS uses them to put contigs in order, turn them the right way, and join them with runs of N into scaffolds. When it works well, each scaffold is one whole chromosome.

Scaffold
Contigs placed in order and orientation, joined by a gap: a run of the letter N that stands for sequence we do not have. The length of the N run is a placeholder unless the tool says it measured it. YaHS writes 100 N at every join.
Orientation
DNA has two strands. A contig can be written as read on one strand (+, forward) or on the other (−, its reverse complement). A scaffolder must pick one for every contig.
Chromosome-level
Most of the assembly (the EBP target is at least 90 %) sits in scaffolds that each match one chromosome. The "C" in the quality code 6.C.Q40 from lesson 1.

How Hi-C works

Inside the nucleus each chromosome is folded into its own region, a chromosome territory. Hi-C (Lieberman-Aiden et al. 2009) freezes this folding and turns "these two pieces of DNA were touching" into a pair of sequencing reads. Step through the protocol:

Two rules follow from the folding, and every Hi-C scaffolder relies on them:

  1. Same chromosome ≫ different chromosomes. Most read pairs join two places on the same chromosome (in cis). Pairs between different chromosomes are rare and look like an even, faint background.
  2. Near ≫ far. Along one chromosome, the number of contacts falls steadily as the distance between the two places grows. Neighbours touch most often.

So if the end of contig A has many contacts with the start of contig B, and few with anything else, the most likely explanation is that A's end and B's start are neighbours on the same chromosome.

Reading a contact map

A contact map is a square picture of all Hi-C read pairs. The assembly is laid along both axes in the same order, cut into equal bins. The colour of each cell is the number of read pairs with one read in the row bin and the other in the column bin. Rule 2 makes the diagonal (each bin with itself and its neighbours) the darkest line. If the order is right, every chromosome appears as a dark square on the diagonal and the rest of the map is pale. Wrong order or orientation shows up as dark patches off the diagonal.

Try it

Put the contigs in order

A toy genome with two chromosomes, 1 (bins 1–28) and 2 (bins 1–24), was assembled into 7 contigs, A to G. They came out of the assembler in a random order and some are reversed. The map is computed from where each bin really lies, with rules 1 and 2 above plus a little noise. Select a contig (click its button or its stripe on the map), then move or flip it until the off-diagonal blobs are gone. Or let the "YaHS-like" button join contigs automatically, one join at a time.

What to look for. A flipped contig makes its darkest off-diagonal corner sit away from the diagonal: the contact is strongest at the end that should touch its neighbour. A contig from the other chromosome gives a whole pale cross through the chromosome's square. With "one contig is a misjoin" ticked, contig D carries the end of chromosome 1 and the start of chromosome 2 glued together; its own square splits into two blocks with a pale gap between them, which no reordering can fix. Only breaking it can (the YaHS-like button does that first, as YaHS does).

Simple explanation with examples: why contacts give order and orientation

Think of a long necklace of numbered beads, crumpled in a bag. Bead 10 touches bead 11 almost always, bead 15 often, bead 60 rarely. Now cut the necklace into pieces and shake them. If you photograph the bag many times before cutting, the "who touched whom" counts still tell you that the last bead of one piece kept touching the first bead of another: they were neighbours.

contig A (bins a1..a5) contig B (bins b1..b5) contacts a5–b1 = 38 a1–b1 = 6 a5–b5 = 7 a1–b5 = 2 → the strongest pair is A's right end with B's left end: A+ B+ (A forward, then B forward) if instead a5–b5 were strongest: A+ B− (B is reversed) contacts A–C ≈ 1 everywhere (the background) → C is on another chromosome

This is also why a scaffolder can only order pieces that are long enough. A contig of one bin has no "left end" and "right end" to compare, so its orientation stays uncertain. Very small contigs are often left unplaced.

Hi-C libraries

The cutting step differs between kits. It matters because YaHS can take the cut-site sequence (-e) and use it to correct for bins that simply have more cut sites. The motifs below are the ones YaHS's README gives; N means any base.

YaHS's README adds a warning: giving the enzyme "may not change the scaffolding result very much if not make it worse", especially for assemblies built from noisy long reads, because cut-site counts depend on the assembly's base accuracy. If unsure, run once with and once without -e and compare the maps.

How much Hi-C? Count it like any coverage: Hi-C read bases ÷ genome size. YaHS's README reports scaffolding the human genome with about 45× Hi-C. The VGP tutorial asks for around 60× (see lesson 1, step 6). Only a minority of Hi-C pairs are informative for scaffolding (the far-apart ones), so Hi-C coverage is not comparable with shotgun coverage.

Step 1 · Map the Hi-C reads to the contigs

Hi-C reads break two assumptions of normal mapping. The two reads of a pair can lie megabases apart or on different contigs, so the aligner must not try to pair them. And a read can run across the ligation junction, so its first part comes from one place and its second part from another (a chimeric read). The usual fix is to keep only the part nearest the read's start (its 5′ end). YaHS does not map reads itself; its README points to the Arima mapping pipeline, the Omni-C pipeline and HiC-Pro, and recommends marking PCR/optical duplicates first. The commands below use bwa, samtools and Picard; BAM is the alignment file they produce.

# Arima-HiC mapping pipeline (ArimaGenomics/mapping_pipeline, version 07/26/2023), file names shortened
samtools faidx contigs.fa
bwa index contigs.fa
bwa mem -t 16 contigs.fa hic_R1.fastq.gz | samtools view -@ 16 -Sb - > r1.bam     # read 1 alone
bwa mem -t 16 contigs.fa hic_R2.fastq.gz | samtools view -@ 16 -Sb - > r2.bam     # read 2 alone
samtools view -h r1.bam | perl filter_five_end.pl | samtools view -Sb - > r1.f.bam  # keep 5' part
samtools view -h r2.bam | perl filter_five_end.pl | samtools view -Sb - > r2.f.bam
perl two_read_bam_combiner.pl r1.f.bam r2.f.bam samtools 10 \
  | samtools view -bS -t contigs.fa.fai - | samtools sort -@ 16 -o paired.bam -     # pair up, MAPQ ≥ 10
java -jar picard.jar AddOrReplaceReadGroups INPUT=paired.bam OUTPUT=paired.rg.bam \
  ID=hic LB=hic SM=sample PL=ILLUMINA PU=none
java -Xmx30G -jar picard.jar MarkDuplicates INPUT=paired.rg.bam OUTPUT=hic.bam \
  METRICS_FILE=dup_metrics.txt ASSUME_SORTED=TRUE REMOVE_DUPLICATES=TRUE

# Omni-C style alternative (Dovetail docs): one bwa run that maps the mates independently
bwa mem -5SP -T0 -t 16 contigs.fa hic_R1.fastq.gz hic_R2.fastq.gz | pairtools parse ...
bwa mem -5SP
Used when both read files go into one bwa run (Omni-C docs; needs bwa 0.7.17 or newer). -5: for a split read, report the part with the smallest coordinate (the 5′ part) as the main alignment. -S: skip mate rescue. -P: skip pairing, so each mate is placed on its own. The Arima pipeline reaches the same goal by mapping the two read files separately.
MAPQ 10
The Arima combiner drops pairs where either read has mapping quality below 10. YaHS's own -q default is also 10.
Sorting
YaHS accepts a BAM sorted by read name or by position, with a difference (README, note 1): with name-sorted input it counts a link between the middles of the two alignments and applies -q; with position-sorted or unsorted input it uses the alignment starts and does not filter by mapping quality. So filter MAPQ before YaHS (the Arima pipeline above does) or give a name-sorted BAM.
Duplicates
In the YaHS source (v1.2.2, parse_bam_rec), alignments flagged as unmapped, secondary, failing QC, duplicate or supplementary are skipped. Marked duplicates are therefore ignored even if they are not removed.

Step 2 · Run YaHS

samtools faidx contigs.fa                    # YaHS needs contigs.fa.fai next to contigs.fa
yahs -o asm contigs.fa hic.bam               # Omni-C: no -e
yahs -e GATC,GANTC -o asm contigs.fa hic.bam # Arima 2-enzyme
yahs -?                                      # long help with all options

Inputs: the contigs in FASTA (indexed) and the Hi-C alignments as BAM, BED, PA5 or YaHS's own .bin. The file type is taken from the extension, or set with --file-type. YaHS first converts the alignments to asm.bin; reuse it for reruns and for contact maps.

What YaHS does, in order

From the YaHS paper (Zhou, McCarthy & Durbin 2023) and the v1.2.2 source code (yahs.c, link.c, graph.c):

  1. Find telomere ends telo.c
    Each contig end is searched for a telomeric repeat (YaHS has a built-in motif list; --telo-motif sets one). An end that is a real chromosome tip is never joined to anything.
  2. Break contigs that look wrong asm_inital_break_01.agp, _02 …
    The contacts inside each contig are counted in 1 kb bins. Where the contact signal across a position drops far below what the rest of the contig shows, the contig is cut there (a likely misjoin, two unrelated pieces glued by the assembler). Repeated until no new breaks are found. Skip with --no-contig-ec. (The file name really is spelled "inital".)
  3. Learn what "neighbours" look like
    For one bin size (the resolution), YaHS cuts every contig into chunks and takes the median of the contacts inside contigs at each distance: the expected count for chunks 1, 2, 3 … apart. This is rule 2, measured on your own data.
  4. Score every possible join
    For each pair of contigs and each of the 4 ways to put them end to end (A+B+, A+B−, A−B+, A−B−), the contacts between the chunks that would become neighbours are compared with the expected counts, after subtracting the background level. Cut-site counts correct the numbers when -e is given. The orientation with the largest score is kept.
  5. Build and prune a graph
    Contigs are nodes, scored joins are edges. Edges are removed when they are weak: in the source, an edge is kept only if it is at least 0.7 × the best edge at that contig end (and above a floor of 0.1). Then tips, repeats, bubbles, transitive edges and ambiguous edges are trimmed. The remaining unbranched paths become scaffolds.
  6. Check the new joins asm_r01_break.agp
    Joins without enough Hi-C support across them are broken again. Skip with --no-scaffold-ec.
  7. Repeat with bigger chunks asm_r01.agp, asm_r02.agp …
    Each round starts from the previous scaffolds with a larger bin. The default list is 10 kb, 20 kb, 50 kb, 100 kb … up to 500 Mb, cut off by assembly size (for a 2–5 Gb assembly the largest is 50 Mb). A round is only run if the current N50 is at least 10 × the bin size; otherwise YaHS retries a smaller bin (at most 3 extra tries) and then stops. -r sets your own list (ascending); smaller first values help very fragmented assemblies.
  8. Write the result asm_scaffolds_final.agp, asm_scaffolds_final.fa
    Scaffolds named scaffold_1, scaffold_2 … sorted longest first; unplaced contigs are kept as their own scaffolds. The log ends with N50 and N90 and the peak memory.

The AGP file

An AGP file (NCBI's "A Golden Path" format) is the recipe for building scaffolds from contigs: one line per piece, in order. W lines are contig pieces with their coordinates and orientation; U lines are gaps of unknown size. YaHS's defaults (source, sdict.c): gap type U, 100 bp, linkage evidence proximity_ligation (= Hi-C). An example of the layout:

# W line: scaffold, start, end, part no., W, contig, contig start, contig end, orientation
# U line: scaffold, start, end, part no., U, gap length, gap type, linked?, evidence
scaffold_1  1      5200000  1    W     contig_12   1      5200000 +
scaffold_1  5200001 5200100 2    U     100         scaffold yes proximity_ligation
scaffold_1  5200101 8900100 3    W     contig_7    1      3700000 -

The AGP matters more than the FASTA: contact-map tools, curation tools and genome submission (section 13) all read it. agp_to_fasta (shipped with YaHS) turns an AGP plus contigs back into FASTA.

Step 3 · Look at the contact map, then fix it by hand

No scaffolder is trusted without looking at the map. Reference genome projects (VGP, Darwin Tree of Life) add manual curation: a person inspects the contact map and fixes joins, breaks, orientation and leftover duplicates. Two viewer families are in use.

# A. Juicebox (Aiden lab). juicer = helper shipped with YaHS; juicer_tools = Aiden lab Java program
juicer pre -a -o asm_JBAT asm.bin asm_scaffolds_final.agp contigs.fa.fai > asm_JBAT.log 2>&1
java -Xmx32G -jar juicer_tools.1.9.9_jcuda.0.8.jar pre asm_JBAT.txt asm_JBAT.hic \
     <(grep PRE_C_SIZE asm_JBAT.log | awk '{print $2" "$3}')
#   open asm_JBAT.hic + asm_JBAT.assembly in Juicebox, edit, save asm_JBAT.review.assembly
juicer post -o asm_JBAT asm_JBAT.review.assembly asm_JBAT.liftover.agp contigs.fa
#   -> asm_JBAT.FINAL.agp and asm_JBAT.FINAL.fa

# B. PretextMap + PretextView (Sanger Tree of Life "rapid curation")
samtools view -h hic_to_scaffolds.bam | PretextMap -o asm.pretext --sortby length --mapq 10
#   open asm.pretext in PretextView (desktop app), edit, "Generate AGP", then pretext-to-asm
  • JBAT and genomes over 2 Gb. YaHS's README: if the assembly is larger than 2 Gb, juicer pre applies a scale factor (a power of two, printed in the log as scale factor: …). You must set it in Juicebox under Assembly › Set Scale, or map and assembly will not match. Every mammal genome in this course is above 2 Gb.
  • PretextMap reads SAM/BAM (via samtools view -h) or pairs, needs about 3 GB of memory by default (16 GB with --highRes, 40 GB with --ultraRes), and writes a 30–50 MB map. Its default minimum MAPQ is 10. The map for curation should be made from the Hi-C reads mapped to the scaffolds, after YaHS.
  • Tracks. PretextView can show coverage, telomere, gap and repeat tracks next to the map. Coverage is what tells a sex chromosome or a duplicated haplotype apart.

Other Hi-C scaffolders

  • SALSA2 (Ghurye et al.; marbl/SALSA). Takes a name-sorted BED of alignments, can use the assembly graph (GFA), -e takes enzymes or DNASE. Its README still lists Python 2.7.
  • 3D-DNA (Dudchenko et al. 2017; aidenlab/3d-dna). Iterative misjoin correction, then builds one "megascaffold" and splits it into chromosomes. Pairs with Juicer and JBAT.
  • pin_hic (dfguan/pin_hic; maps with bwa mem -SP -B10). Joins contigs by a "dual selection" rule over 3 rounds by default.

On a simulated human assembly in the YaHS paper, scaffold N50 / N90 were 132.6 / 36.5 Mb for YaHS, 43.9 / 3.3 Mb for SALSA2 and 51.5 / 8.5 Mb for pin_hic. YaHS is the scaffolder in the VGP Galaxy workflow (WF8).

Reference-guided scaffolding: RagTag, and why it is not Hi-C

ragtag.py scaffold reference.fa contigs.fa (RagTag v2.1.1) aligns your contigs to a related species' genome (minimap2 -x asm5 by default) and orders them along its chromosomes. It does not change contig sequence. Every join gets 100 N unless you add -r to infer gap sizes from the reference.

The danger. The result has the related species' chromosome order, not yours. Inversions, fusions and fissions between the two species disappear: you get chromosome-sized N50 and good-looking statistics, but the arrangement is borrowed. Never use a reference-scaffolded genome for synteny, karyotype or rearrangement studies. Scaffold N50 from RagTag is not a quality measure. It is reasonable only as a temporary frame, for example for whole-genome alignment, with the contigs kept as the real result.

In our projects

  • None of our assemblies has Hi-C yet. Species B and C (two unpublished small-mammal genomes) are contig-level only: tens of thousands of contigs each, with contig N50 in the hundreds of kb. The sequencing provider declined Pore-C for them because the DNA was too short.
  • Species A (an unpublished rodent genome): the hifiasm primary has about a thousand contigs (N50 above 10 Mb), several times more pieces than the published chromosome-level genome of a close relative, whose scaffolds are whole chromosomes. The options considered for our animal were Hi-C at about 45× (recommended by collaborators) or Pore-C. RagTag onto the relative's genome was allowed only for whole-genome alignment and TOGA2, never for rearrangement analyses.
  • What RagTag did to the desman. The published Desmana moschata assembly (GCA_051107935.1) is SPAdes + MaSuRCA contigs scaffolded with RagTag 2.1 onto the Galemys pyrenaicus genome. Where a young SINE copy sat at a contig break, the MaSuRCA contigs on both sides each contained the whole copy, and RagTag joined them with 100 N. The same ~250 bp copy is written twice: 19,250 such duplicated copies among 66,963 N-runs, almost all at 100-N joins. YaHS also joins with a fixed 100 N, so after any scaffolding it is worth checking whether the two sides of a join end in the same sequence.
  • Cricetulus sokolovi: RagTag twice (C. griseus, then Cricetus cricetus) gave QUAST N50 267.7 Mb, chromosome-sized only because of reference scaffolding.
Simple explanation with examples: Hi-C scaffolding vs reference scaffolding

You have torn pages of a book. Hi-C is like noticing which page edges were stuck together by a coffee stain: the evidence comes from your own copy. Reference scaffolding is like sorting your pages by the page order of a different edition. Most pages land correctly, but if your edition has a chapter moved or printed upside down, you will never see it, because you sorted by the other book.

your genome (truth): chr1 = A B C D chr2 = E F relative's genome: chr1 = A B −C D chr2 = E F (C is inverted in the relative) RagTag result: chr1 = A B −C D ← the relative's inversion is now "in" your genome Hi-C result: chr1 = A B C D ← contacts between B's end and C's start fix the orientation
