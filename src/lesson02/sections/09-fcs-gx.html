09Contamination screening with NCBI FCS-GX

Some of the sequence in a new assembly does not come from your animal. Bacteria from the skin or gut, a fungus on the tissue, human DNA from whoever handled the sample, and short synthetic sequences added in the lab all end up in the reads and then in the contigs. The NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen (FCS) finds these pieces and says what to do with each one.

A contaminant, in NCBI's definition, is any sequence "that does not originate from the biological source organism". Left in, it causes real damage. Bacterial genes get annotated as genes of your species, they look like horizontal gene transfer, and they spread into every database that later copies your genome. FCS has two parts, and you run both:

  • FCS-adaptor finds adaptors and vectors. It is a fast version of NCBI's VecScreen, which uses strict BLAST searches against a small database of synthetic sequences. Any ordinary computer can run it.
  • FCS-GX finds DNA from other organisms. It aligns every contig to a very large database of genomes and asks which group of organisms each piece most resembles. A piece that resembles a group other than your animal's is reported as a contaminant. This part needs a large server, or Galaxy.

The FCS documentation recommends running both tools twice: once on the first contig assembly, and again on the final assembly before you submit it to NCBI. If the first run finds a lot of contamination, screen the cleaned genome a second time, because more can turn up. FCS-GX is built for assemblies and does not read FASTQ files. Screening reads is done earlier, with the tools in lesson 1.

Simple explanation with examples: what FCS-GX is checking

Imagine sorting a box of photos from a field trip. You know the trip was to a pine forest. A photo of pines is fine. A photo of a palm beach was put in the wrong box: throw it out (EXCLUDE). A pine photo with someone's thumb over one edge: cut the edge off (TRIM). A pine photo with a sticker in the middle: cover the sticker, or cut the photo in two (FIX or SPLIT). A photo of an oak forest is a harder case. It could belong to the same trip, so put it aside and look at it yourself (REVIEW).

FCS-GX does the same with contigs. You tell it what the animal is (the tax-id, for example the house mouse Mus musculus = 10090). It compares each stretch of each contig with about 3 million sequences from known organisms and asks which group the stretch looks most like. Rodent-like stretches stay. A contig that looks like Pseudomonas from end to end is removed. A rodent contig whose last 8 kb looks bacterial gets those 8 kb cut off.

FCS-GX can only recognise organisms that are in its database or have relatives there. It cannot recognise a bacterium unlike anything known, and it can be fooled by DNA that really did move between species. So its report is a strong suggestion, and the REVIEW category is there for exactly these cases.

How GX finds foreign pieces

The FCS-GX documentation describes six steps:

  1. Mask repeats. Low-complexity sequence (such as CACACA…) and transposons are marked so that they cannot start alignments. They resemble too many genomes to be informative.
  2. Align to the GX database with the GX cross-species aligner (described below).
  3. Refine the alignments against the best-matching organisms (taxa).
  4. Classify each sequence to a taxonomic division: either the division of your animal or a contaminant division.
  5. Write cleaning actions to the action report (*.fcs_gx_report.txt).
  6. Clean the genome with fcs.py clean genome, a separate command that you run after reading the report.

Seeds that tolerate differences between species. Usual k-mers (lesson 1) match only identical sequence, but a contaminating bacterium is rarely the exact strain that is in the database. GX therefore uses modified k-mers, which its authors call h-mers. Each starts as a 56-base window and is changed in three ways:

  • Every third base is dropped, as in discontiguous megablast.
  • The remaining bases are reduced to two letters: R for the purines A and G, and Y for the pyrimidines C and T. Transitions (A↔G, C↔T) are the most common mutations, so after this step they no longer break a match.
  • A minword rule makes the h-mer the same on both DNA strands.

The result is a 38-bit word that, according to the documentation, finds matches between species more readily than an ordinary 19-base k-mer. Seeds are then extended into alignments, which can reach down to 65–75 % identity. The documentation also says the method favours hits in protein-coding regions.

T T . A G . C T . B G C . T C . G G . C C . R/Y code R Y . Y Y . R R . Y Y . identical for A and B: still a seed match

Genome B differs from genome A at six positions. Two of them (positions 3 and 9) are among the dropped bases. The other four (positions 1, 5, 7 and 11) are transitions (A↔G or T↔C), which the R/Y code cannot see. The toy uses 12 bases and skips the strand step; the real h-mer is built from 56 bases.

Divisions, not species. FCS-GX does not ask "which species is this?". It asks which GX division the sequence belongs to. Divisions are built from NCBI's "BLAST names" (an informal label NCBI gives each taxon). They are grouped into eight kingdoms: animals, plants, fungi, protists, bacteria, archaea, viruses and synthetic. Examples from the mapping table in the FCS-GX source:

This is why the tax-id is required. It tells GX which division is "self" (the primary division). A hit to any other division counts as foreign. If your species is not in NCBI Taxonomy, use a congener or the genus. A very high rank such as Metazoa (33208) is not supported. Hits between divisions of the same kingdom, for example a primate stretch in a rodent, have to pass stricter thresholds before GX reports them.

In words, the rules in the source (action_report.py, default thresholds) are:

  • The whole contig is foreign → EXCLUDE. Within the same kingdom (for example animal in animal), coverage below 20 % turns this into REVIEW.
  • Coverage of 10 % or less → no action. The source calls this "coverage too low to decide". The cutoff is the environment variable GX_ACTION_REPORT_LOW_COVERAGE_THRESHOLD, default 10.
  • A mixed contig with too little of your animal left → the whole contig is EXCLUDEd. "Too little" means the part assigned to your animal is under 200 bp, or under both 50,000 bp and 30 % of the contig.
  • Otherwise each foreign stretch is handled separately. A stretch that touches either end is TRIMmed. A stretch inside the contig is FIXed (masked with N).
  • A foreign stretch from the same kingdom (a human-like stretch in a rodent) → kept when its effective length (length × coverage) is under 10,000 bp, otherwise REVIEW.
  • Mostly low-coverage hits → REVIEW. A Wolbachia stretch in an insect → INFO (a known integration into the host genome).
  • A contig of your own division, >80 % covered by hits to mitochondrial sequence → MITOCHONDRION. This rule applies to eukaryotes only. It is in the source code of v0.5.4 and v0.5.5 but not yet described in the wiki.

The two reports and what clean does

fcs.py screen genome writes two files to --out-dir. Both are named <fasta name>.<tax-id>.*.

*.fcs_gx_report.txt
The action report: one row per action, with 8 tab-separated columns. seq_id; start_pos, end_pos (the range to act on); seq_len (the whole contig); action; div (the contaminant division); agg_cont_cov (the percentage of the range covered by alignments to that division); top_tax_name (the best-matching organism). The documentation warns that the real contaminant may be a different species, or even a different genus, from top_tax_name.
*.taxonomy.rpt
The intermediate per-sequence report. It lists up to four best taxa with their coverage and score, and a result column (primary-div, contaminant, contaminant(human), low-coverage, repeat, inconclusive, …). Contigs are split at runs of 10 or more N. These pieces get IDs like ctg~1..212539; pieces of a chimera get ~~start..end. Read this file when an action looks wrong.
actionmeaningwhat fcs.py clean genome does
EXCLUDEthe whole sequence is foreignremoves it from clean.fasta, writes it to contam.fasta
TRIMforeign stretch at the start or endcuts it off and writes it to contam.fasta. GenBank does not accept sequences that start or end with N, so the end is removed rather than masked.
FIXforeign stretch inside the sequencereplaces it with N (hard-masks it); the stretch also goes to contam.fasta
SPLITnever written by the screen; you substitute it for FIX when the insert looks like a misassembly (sed -i 's/FIX/SPLIT/g' report.txt)cuts the sequence into two pieces at the range
REVIEW, REVIEW_RARE, INFOweaker or special evidence (REVIEW_RARE: prokaryote in prokaryote, under 1 % of the genome)nothing. Check these yourself and change them to EXCLUDE, TRIM or FIX if you agree. The documentation notes that many REVIEW sequences are in fact contaminants.
MITOCHONDRION, PLASTIDan organelle genome among the nuclear contigsremoves the whole sequence (source: fasta.cpp)

After cleaning, sequences shorter than 200 bp are dropped from clean.fasta. The command-line option is --min-seq-len, default 200. NCBI's WGS submission page likewise asks for contigs longer than 199 nt and no N at either end.

Organelles are not contamination, but they do not belong in the nuclear assembly. The mitochondrial genome is your animal's own DNA, so GX classifies it under your animal's division. In a eukaryote, the MITOCHONDRION action catches a contig only when more than 80 % of it is covered by mitochondrial hits. A multimer contig made of several tandem copies of the mitogenome should pass that test. A nuclear contig carrying a short NUMT should not, and it must stay. Assemble the mitogenome separately (section 11) and submit it as its own record. NCBI's FASTA format marks such records with [location=mitochondrion].

Hardware and database

  • Database: the "all" GX database. Its manifest, read on 2026-10-31, lists all.gxi at 321.2 GB and all.gxs at 177.3 GB, about 464 GiB in total. The docs plan for 470 GiB of disk. It holds 3.03 million sequences (709 Gbp) and was built on 2023-01-24 (all.meta.jsonl). It is made from representative RefSeq prokaryotes and eukaryotes, RefSeq viruses and plasmids, and extra GenBank fungi, nematodes, protists and bacteria. Eukaryote sequences under 10 kb are left out. A small test-only database checks the installation; keep it in a different folder from "all".
  • RAM: the documentation asks for a host with 512 GiB of shared memory, so that the whole database stays in RAM. If the database has to be read from disk, runs can be "as much as a 10000x" slower. The fastest setup copies the database into a RAM disk (tmpfs, such as /dev/shm). Without root rights, GX memory-maps the files from disk and slowly fills the RAM as it runs.
  • CPU and time: up to 48 cores. The troubleshooting page lists run times with 48 cores and the database in RAM: Escherichia coli (4.6 Mb) 10 s, Drosophila melanogaster (144 Mb) 34 s, human (3.1 Gb) 6 min 57 s, wheat (14.6 Gb) 17 min 23 s. Downloading the database from the NCBI FTP site takes from 40 minutes to several hours.
  • Input rules: FASTA or gzipped FASTA. Sequence IDs must be unique, shorter than 50 characters, and contain only letters, digits and - _ . : * #. Each sequence must be shorter than 2 Gbp. Runs of 10 or more N are split during alignment.
  • FCS-adaptor needs only an ordinary computer.

Commands

Both tools run inside a container. The runner script fcs.py downloads the Docker image itself; for Singularity you download the .sif image file. In fcs.py as of 2025-03 the default image version is 0.5.5.

# 1. runner script + Singularity images (skip the .sif lines if you use Docker)
curl -LO https://github.com/ncbi/fcs/raw/main/dist/fcs.py
curl -LO https://github.com/ncbi/fcs/raw/main/dist/run_fcsadaptor.sh && chmod 755 run_fcsadaptor.sh
curl https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/TOOLS/FCS/releases/latest/fcs-gx.sif -Lo fcs-gx.sif
curl https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/TOOLS/FCS/releases/latest/fcs-adaptor.sif -Lo fcs-adaptor.sif
export FCS_DEFAULT_IMAGE=fcs-gx.sif

# 2. the GX database (~464 GiB) to disk, then check it against the manifest
SOURCE_DB_MANIFEST="https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/TOOLS/FCS/database/latest/all.manifest"
LOCAL_DB=/big/disk/fcs
python3 fcs.py db get   --mft "$SOURCE_DB_MANIFEST" --dir "$LOCAL_DB/gxdb"
python3 fcs.py db check --mft "$SOURCE_DB_MANIFEST" --dir "$LOCAL_DB/gxdb"
# optional, needs root: copy it into RAM for fast repeated runs
sudo mkdir /my_tmpfs && sudo mount -t tmpfs tmpfs /my_tmpfs -o size=470G
python3 fcs.py db get --mft "$LOCAL_DB/gxdb/all.manifest" --dir /my_tmpfs/gxdb

# 3. adaptors and vectors (--euk for eukaryotes)
mkdir adaptor_out
./run_fcsadaptor.sh --fasta-input asm.fa.gz --output-dir ./adaptor_out --euk \
    --container-engine singularity --image fcs-adaptor.sif

# 4. foreign organisms; 10090 = Mus musculus in NCBI Taxonomy (use your own species' tax-id)
printf 'GX_NUM_CORES=32\n' > env.txt
python3 fcs.py --env-file env.txt screen genome --fasta asm.fa.gz \
    --out-dir ./gx_out/ --gx-db "$LOCAL_DB/gxdb" --tax-id 10090
#   → gx_out/asm.fa.10090.fcs_gx_report.txt   gx_out/asm.fa.10090.taxonomy.rpt

# 5. read the report, edit REVIEW rows by hand if needed, then clean
grep -w EXCLUDE gx_out/asm.fa.10090.fcs_gx_report.txt | cut -f 1 | sort -u | wc -l
zcat asm.fa.gz | python3 fcs.py clean genome \
    --action-report gx_out/asm.fa.10090.fcs_gx_report.txt \
    --output clean.fasta --contam-fasta-out contam.fasta
#   prints e.g. "Applied 405 actions; 402417 bps dropped; 1922 bps hardmasked."
cat clean.fasta | python3 fcs.py clean genome \
    --action-report adaptor_out/fcs_adaptor_report.txt \
    --output clean2.fasta --contam-fasta-out contam2.fasta
  • FCS-adaptor rules (from its output documentation): a hit at an end, or within 100 bp of an end, is trimmed to that end. A hit more than 100 bp inside a contig is reported as ACTION_TRIM, but the tool's own cleaned_sequences/ file leaves it in place. fcs.py clean genome instead splits the contig at that hit. To mask it with N instead, change the action to FIX. A sequence that is more than 75 % adaptor, or has less than 200 bp left over, is reported as ACTION_EXCLUDE.
  • Options worth knowing (put them in the --env-file). GX_NUM_CORES sets the number of cores. GX_ALIGN_EXCLUDE_TAXA ignores hits to listed tax-ids; it works only for tax-ids that are themselves in the database, not for higher groups. GX_EXTRA_CONTAM_DIVS adds contaminant divisions. Use --no-report-analytics to stop usage statistics being sent to NCBI.
  • Re-screen after cleaning. When contamination was heavy, run the screen again on clean.fasta.
  • Reasons for false calls listed in the docs: contaminants inside the database itself, groups poorly represented in it (sponges, crustaceans), recent horizontal gene transfer, repeats, and conserved sequence such as rDNA and mtDNA. Before accepting long contigs (over 50 kb) with low coverage (under 20 %), look them up in taxonomy.rpt.

The easy route: Galaxy

On usegalaxy.org the GX database is already on the server. The Galaxy tool NCBI FCS GX (wrapper version 0.5.5) has two modes. screen takes the assembly FASTA, and under "Taxonomy entry" you pick either a GX division or an NCBI taxonomic identifier. clean takes the FASTA and the action report and returns "Cleaned Fasta" and "Fasta for EXCLUDE entries". Its "Minimum sequence length to keep" is 200 by default. The Galaxy Training Network tutorial "Screening assembled genomes for contamination using NCBI FCS" has a ready workflow, NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen Workflow, which runs GX screen, GX clean, FCS-adaptor and a final clean. Galaxy setup is covered in lesson 1. For a 2–3 Gb mammal, the uploads are the hardest part.

A picture to go with it: BlobToolKit

FCS gives you a table. BlobToolKit gives you a picture of the same question, and it uses a second kind of evidence: read coverage. Its blob plot draws every contig as a circle. The x-axis is GC content, the y-axis is read coverage, circle size is contig length, and the colour is the taxon assigned from BLAST or DIAMOND hits (the bestsum rule adds up hit scores per taxon). Contigs of your animal form one cloud at the genome's GC and the expected coverage. A bacterium often sits apart, with different GC and very different coverage, because its DNA was present in a different amount from the animal's. Mitochondrial contigs sit far above the cloud, because mitochondrial DNA is present in many copies per cell (in the long reads of one of our rodent projects the mitogenome had a depth of thousands).

# BlobToolKit 4.x (pip install blobtoolkit); the sanger-tol/blobtoolkit Nextflow pipeline runs the whole set; ~/taxdump = NCBI taxonomy files, downloaded once
blobtools create --fasta asm.fa --taxid 10090 --taxdump ~/taxdump AssemblyName
blobtools add --cov reads_vs_asm.bam AssemblyName
blobtools add --hits blast.out --hits diamond.out --taxrule bestsumorder --taxdump ~/taxdump AssemblyName
blobtools add --busco full_table.tsv AssemblyName
blobtools view --local AssemblyName    # opens the interactive viewer in a browser

  • As far as our project notes show, none of our assemblies (species A–D, the desmans) has been screened with FCS-GX yet. FCS-GX should run before any of them goes to NCBI.
  • The tax-ids and divisions are fixed by the species. A rodent such as Mus musculus (10090) has the BLAST name "rodents", so the GX division is anml:rodents. Desmana moschata is 182682, BLAST name "insectivores", so the division is the broader anml:mammals. Looked up in NCBI Taxonomy on 2026-10-31.
  • Organelles: the hifiasm primary assembly of our species A (an unpublished rodent genome) contains dozens of mitochondria-like contigs (more than a megabase in total), which must be removed from the nuclear set. One of them is several tandem copies of the mitogenome. The same genome also holds dozens of full-length NUMTs, which belong to the nuclear genome. Telling the two apart is the job of section 11, not of FCS.
  • Memory: DRAGEN has 503 GB of RAM and therioserver about 499 GiB. Both are just under the 512 GiB that the FCS-GX documentation asks for. We have not tried it on either server, so whether the 464-GiB database runs at full speed there is unknown. Galaxy avoids the question.
