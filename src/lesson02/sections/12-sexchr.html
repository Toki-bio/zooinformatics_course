12Sex chromosomes in an assembly

In most animals one sex carries two different sex chromosomes. Sequence an individual of that sex and its X (or Z) and Y (or W) each come in one copy instead of two. That single fact changes the read depth, the k-mer spectrum, the GenomeScope estimate, how the assembler sorts contigs into haplotypes, and how you should call variants. This section shows what to expect and how to find the sex-linked contigs in your own assembly.

Sex chromosome systems in animals

XY system
Females are XX, males XY. The male is the heterogametic sex (it makes two kinds of sperm, X-carrying and Y-carrying); the female is the homogametic sex. Therian mammals (placentals and marsupials), many insects such as Drosophila, and some fish, amphibians and reptiles.
ZW system
The mirror image: females are ZW (heterogametic), males ZZ. Birds, snakes, butterflies and moths (Lepidoptera).
X0 and Z0
The Y (or W) has been lost completely, so one sex has a single X and no partner: X0 males in some grasshoppers; X0 animals also occur in a few rodents (some voles, Japanese spiny rats, mole voles).
Homomorphic / heteromorphic
Heteromorphic sex chromosomes look different under the microscope (the human Y is much smaller than the X). Homomorphic ones look alike and differ only in a small region, sometimes a single sex-determining gene, as in pufferfish.
Young vs old
When X and Y stop exchanging DNA (recombination), the Y slowly loses genes and fills with repeats. An old, degenerated Y differs from its X over most of its length; a young one is still almost identical. Age alone does not decide it: python snakes and ratite birds have homomorphic sex chromosomes about 140 and 120 million years old.
PAR
The pseudoautosomal region: a stretch at the tip of X and Y that still recombines and is present on both. A male has two copies of the PAR, just as of an autosome. In the human Y, PAR1 is 2.77 Mb and PAR2 330 kb.
Gametologs
Pairs of genes, one on the X and one on the Y (or Z and W), that descend from the same ancestral gene. Old pairs differ a lot; young pairs are so similar that software can take them for two alleles of one gene.
Hemizygous
Present in one copy in a diploid cell. The X of a male mammal, the Z of a female bird and every Y or W are hemizygous outside the PAR.
Simple explanation with examples: who is heterogametic

Ask which sex has two different sex chromosomes. That sex is heterogametic, and an individual of that sex gives you both sex chromosomes in one genome, each at single copy.

species system heterogametic sequence a ... to get both sex chromosomes house mouse XY male male (XY) zebra finch ZW female female (ZW) silkworm moth ZW female female (ZW) grasshopper (some) X0 male either: there is no Y to find platypus 5X + 5Y male male (all ten sex chromosomes)

Sequencing the homogametic sex (a female mouse, a male bird) gives two copies of X (or Z), so the X looks exactly like an autosome in depth and k-mers, and the Y (or W) is simply absent from the assembly.

What one copy does to read depth

Reads are sampled in proportion to how many copies of a sequence the cell has. In the heterogametic sex the autosomes and the PAR have two copies, while the X (or Z) and the Y (or W) outside the PAR have one. So, mapped back to the assembly, X and Y contigs show about half the autosomal depth. The homogametic sex shows the X at full depth.

What to notice. In the heterogametic sex, X and Y (or Z and W) both sit near 0.5, the PAR contig sits at 1 with the autosomes, and two unpurged haplotigs also sit at 0.5. Depth alone cannot tell them apart: a haplotig has a near-identical partner elsewhere in the assembly, an X contig does not. In the homogametic sex there is nothing at 0.5 except haplotigs, and the X hides among the autosomes. With high noise (short contigs, low coverage) the two clouds merge; then use the other evidence below.

What one copy does to the k-mer spectrum and GenomeScope

In lesson 1 the heterozygous peak sits at half coverage because each allele lies on one haplotype only. A hemizygous X k-mer also lies on one chromosome copy, so it is seen at the same half coverage and falls into the same peak. GenomeScope has no way to know these k-mers are not heterozygous. In the heterogametic sex this has two consequences:

  • Heterozygosity is overestimated. The het peak is taller than heterozygosity alone would make it.
  • The size estimate counts X and Y at half weight. Each hemizygous k-mer adds half a homozygous-peak's worth of sightings, so the estimate is about autosomes + (X + Y) / 2, while the assembly of that animal contains autosomes + X + Y. Expect the assembly to be larger than the estimate by about half of the sex chromosomes, before any other cause.
Simple explanation with examples: the size estimate of a male

Take a mammal with 1,000 Mb of autosomes, a 50 Mb X and a 20 Mb Y, sequenced at 30× k-mer coverage. Every autosomal position is seen ~30 times, every X or Y position ~15 times.

sightings from autosomes 1,000 Mb × 30 = 30,000 M sightings from X 50 Mb × 15 = 750 M sightings from Y 20 Mb × 15 = 300 M total 31,050 M ÷ homozygous peak (30) = 1,035 Mb (GenomeScope-style estimate) the assembly of this male holds 1,000 + 50 + 20 = 1,070 Mb the female's assembly would hold 1,000 + 50 = 1,050 Mb

So for a male the k-mer estimate is about (X + Y) / 2 below the assembly. That is fine for choosing an assembler's expected genome size, which only needs to be roughly right, but keep it in mind when you judge whether an assembly is "too big".

Why Y and W are hard to assemble

Once X and Y stop recombining, the Y (or W) accumulates repeats, and assemblers break wherever reads cannot span a repeat. The complete human Y (Rhie et al. 2023), sequenced with PacBio HiFi and ONT ultra-long reads, is 62.46 Mb; the previous reference Y (GRCh38) had 30.8 Mb of its 57.2 Mb as unresolved gaps, and about 30 Mb of the new sequence is the heterochromatic block on the long arm. The human Y is made of these sequence classes:

  • Ampliconic regions (~10 Mb in the human Y): long segments present in many near-identical copies, carrying multi-copy gene families expressed in testis.
  • Palindromes: an ampliconic segment followed by its own reverse complement. In the five palindromes that lie inside X-degenerate regions the two arms are 99.84–99.96 % identical, so reads from the two arms look the same. Even in the complete T2T Y, Strand-seq found one inversion error inside palindrome P5; the authors left it in place because the inversion also occurs as a real variant between men.
  • Heterochromatin and satellites: megabases of short tandem repeats (satellite arrays such as HSat1B and HSat3 in human), often collapsed or missing in normal assemblies.
  • X-degenerate regions (~8.7 Mb): single-copy remains of the ancestral chromosome, with gametologs of X genes. These assemble best.
  • X-transposed region (XTR): a block that came from the X and is still very similar to it, which confuses read mapping between the two.
  • PAR: identical in kind on X and Y. In the human assembly graph, the X and Y components were joined to each other only through the PARs. PAR1 is also rich in GA repeats where HiFi coverage drops.

Add the half depth of every sex chromosome in the heterogametic sex, and it is no surprise that the Vertebrate Genomes Project (Rhie et al. 2021) found its sex chromosome assemblies more fragmented than the autosomes, "probably owing to their lower sequencing depth and high repeat content". Even so, it assembled Z and W in three birds, X and/or Y in several mammals, and all ten sex chromosomes of the platypus, by sequencing the heterogametic sex: for example, it chose a female Anna's hummingbird (Calypte anna) partly because she carries both Z and W.

Where X and Y end up in hifiasm's output

A diploid-aware assembler such as hifiasm tries to split each chromosome pair into two haplotypes. X and Y are not a normal pair: they are allelic only in the PAR (and, if young, in similar gametolog stretches), and different everywhere else. Where they land depends on the mode (section 07 explains the products and trio binning).

  • Trio binning (hifiasm -1 pat.yak -2 mat.yak): reads are sorted by the parents' k-mers, so hap1 is paternal and hap2 maternal. A son's Y comes from the father and his X from the mother, so the Y lands in hap1 and the X in hap2. The hifiasm FAQ notes that for a human male "the paternal haplotype should be larger than the maternal haplotype". In a ZW bird the daughter's W comes from the mother and her Z from the father; the VGP grouped the zebra finch Z in the paternal and the W in the maternal assembly. Because neither haplotype then holds both, the VGP added "the heterogametic sex chromosome from the unchosen haplotype" to the representative assembly. They also found a pitfall: in low-divergence regions of the father's and mother's X (or Z), a few offspring reads went to the wrong parent and made a duplicate, low-coverage X (or Z) fragment that had to be removed.
  • Hi-C phasing (hifiasm --h1 R1.fq.gz --h2 R2.fq.gz): Hi-C links keep each chromosome in one haplotype, and the hifiasm documentation says it "cleanly separated chrX and chrY for a human male dataset". But it cannot know which parent each chromosome came from: "contigs from different parental chromosomes are randomly mixed". So the X can be in hap1 and the Y in hap2, or the reverse, and the autosomes are shuffled the same way. Look for them in both.
  • Primary / alternate and hifiasm's default bp.hap1/bp.hap2: the primary contigs are one copy of each region. Divergent X and Y sequence has no allelic partner, so both are kept in the primary assembly at half depth (that is what we saw in our species A, below). The rest of this point is our inference from how hifiasm and purge_dups treat near-identical sequence; the hifiasm documentation does not describe it, and we have not checked it in an assembly: the PAR is a real allelic pair, so we expect one copy in the primary and the other in the alternate (or purged), and very similar young gametologs or the XTR may be treated the same way, leaving a gap in the Y. The partially phased bp.hap1/bp.hap2 sets are not chromosome-scale; X and Y pieces can sit in either.

Whatever you choose, record the sex of the specimen in the metadata; and if it was not recorded, the depth test below will usually tell you.

Finding the sex-linked contigs

The VGP identified sex chromosomes in heterogametic samples "based on half coverage, homology alignments to sex chromosomes in other species, and the presence of sex chromosome-specific genes". Use several independent lines of evidence, because each one has its own false positives.

  1. Depth ratio
    Map the reads of the same animal back to the assembly (minimap2 -ax map-ont or map-hifi), measure depth in windows with mosdepth, and take each contig's median window depth divided by the median of all windows. Hemizygous contigs sit near 0.5. Use the median, not the mean, because collapsed repeats pull the mean up.
  2. Rule out haplotigs
    A half-depth contig with a ≥ 90 %-identity counterpart elsewhere in the same assembly is more likely an unpurged second allele than an X. Align the assembly to itself (minimap2 -x asm5 -DP, the setting purge_dups uses) or check the purge_dups result (section 06).
  3. No heterozygous SNPs on the X
    A male has one X, so true heterozygous SNPs cannot exist outside the PAR. Call variants and count het SNPs per Mb per contig: X contigs should have almost none, autosomes the genome's normal rate, PAR contigs the normal rate too. The leftover het calls on the X are your false-positive rate (see "In our projects").
  4. Synteny to a related chromosome-level genome
    Align the contigs to a related species' assembly that has named sex chromosomes (minimap2 -x asm20 for "several percent" divergence) and see which chromosome each half-depth contig hits. The X is usually well conserved between related mammals; a Y or W often has no good counterpart.
  5. Compare male and female reads
    Map short reads of both sexes to the assembly and compare depth per contig or window: X (or Z) shows a female:male ratio near 2:1 (male:female for Z), Y (or W) reads come from one sex only. The tools below do this. The k-mer version (YGS) needs no mapping.
  6. Known sex-linked genes
    For example the mammalian SRY on the Y (the male-determining gene); X gene content is conserved enough that gene annotation or BUSCO-style protein matches confirm the call.

Notes on the commands. -n skips per-base output and -x (fast mode) skips CIGAR details, both recommended in the mosdepth README; -Q 10 drops reads with mapping quality below 10, which removes repeats but also part of the X if it is repeat-rich, so compare with -Q 0. In the PAF, column 1 is the contig, 6 the target chromosome and 11 the alignment block length. -g het keeps sites where a sample is heterozygous. The -DP pair in the self-alignment ignores each contig's hit to itself.

ctg_B and ctg_C have the same depth. Only the partner test and the het SNPs separate them. ctg_D has no counterpart in the relative because the Y changes fast between species; female reads (YGS, findZX) would show that none of its single-copy k-mers occur in females.

In our projects: the species A male

Species A, an unpublished rodent genome, was assembled from long reads only with hifiasm (--ont; primary bp.p_ctg, about a thousand contigs). Values below are rounded. Its sex was not recorded with the sample; the assembly told us.

  • Depth. All reads mapped back to the primary give a median depth of a few tens × in 10-kb windows; about three quarters of the windows lie at 0.75–1.25× of the median. More than 100 Mb sits in contigs at half depth.
  • Not haplotigs. The half-depth contigs ≥ 1 Mb (several dozen) have no ≥ 90 %-identity counterpart in the assembly.
  • Synteny. Most of their alignment (close to nine tenths) to the published chromosome-level genome of a close relative goes to one chromosome, over a hundred times more than its share of the genome would predict, and a few percent to one small chromosome. So these contigs are the X (+ Y), and the animal is almost certainly male. They were excluded from the autosomal PSMC analysis.
  • A built-in false-positive control. Because a male cannot be heterozygous on the X, variants were called on the long half-depth contigs (about 100 Mb, at roughly half the genome-wide depth). A few of them had several hundred het SNPs per Mb, so they behave as diploid (PAR or duplicated) and were set aside. On the rest there were, unfiltered, under a tenth of the autosomal het rate at the same depth, and after keeping only allele fractions 0.35–0.65, about 1 % of the autosomal filtered rate. So roughly 1 % of het calls are false after the allele-fraction filter (an upper bound, since mismapped paralogs count too). The false hets had a median allele fraction below 0.3, against close to 0.5 for real ones.
  • The spectrum. GenomeScope2 on the long reads gave a haploid size about a tenth smaller than the assembly. Counting the hemizygous sequence at half weight accounts for less than a third of that difference, so most of the gap has other causes.

Caveat. The read headers suggest the reads may come from a CycloneSEQ instrument rather than ONT; the variant caller used an ONT R10.4.1 model, so the 1 % figure applies to that combination of reads and model.

Pitfalls

  • Half depth is not proof. Unpurged haplotigs, contigs from CNV regions, GC-poor sequence that sequences badly, and the ends of contigs also show low depth. Combine with partner, het SNP and synteny tests.
  • Do not purge the X. Depth-based duplicate removal tests contigs below a depth cutoff for duplication. A hemizygous X contig with no partner should survive, but check: count half-depth Mb before and after purging.
  • Do not run PSMC or heterozygosity on the X of a male. Exclude sex-linked contigs from autosomal analyses (as we did for species A); otherwise they look like long runs of homozygosity.
  • Variant calling on the heterogametic sex needs ploidy 1 outside the PAR. Diploid callers will report some false "het" calls on the X; use them as an error estimate, or call with haploid ploidy there.
  • Look for X and Y in both haplotypes of a Hi-C-phased assembly, and expect the Y (or W) to be fragmented and incomplete in any assembly short of a T2T effort.
