10Gene completeness with BUSCO

The size, N50 and QV of an assembly (section 08) say nothing direct about genes. BUSCO answers a gene question: of a fixed list of genes that almost every relative of your animal has exactly once, how many are in the assembly whole, how many twice, how many broken, and how many not found?

What a BUSCO is

BUSCO
Short for Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs. It is the name of the program and also of each gene on its list: "a BUSCO" is one marker gene.
Ortholog
The same gene in different species, inherited from their common ancestor (for example the gene for one particular enzyme in a mouse and in a shrew). Not to be confused with a paralog, a second copy made by duplication inside one genome.
Single-copy, near-universal
BUSCO picks genes that are present in at least 90 % of the species of a group and present once in at least 90 % of those (the rule is made stricter for groups with more than 100 species). So a healthy assembly should contain almost all of them, each once.
OrthoDB
The database of ortholog groups from which the lists are built, by the same lab (Zdobnov group, Geneva). Its release number is part of every dataset name.
Lineage dataset
One list of marker genes for one group of organisms, for example mammalia_odb10 (9,226 genes) or vertebrata_odb12.2. The suffix is the OrthoDB release: odb10, odb12 and odb12.2. Each release is clustered from scratch, so the gene lists differ and numbers from different releases cannot be compared.

Each dataset folder holds, per gene, a profile HMM (in hmms/), the reference protein sequences of the species used (refseq_db.faa.gz), the minimum score for a real hit (scores_cutoff) and the expected length (lengths_cutoff).

How BUSCO looks for them in a genome

  1. Basic statistics
    BBTools stats.sh reports sizes and N50. A run of more than 10 Ns (--contig_break) is counted as a break between contigs. These numbers are not used for the gene score.
  2. Place the reference proteins on your contigs
    In genome mode for animals and other eukaryotes, the default since BUSCO 5.7.0 is miniprot, a protein-to-genome aligner by Heng Li. It aligns the dataset's reference proteins to the assembly across introns and tolerates frameshifts. Older choices are still available: --metaeuk (MetaEuk, default in BUSCO 5.0-5.6) and --augustus (Augustus, a gene predictor trained on each lineage; default up to 4.0).
  3. Score each candidate against the profile
    HMMER compares each translated candidate with the profile HMM of its BUSCO. Candidates under the score cutoff are ignored.
  4. Check the length
    For odb12.2 a hit is "complete" when its length lies between a lower bound (the smallest of 90 % of the median length of that gene, median − 2 × sMAD, or 80 % of the profile length) and 1.5 × median. Shorter hits are fragments. odb10 uses a mean ± 2 standard deviations rule; odb12 uses 80 % of the profile length.
  5. Sort and clean up
    A gene region can count for one BUSCO only; overlapping hits keep the better one; extra copies scoring below 85 % of the best copy are dropped. In BUSCO 6.1 with miniprot, a copy with an internal stop codon is also dropped when another complete copy has none.

BUSCO prints the result in one line, which is also the form to report: C:97.6%[S:95.9%,D:1.6%],F:0.9%,M:1.6%,n:9226,E:8.5% (the made-up example below). Here n is the number of genes in the dataset.

Try it

A toy assembly scored by toy BUSCO

Presets:

Toy rules (what the figure computes)
  • Each gene has a span of 5-60 kb on the genome (exons plus introns) and 4-14 exons. Contig ends fall at random, on average one per 0.6 × N50 (for random breaks the N50 is about 1.7 times the mean contig length).
  • A gene cut by contig ends is scored on the piece that holds most of its exons. At least 90 % of the coding sequence on one piece: complete. 20-90 %: fragmented. Less: missing (too little to pass the score cutoff).
  • Base errors land in the coding sequence (0.6-3 kb per gene). An insertion or deletion is a frameshift that miniprot aligns through with a penalty. A substitution creates a stop codon in 23 of 549 cases (the share of single-base changes in the 61 sense codons that give TAA, TAG or TGA). Four or more errors in one gene lower the score enough to make it fragmented (a toy threshold).
  • A gene in a doubled region has a second copy with its own contig ends and errors. Two complete copies: duplicated, unless exactly one of them has a stop codon; then that one is dropped and the gene is single-copy (BUSCO 6.1 rule).
  • A gene in an absent region is missing, unless its haplotig copy survives.
  • Presets only set the sliders to values of the kind seen in real projects; the toy's percentages are not predictions for any real assembly.
Simple explanation with examples: the moving-house checklist

You move house. Before the move you write a list of 100 things nearly every home has exactly one of: one fridge, one kettle, one front-door key. After the move you open the boxes and tick the list.

kettle found, whole S single-copy complete key found twice D your partner packed one too (two haplotypes) lamp only the shade F broken in two boxes (a contig ends inside the gene) fridge not found M the box never arrived (region not assembled) toaster found, whole, but a C, counted in E fuse is blown (complete, with an internal stop codon)

The checklist tells you nothing about the books, the paintings or anything not on the list. In the same way BUSCO says nothing about repeats, satellite DNA, centromeres or the large part of a mammal genome that lies outside these genes. And if you use a checklist written for a different kind of household (a lineage too distant), items will be "missing" that were never expected.

Example with numbers (illustrative counts). mammalia_odb10 has 9,226 genes. If 9,000 are complete, of which 150 twice, 80 fragmented and 146 not found, BUSCO reports C = 9,000 ÷ 9,226 = 97.6 %, D = 150 ÷ 9,226 = 1.6 %, S = 8,850 ÷ 9,226 = 95.9 %, F = 0.9 %, M = 1.6 %.

Reading the numbers

  • Compare like with like. The BUSCO user guide recommends comparing your result with the same assessment of public assemblies of close relatives. Same BUSCO version, same dataset (and release), same mode. An odb10 result and an odb12.2 result are different tests.
  • D rises when haplotypes are not separated. A primary or pseudohaploid assembly (section 07) should have D close to that of a reference relative (a few percent at most). A D of tens of percent in an animal almost always means both parental copies were kept; run purge_dups (section 06) and measure again. A haplotype-resolved assembly is measured one haplotype file at a time.
  • F and M rise with fragmentation and with lost sequence. Short contigs cut genes; gaps hide exons; sequence missing from one file (for example a single haplotype from a partially phased assembly) turns genes into M, not F.
  • Base errors are measured better by QV. With miniprot a frameshift does not stop the alignment, so moderate error rates move few genes out of C. Use Merqury QV (section 08) for base accuracy, and BUSCO for gene presence. The two answer different questions: an assembly can have a high QV and a low BUSCO (sequence correct but missing) or the reverse.
  • E is a caution, not a score. The user guide says miniprot predictions often contain internal stop codons and are not fit for downstream use as they are; MetaEuk and Augustus predictions never contain them. If you need the gene sequences (for a phylogeny, for example), rerun with --augustus or --metaeuk.

Limits. BUSCO genes are a sample of conserved genes; a high C does not mean repeats, gene families, sex chromosomes or GC-rich regions are assembled. Miniprot may underperform on genomes distant from the species in the dataset (user guide); then try --metaeuk, which also needs less memory than miniprot (user guide). A missing ortholog can be replaced by a full-length close paralog that BUSCO counts as complete (user guide). Too broad a lineage gives fewer genes and less resolution; a wrong lineage gives false "missing" genes.

Choosing the lineage

  • Use the most specific dataset that contains your animal (user guide). List them with busco --list-datasets. For mammals there are, among others, mammalia, eutheria, euarchontoglires, glires, rodentia (odb12 and odb12.2 only), laurasiatheria, carnivora, primates; for reptiles sauropsida and squamata (odb12 and odb12.2 only).
  • If you also want comparisons with older published numbers, run a second time with the dataset they used (often mammalia_odb10 or vertebrata_odb10).
  • --auto-lineage-euk lets BUSCO choose: it scores the assembly against the general eukaryote set, then places it on a tree with SEPP and picks the dataset at that position. The user guide warns that SEPP 4.5.6 gives wrong assignments; use SEPP 4.5.5. For a vertebrate you know, naming the lineage yourself is simpler and faster.
  • Writing -l mammalia without a suffix makes BUSCO 6.1 add the default release, _odb12.2. Write the suffix to be explicit.

Commands

# install (versions from the BUSCO 6.1.0 user guide)
conda create -n busco6 -c conda-forge -c bioconda busco=6.1.0
conda activate busco6

# download datasets once, into a shared folder (--download must come last)
busco --list-datasets
busco --download_path /data/busco_downloads --download mammalia_odb12.2
busco --download_path /data/busco_downloads --download mammalia_odb10

# score one assembly (genome mode; miniprot is used by default for eukaryotes)
busco -i asm.p_ctg.fa -m genome -l mammalia_odb12.2 -c 16 \
      -o asm_mammalia12 --out_path busco_runs \
      --download_path /data/busco_downloads --offline --opt-out-run-stats

# the same with the older release, for comparison with published numbers
busco -i asm.p_ctg.fa -m genome -l mammalia_odb10 -c 16 \
      -o asm_mammalia10 --out_path busco_runs \
      --download_path /data/busco_downloads --offline --opt-out-run-stats

# one figure for several runs: collect their short_summary JSON files in one folder
mkdir -p busco_plot
cp busco_runs/*/short_summary.specific.*.json busco_plot/
busco --plot busco_plot --plot_percentages            # → busco_plot/busco_figure.png
-i, -m genome
Input FASTA (gzipped is accepted since 6.0) and the mode. Other modes: transcriptome and proteins (for an annotated gene set).
-l
Lineage dataset name, or a path to a dataset folder.
-c
Threads. Without it BUSCO uses one CPU (user guide).
-o, --out_path, -f
Run name, parent folder, and overwrite an existing run of that name (old results are lost). -r continues an interrupted run instead.
--download_path, --offline
Where datasets are kept (default: busco_downloads/ in the current folder), and never try to download. Use both on compute nodes without internet.
--opt-out-run-stats
Do not send the anonymous usage report (on by default since 5.6.0). See the pitfall below.
--miniprot / --metaeuk / --augustus
Choose the gene finder; miniprot is the default for eukaryotes in genome mode.

What comes out

short_summary.specific.<dataset>.<run>.txt / .json
The one-line result, the counts per category, the dataset and the versions of every tool used. The .json is what --plot reads.
run_<dataset>/full_table.tsv
One line per BUSCO hit: Busco id, Status (Complete, Duplicated, Fragmented, Missing), Sequence (the contig), start, end, strand, score, length. Use it to find which genes are missing or duplicated and on which contigs, for example to check whether the duplicated ones sit on small contigs that look like haplotigs.
run_<dataset>/missing_busco_list.tsv
The missing gene IDs only.
run_<dataset>/busco_sequences/
Protein sequences and GFF coordinates of the single-copy, multi-copy and fragmented hits. The miniprot pipeline writes no nucleotide sequences, because they often contain internal stop codons.
logs/busco.log
The full log; read it when numbers look odd.

Pitfall: BUSCO "hangs" after it has finished. Unless --opt-out-run-stats is given, BUSCO ends every run by looking up the machine's country at ipinfo.io and posting a usage report to busco-data.ezlab.org. In the 6.1.0 source both requests are made without a time limit. So on a server whose outgoing connections are blocked or very slow, the program can sit doing nothing after the results are written. Always add --opt-out-run-stats on servers.

  • Calibrate with a relative. The species A primary scored at least as high as the published chromosome-level genome of a close relative with the same dataset. Without that comparison its score would be just a number.
  • E ≈ 9 % is a baseline. Every species A assembly, the relative's published reference and species B and C all show it. It does not track base accuracy: B and C differ more than three-fold in Merqury error rate but only about half a point in E.
  • Coverage shows up as F and M. C had less than half of B's long-read coverage, shorter contigs and a C score about 10 points lower, mostly through more fragmented and missing genes. Polishing (Medaka, then NextPolish) left C unchanged in both (within 0.1 point) while halving the error rate.
  • One haplotype loses genes as Missing. hifiasm's haplotype 1, made without Hi-C or parental data, lost about 8 points of C, almost all as missing and only about 1 % fragmented: whole regions are simply not in that file. Polishing it did not help. The first numbers sent to collaborators came from this file by mistake (a file-name pattern picked bp.hap1.p_ctg.gfa before bp.p_ctg.gfa). Check the contig names (ptg = primary, h1tg/h2tg = haplotypes) before you report.
  • Label the file. For B and C the BUSCO and QV were measured on the polished file before purge_dups, and size and N50 on the purged file; an early report mixed them.
  • The hang. BUSCO 6.1 on therioserver sat for minutes after finishing, during the usage-stats upload; --opt-out-run-stats avoids it.

A faster alternative: compleasm

compleasm (Neng Huang and Heng Li, 2023) re-implements the BUSCO test with miniprot and HMMER and the same BUSCO datasets. Its paper reports it 14 times faster than BUSCO on human assemblies, with a completeness of 99.6 % for the T2T-CHM13 human genome against BUSCO's 95.7 % (the annotation itself is 99.5 % complete). Version 0.2.10 (October 2026) reads odb10, odb12 and odb12.2; the default is odb12, so set --odb explicitly. It reports S, D, F, M and an extra class I: a gene split so that one part aligns in one place and the rest elsewhere (it counts as fragmented).

conda create -n compleasm -c conda-forge -c bioconda compleasm
compleasm download mammalia --odb odb12.2 -L /data/mb_downloads
compleasm run -a asm.p_ctg.fa -o asm_compleasm -l mammalia --odb odb12.2 -t 16 -L /data/mb_downloads
# result: asm_compleasm/summary.txt

Do not mix compleasm and BUSCO numbers in one comparison table; run the same tool on every assembly you compare.
