06Diversity from a sample: π, θW and Tajima's D

With sequences from several animals of one population you can measure diversity in two ways: by counting differences between pairs, and by counting the variable sites. In a population of constant size with no selection the two measures agree. Tajima's D (Tajima 1989) is the scaled difference between them. It is the standard first summary of a population sample, and the baseline for the selection scans in s07. It is also easy to misread, because changes in population size move it just as selection does.

1. Two ways to count diversity

Take n sequences of the same region, one per chromosome copy (a diploid animal gives two, once phased). A site where they do not all agree is a segregating site; let S be their number.

π (or k)
The average number of differences between two sequences, over all n(n−1)/2 pairs. Per base, divide by the region length. Each variable site adds to π in proportion to how many pairs it separates: a site where one sequence differs separates only n−1 pairs, a site split half and half separates the most.
θW (Watterson's estimator)
S / a1, with a1 = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/(n−1). Every variable site counts once, whatever its frequency. The divisor corrects for the fact that more sequences reveal more sites.

Both estimate the same quantity, θ = 4Neμ (lesson 4 uses the same θ), but they weight sites differently: θW counts rare and common variants equally, π counts mostly the common ones. Comparing them is therefore a comparison of the site frequency spectrum (lesson 4, s05): how many variants sit in 1, 2, 3 … of the n sequences.

D = (k − S/a1) / √(e1S + e2S(S−1))
a1 = Σi=1..n−1 1/i    a2 = Σi=1..n−1 1/i²
b1 = (n+1) / (3(n−1))    b2 = 2(n²+n+3) / (9n(n−1))
c1 = b1 − 1/a1    c2 = b2 − (n+2)/(a1n) + a2/a1²
e1 = c1/a1    e2 = c2/(a1²+a2)

The numerator is π minus θW, both for the whole region (not per base). The denominator is the expected spread of that difference under neutrality, so under neutrality D stays near 0 and values beyond about ±2 are unusual. That ±2 is only a rough rule of thumb, not a test threshold: even under neutrality the mean of D is not exactly 0 (the toy below shows it slightly negative), and a real test needs a null distribution (part 4).

2. Reading the sign

  • D near 0. Rare and common variants in the proportions a steady neutral population gives.
  • D negative. More rare variants than expected: many sites carried by one or two sequences. θW counts them fully, π hardly at all. A recent expansion makes this: a large population collects many new, still rare mutations. So does a recent selective sweep in its genomic neighbourhood (the shallow trees of s07).
  • D positive. Too few rare variants, many at middle frequency. A recent bottleneck does this (rare variants are lost first), as does balancing selection, which keeps two alleles at intermediate frequency for a long time.

3. Demography moves D too

Selection acts on one locus and its neighbours. A change in population size acts on every locus of the genome at once. Simonsen et al., as summarised by Nielsen (2005), showed that Tajima's test "would reject a neutral model very frequently in the presence of population growth", and population subdivision can do the same. 4. Using D on real genomes

Because demography shifts D everywhere and selection only in places, the usual approach is to compute D in windows along the genome and treat the genome-wide distribution as the background. A single window far out in the tail is a candidate, not a finding. Nielsen (2005) puts the logic this way: if strong departures "are seen only on one or a few outlier loci, this may be interpreted as evidence for selection on these loci", but population subdivision "may increase the variance". Four practical rules:

  • The genome-wide mean is a statement about history, not about selection. A mean clearly away from 0 points to a change in population size or to population structure; the toy shows growth pulling it down and a recent bottleneck pushing it up.
  • Sample from one population. Pooling animals from separate populations changes the frequency spectrum.
  • Use sequencing data, not array SNPs. Array SNPs were chosen because they were common in a discovery panel, so rare variants are missing and D is pushed up (s04 shows this on a toy).
  • Count the sites you could have seen. π per base needs the number of bases that were well covered in all samples, including invariant ones; a VCF of variant sites alone does not hold it (tools, below).

Two warnings from the pixy documentation hold for every tool. D "is sound for comparing windows, populations, or groups computed the same way on the same data", but it "is not a p-value": "under neutrality its expectation is not 0 and its standard deviation is not 1". So compare D between windows or populations processed identically, and do not read one window's value as a test. Values from different tools, window sizes or sample sizes are not directly comparable.

Simple explanation with examples: what Tajima's D compares

Line up the same gene from eight animals. You can count diversity in two ways. One: count the positions where anyone differs (S). Two: take every pair of animals and count their differences, then average (π). A new mutation that only one animal carries adds a whole position to S, but makes only that animal different from the others, so it adds little to the average over pairs. A variant carried by half the animals adds the same one position to S, but separates many pairs, so it adds a lot to π.

8 animals, one variable site each: carried by 1 animal S +1 pairs differing 7 of 28 (adds 0.25 to π) carried by 4 animals S +1 pairs differing 16 of 28 (adds 0.57 to π)

Tajima's D asks whether S and π tell the same story. Many rare variants (lots of S, little π) give a negative D; variants at middle frequency (little S for its π) give a positive D. Both a change in population size and selection can make either happen, which is why D is a first look, not a verdict.
