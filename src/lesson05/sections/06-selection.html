06Selection from genealogies

A genealogy along the chromosome (s02) is also a record of selection. Where a favoured variant spread quickly, the local trees of that population are unusually shallow: its copies found a common ancestor recently. This section shows how to read that shape, how one published scan turns it into a number, how a per-mutation test works, and why a peak in such a scan is a place to look and not yet a finding.

What a sweep does to a local tree

Selective sweep
A favoured new variant spreads until the whole population carries it. Schweinsberg & Durrett (2005): "When a beneficial mutation occurs in a population, the new, favored allele may spread to the entire population. This process is known as a selective sweep."
Hitchhiking
Neutral sites near the favoured variant are carried along with it, because they sit on the same piece of chromosome. The term goes back to Maynard Smith & Haigh (1974), as cited in the paper above.
Escape
A lineage at a neutral site escapes when recombination during the sweep moved it off the chromosome that carried the favoured variant. It then traces back to a different ancestor.
Selection coefficient (s)
The fitness advantage of the favoured variant: with s = 0.02, its carriers leave about 2 % more offspring.

Go backwards in time at a neutral site close to the favoured variant. Every sampled lineage that did not escape runs back into the one chromosome where the mutation arose, so they all coalesce within the short time the sweep took. The local tree has many lineages joining at nearly one date, and that date is recent. Far from the variant, lineages escape and the tree is ordinary. Hejase et al. (2022) describe what they saw in inferred trees of human loci as "a burst of recent coalescence among the derived lineages", and in southern capuchino seedeaters (genus Sporophila) as "a burst of coalescence events, corresponding to unusually large and young clades".

How far the effect reaches can be estimated with a simple rule. Schweinsberg & Durrett treat each lineage as a coin: it is caught by the sweep with chance p, independently of the others.

p (caught by the sweep) = exp( − r × ln(2N) / s ) r = chance of recombination between the two sites, per generation

Stronger selection gives recombination less time to act, so the affected stretch is wider. With a recombination rate of 10−8 per base per generation and N = 5,000, half of the lineages are caught 150 kb from the variant at s = 0.02, and 750 kb away at s = 0.1 (our arithmetic). Treat it as a guide only: the authors themselves say that this first approximation "is not very accurate for realistic values of N". We use it because it is short enough to read.

Simple explanation with examples: one mutation, one shallow tree

Imagine a vole population and a new variant that protects against a parasite. After many generations nearly every vole carries it, and all those copies descend from the one vole in which it arose. A site right beside the variant came along with it, so at that site all voles share that one ancestor, a short time ago. A site far along the chromosome has had many chances to recombine onto other backgrounds, so its lineages go their separate ways into the deep past.

A tree cannot be seen directly: it is inferred from the data (s03). And "shallow" has to be judged against something, which is what the next part is about.

From tree shape to a number: Tpooled / Twithin

Deng, Nielsen & Song (2025), in the SINGER paper, ran this scan on 200 genomes of the 1000 Genomes Project: five African populations (GWD, YRI, ESN, LWK, MSL) with 40 genomes each. For every 1-kb window they took two averages from the inferred genealogies:

Twithin
The average pairwise coalescence time among genomes of one population. Two copies with a coalescence time T differ by about 2μT per base, so this is a diversity measure read from trees (lesson 4, s01).
Tpooled
The same average over all pairs in the combined sample of all five populations.
scan value of a window for one population = Tpooled / Twithin high value = this population is unusually shallow here

In their words, they "computed the ratio of the ARG-based diversity estimate for the combined sample to that for each of the five populations; reductions in local diversity would show up as peaks when these ratios are plotted along the genome". The ratio goes this way round, pooled over within, so a drop in one population appears as a peak. It sits above 1 even in ordinary windows, because the pooled sample contains pairs from different populations, which coalesce later. Their line for a peak is "the genome-wide 99.99% quantile" of the ratio (Figure 5 legend). A second panel of that figure shows Twithin of each population around each peak, and that panel matters, as the toy will show.

Why use trees for this, and not count differences in the sequence? Because a 1-kb window holds very few variants: the authors note that "SNP-based diversity can be noisy at fine scales", and report that SINGER gave more accurate fine-scale diversity than Relate and tsinfer + tsdate. Inference errors do not vanish. SINGER samples many genealogies, and even so, in their simulations its 90 % credible interval covered the true pairwise coalescence time in 85 % of cases (Relate 44 %, ARGweaver 54 %).

Among the peaks they highlight MITF (reduced diversity in GWD; related to skin), SPCS3 (reduced in YRI; an immune-related protein believed to affect flavivirus production) and SCN9A (a sodium channel involved in pain perception). Then comes the sentence to keep:

The authors' own caution. "These examples illustrate the utility of SINGER for exploratory evolutionary analysis, but additional studies are needed to investigate if, and how, selection is acting in these loci."

Run the scan on a toy chromosome

Three populations split 4,000 generations ago (each N = 5,000; ancestor N = 10,000), 12 sampled chromosomes each. A 2-Mb chromosome is cut into 400 windows of 5 kb, and each window gets one coalescent tree drawn on the page. The sweep sits in the middle, in population 1 only, and uses the coin rule above. Simplifications: the page knows the true trees (real ones are inferred); windows are drawn independently (real neighbours are correlated, so real peaks are smoother); the sweep is instantaneous. The threshold comes from 20,000 neutral windows standing for the rest of the genome. Click a track, or move the last slider, to see the tree of that window.

  • A quantile is not a test. The line cuts off a fixed share of windows, whatever happened in the genome. A human genome holds about 3 million 1-kb windows, so a 99.99 % line passes about 300 of them per population even if none was selected (our arithmetic). The published list is a list of the most extreme windows.
  • Age matters. The caught lineages meet at the date of the sweep, so the older the sweep, the deeper the tree. Once that date is not far from ordinary coalescence times, nothing stands out. In the toy a sweep 3,500 generations old is almost invisible at this line.
  • The top window is not the target. In the toy with s = 0.1 the highest window lay a median of about 90 kb from the true site, because the whole plateau is equally shallow. A peak gives a region, and the gene names in a figure are the genes that overlap that region.
  • A ratio can rise without any sweep. Where every population has a small local size, Twithin falls further than Tpooled, because the pooled pairs still have to wait for the population split. The ratio rises in all populations at once, which is why the per-population Twithin panel is needed. In the toy this effect is weaker than a sweep: with the local size cut by 85 % at the centre, no excess shows at the 99.99 % line, but at the 99 % line the central 300 kb has about five times as many windows above the line as chance predicts, in all three populations alike. This is our reading of the toy, not a claim of the paper. Something of this kind can happen in real genomes: background selection, the loss of neutral variation through "selection against the steady input of deleterious mutations" (Stephan 2019), is called "pervasive" in the SINGER paper, and Stephan writes that such processes "reduce levels of genetic diversity and are hard to distinguish".

Testing one mutation: the Relate selection test

The scan above asks about windows. Speidel et al. (2019) ask about a single mutation, using the tree at that site: did the lineage carrying the mutation spread faster than the lineages competing with it? Their test is "based on estimating the speed of spread of a particular lineage (carrying a particular mutation), relative to other 'competing' lineages, over some chosen time range". To test the whole lifetime of a mutation they "condition on the number of lineages present when it first arises, and use as a test statistic the number of present-day carriers". This gives a real p-value, not a quantile, with stated limits: the null distribution holds "assuming no population stratification" (no hidden substructure in the sample), and the authors report reduced power at rarer mutations. In 20 human populations of the 1000 Genomes Project they found 35 stringent signals (p < 5 × 10−8 in each of three or more groups), including the LCT region in Europeans, and they warn that "interpretation of our findings regarding natural selection requires some care".

The test ships with Relate as a module. This is the example from the manual (PATH_TO_RELATE is your installation; -m is the mutation rate):

PATH_TO_RELATE/scripts/DetectSelection/DetectSelection.sh \
  -i example \
  -o example_selection \
  -m 1.25e-8 \
  --first_bp 1000000 \
  --last_bp 1000000 \
  --years_per_gen 28 \
  --coal popsize.coal

The manual recommends estimating the population size history first, so that the branch lengths reflect it. The output .sele "records the log10 p-value for selection evidence", one row per mutation, and p-values "can only be calculated for mutations with derived allele frequency of at least 3" (derived copies in the sample). The column when_mutation_has_freq2 holds the value for the lifetime of the mutation. Going further, CLUES (Stern et al. 2019) estimates a selection coefficient and a frequency curve through time from local trees; the SINGER paper notes that it "performs better with a sample of local trees", which is one reason for sampling trees at all.

For a zoologist the hard part comes before all of this. SINGER "requires phased, contemporary genomes" and whole-genome data, and the authors add that "high-quality phasing is often challenging, especially for ancient DNA and non-model organisms" (see s03 and s04).

In our projects. A genealogy study of selection in an Asian human cohort was planned around candidate regions from a differentiation scan. The candidates did not survive a comparison with an independent call set, so nothing was left to test; s08 tells that story. A genealogy can only sharpen a question about a locus that is real in the first place.
