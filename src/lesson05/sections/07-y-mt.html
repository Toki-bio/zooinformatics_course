07Y chromosome and mitochondrial DNA as records

Two pieces of the genome are not shuffled between generations. The mitochondrial genome travels from mother to child, and the Y chromosome from father to son. Each therefore carries a single family tree of the whole population, and a view of the past that belongs to one sex only. Comparing the two, or either with the rest of the genome, is the classical way to ask whether females and males had different histories.

One block, one tree

mtDNA
The mitogenome is passed on through eggs. Every child of a mother carries her mtDNA, but only her daughters pass it on. It has no partner to swap pieces with (lesson 2, section 11 describes how to assemble it and why its nuclear copies, NUMTs, are a trap).
Y chromosome
The male-specific part of the Y is passed from father to son only. In mammals it does not recombine with the X over most of its length (lesson 2, section 12 explains how sex chromosomes look in an assembly). In birds and other species with ZW sex determination the mother-to-daughter W chromosome plays the Y's role; the Z and W are reversed relative to X and Y, but the idea is the same (Wilson Sayres 2018).
One tree each
Because nothing is shuffled, every position in the block shares one genealogy. Mutations that arrive on a branch stay together, which is what makes haplogroups possible: a haplogroup is a named branch of that one tree, defined by the mutations on it.
A single draw
One tree is one random outcome of the family-tree process. The autosomes hold thousands of independent trees. A Y or mtDNA result is therefore one noisy sample of history, however many bases were read. Wilson Sayres (2018) puts it the same way: each of these regions "adds only a single linked marker to the analysis".

Over many generations most lines end this way, and the survivors all descend from a few people. That loss is why the effective size of each marker is small, as the next part counts.

Simple explanation with examples: why a haplogroup is a branch, not a label

Take a grandmother whose mtDNA has one new mutation, call it M1. All her daughters and granddaughters through daughters carry M1, and so do her sons, but her sons' children do not. Years later a great-granddaughter picks up a second mutation, M2. Now the family has two nested groups:

grandmother carries M1 ├─ daughter A carries M1 │ └─ granddaughter carries M1 + M2 └─ daughter B carries M1

"Has M1" names the larger group, "has M1 and M2" a smaller group inside it. A haplogroup name is the shorthand for such a nested group. Deeper names mean more mutations were needed to place the person, so a call that rests on one last mutation is more fragile than the broad group above it.

Effective size: why each marker is small

The effective population size (Ne) says how fast diversity is lost by chance. Under the textbook assumptions, the autosomes, the X, the Y and mtDNA differ only in how many copies of each exist in the population. Wilson Sayres (2018) states the assumptions: constant population size, random mating, equal contributions of the two sexes (the effective number of males equals that of females) and a Poisson distribution of offspring number. Under them, X diversity is expected to be three-quarters of autosomal diversity and Y and mtDNA one-quarter, "because there are three X chromosomes for every four autosomes" and one Y or mtDNA copy for every four (Wilson Sayres et al., on human Y diversity).

Most real populations break the equal-sex assumption. A few males may father most offspring while many females breed. When the numbers of breeding males (Nm) and females (Nf) differ, the autosomal effective size is, as given in a university population-genetics lecture (Carr, Memorial University):

Ne = 4 Nm Nf / (Nm + Nf)

With equal numbers this gives Nm + Nf, the total. With one male and many females Ne approaches 4, however many females there are. Here Nm and Nf are the numbers that actually leave offspring, not the census counts; unequal success among breeders of the same sex lowers them further. The same lecture derives the formula assuming a Poisson-like spread of offspring within each sex; it does not attribute it to Wright (1931), whose paper we did not open.

Our derivation, which is not from a source: count gene copies. An autosome has two copies per individual, so 2Ne in the autosomal units above. mtDNA has one copy per breeding female (Nf), and the Y one copy per breeding male (Nm). Then diversity of Y or mtDNA relative to the autosomes is the ratio of these counts. For equal sexes it gives the cited one-quarter. We checked the algebra by a simulation (below).

What to take away. A skew in breeding numbers does not shrink both markers: it helps one and hurts the other. Few breeding males shrink the Y, while mtDNA relative to the autosomes rises above one-quarter. This is the logic behind using Y and mtDNA together: a Y-to-mtDNA diversity ratio far from one fits unequal numbers of breeding males and females, but selection on the Y can produce a similar pattern (below).

Reading a skyline: Bayesian skyline plots in BEAST2

With a tree for one marker, the standard tool for past size is the Bayesian skyline plot (Drummond et al. 2005), a method for estimating past population dynamics from a sample of sequences "without dependence on a prespecified parametric model of demographic history". It is run in BEAST2, which "samples from the posterior distribution of trees (or networks) and parameters" (Bouckaert et al. 2019). In the BEAST2 skyline tutorial the coalescent skyline "divides the time between the present and the root of the tree" into segments, each with its own effective size, and the x-axis is in years once the clock rate scales branch lengths from substitutions per site into calendar time.

  • Assumption of one mixed population. The tutorial states that the skylines "assume that the population is well-mixed" and "assume that there is no significant population structure", and that results "will be biased" otherwise. Karmin et al. (2015) warn of the same trap: structure combined with unbalanced sampling can lead the skyline plot to infer false declines under constant size.
  • Needs a rate. Without a mutation rate the answer is in substitutions, not years (below).
  • Sequences from all sites. It uses the observed differences between sequences; an unbiased sample of sites is needed (last section).
  • For methods that read the same question from the autosomes of one genome (PSMC and relatives), see lesson 4; they are not repeated here.

Simple explanation with examples: how often a lineage gets a new difference

Take the rates above and multiply by the length that was read. For mtDNA, 16,569 sites (the length Karmin et al. used in their simulations) × 2.67 × 10−8 = 4.4 × 10−4 new changes per lineage per year, about one every 2,260 years. For the Y, 8.8 Mb of filtered sequence (what Karmin et al. kept per man) × 0.74 × 10−9 = 6.5 × 10−3 per year, about one every 150 years.

mtDNA 16,569 sites × 2.67e-8 = 4.4e-4 per year → 1 per ~2,260 yr Y 8.8e6 bp × 0.74e-9 = 6.5e-3 per year → 1 per ~150 yr

The per-site mtDNA rate is about 36 times the per-base Y rate, but the Y sequence read is about 500 times longer, so the Y carries more dating information per lineage. This reproduces the opening sentence of Karmin et al. (2015): "Despite the higher per-base-mutation rate of mtDNA, the much greater length of the Y chromosome ... offers the highest genealogical resolution of all non-recombining loci in the human genome." Both numbers carry the uncertainty of their rates.

Sex-biased demography: reading Y against mtDNA

Karmin et al. (2015) sequenced 456 Y chromosomes (299 new) and made Bayesian skyline plots for Y and for mtDNA of a subset of 320 individuals. The two curves rose together 40 000 to 60 000 years ago, but differed: the mtDNA estimates were consistently more than twice those of the Y, and the Y plot "suggested a reduction at around 8–4 kya" when the female effective size was "up to 17-fold higher than the male". In their abstract: "In contrast to demographic reconstructions based on mtDNA, we infer a second strong bottleneck in Y-chromosome lineages dating to the last 10 ky."

Their reading: the Y bottleneck is not limited to a few haplotypes, so selection is "not a likely explanation", and they hypothesise that cultural change raised the variance in male reproductive success, since it coincides with the spread of Neolithic cultures. That is the paper's hypothesis, not a measurement. A different study that compared autosomes, X, Y and mtDNA in the same men (Wilson Sayres et al., read as the preprint) wrote that models of extreme differences in reproductive success between males and females "are insufficient as the sole explanation" for the low Y diversity, and that natural selection "appears to be acting to reduce diversity on the Y". The two papers ask different questions and do not contradict each other, but both show that Y alone cannot say which cause acted. Karmin et al. add that most methods using the recombining autosomes are underpowered to see changes within the past few thousand years, so the autosomes cannot yet confirm or refute it.

  • A Y-to-mtDNA contrast is a hypothesis generator. It cannot by itself say whether the cause was fewer breeding males, unequal migration of the sexes, selection on the Y or population structure.
  • The calculator above shows only the neutral breeding-numbers part.
  • For animals, the same logic holds with the roles set by the sex-determination system (Wilson Sayres 2018), and the genomes are those you assemble in lesson 2.

Why array markers cannot be dated

A genotyping array reads a fixed list of positions that were already known to vary. Albrechtsen et al. (2010) explain in their abstract that chip data "suffer from ascertainment biases caused by the SNP discovery process in which a small number of individuals from selected populations are used as discovery panels", that these biases "will distort measures of human diversity", and that correcting them is "difficult to generalize among studies". (We read the abstract only.) See section 4 on what arrays can and cannot do.

Our reasoning about dating, not a quotation: a molecular clock counts the new mutations on each branch. Probes were chosen from variants already seen in earlier samples, so mutations private to your own samples are mostly missing, and branch lengths carry little time information. Which branches are long or short depends on whose DNA picked the probes. The array can still tell you which named branch a person sits on (the tools above say how they handle arrays), but not when branches split. A skyline needs sequence of all sites in the region, which any whole-genome read set provides for free: its mitogenome and, for males, its Y.

Check before using. The 1/4 ratios, the formula and the calculator are neutral-model baselines. Real data are often far from them for reasons other than breeding sex ratio (selection on the Y, population structure, one noisy tree). Treat a result as a pointer to a hypothesis.
