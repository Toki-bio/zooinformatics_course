01What DNA methylation is and why zoologists care

The same genome sits in every cell of an animal, yet a liver cell and a neuron use different genes, a transposon copy stays quiet for millions of years, and only the mother's or only the father's copy of some genes is switched on. Part of that cell memory is written on the DNA itself, as small chemical marks on some bases. The commonest mark in vertebrates is a methyl group on cytosine. Long reads can see it directly, which is what this lesson is about. This first section explains what the mark is, where it sits, what it does, and what a zoologist can and cannot read from it.

A methyl group on a cytosine

5mC
5-methylcytosine: a cytosine (C) with a methyl group (CH3) added at carbon 5 of the ring. The base still pairs with G, so the DNA sequence is unchanged; the mark sits on top of it. That is why it is called epigenetic: information on the DNA that is not part of the sequence.
CpG
A C followed directly by a G on the same strand (the "p" is the phosphate between them). In vertebrates nearly all 5mC sits at CpG. CpG reads the same on both strands (5′-CG-3′ on one, 5′-CG-3′ on the other), so a methylated CpG carries a methyl on both strands.
DNMT
DNA methyltransferase, the enzyme that adds the methyl group. The canonical animal enzymes are DNMT1, DNMT3A and DNMT3B (Lyko 2018). Methylation is put down new (de novo) by DNMT3 and kept by the maintenance enzyme DNMT1 (Bewick et al. 2017). After replication the new strand of a methylated CpG is unmethylated at first, and maintenance methylation restores the mark, which is how the pattern survives cell division.
Methylation level
A tissue sample holds DNA from thousands of cells, and at one CpG some copies carry the mark and some do not. The level of a site is the share of copies that are methylated, from 0 to 100 %. A long read is one DNA molecule from one cell, so it shows the mark of that one copy (s02, s03).
5hmC
5-hydroxymethylcytosine: 5mC oxidised by the TET enzymes (a family of enzymes; TET1 was the first shown to do this) (Tahiliani et al. 2009). It is a step on the way to removing the mark and is abundant in some cells: Kriaucionis & Heintz (2009) measured it at 0.6 % of all nucleotides in cerebellar Purkinje neurons. Bisulfite sequencing, the classic short-read method, cannot tell 5hmC from 5mC (Huang et al. 2010); some long-read models report the two separately (s02).
6mA
N6-methyladenine: a methyl on adenine. Bacteria carry plenty of it. It was reported in several animals, starting with the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans (Greer et al. 2015), but a later test that traced where the signal came from found that bacteria explained most 6mA in insect and plant samples, and found no evidence of high 6mA in Drosophila, Arabidopsis or humans (Kong et al. 2022). Treat an animal 6mA claim as unproven until contamination is ruled out.
Simple explanation with examples: a mark that rides on the sequence

Think of the genome as a printed book and methylation as sticky notes on some letters. The notes do not change the text, but they tell the reader "skip this page". Every cell copies the book before it divides, and a copying enzyme also copies the sticky notes onto the new book, so a liver cell's daughters keep the liver cell's notes.

sequence (both strands) 5′ ...A T C G T T A C G G A... 3′ 3′ ...T A G C A A T G C C T... 5′ copy 1 (one cell) m m both CpGs methylated copy 2 (another cell) m . one methylated copy 3 . . none level at each CpG 67 % 33 % = methylated copies / all copies

A short read after bisulfite treatment and a long read both look at single copies; the level is what you get when you count many of them. A level of 50 % can mean that half the cells are methylated, or that in every cell one of the two parental copies is methylated and the other is not. Only reads that also tell the two copies apart can separate those two cases (s04).

Who methylates what: it depends on the lineage

DNA methylation is old, but how much of the genome carries it, and where, differs a lot between animal groups. Do not carry the vertebrate picture over to an insect or a nematode.

Methylation leaves a scar in the sequence: CpG depletion

A methylated C can lose its amino group by spontaneous deamination and become T. Over evolutionary time this turns CpG into TpG (or CpA on the other strand), so genomes that methylate their CpGs lose them. Bird (1980) proposed this as "the likely cause of the CpG deficiency in heavily methylated genomes". The measure is the ratio of observed to expected CpG, where "expected" is what the C and G content alone predicts:

CpG observed / expected = (number of CpG × N) / (number of C × number of G) N = length of the sequence

This is the definition used by Gardiner-Garden & Frommer (1987), as quoted in the UCSC Genome Browser's CpG-island track description; Zhou et al. (2020) use the same formula for whole genomes. Random sequence gives about 1. Human DNA gives about 0.25 (Zhou et al. 2020). Across 53 genomes, larger genomes held more transposable elements and had lower CpG observed/expected, in the transposons and in the rest of the DNA (Zhou et al. 2020). Bewick et al. (2017) used CpG observed/expected to predict whether an insect methylates its DNA, and the prediction matched the bisulfite data in all 18 species they could check.

The same scar dates repeats. Young copies of a CpG-rich transposon family have lost many CpGs since they were inserted, much faster than their other sites changed; very old families have lost almost all of them. Lesson 3 corrects repeat ages for exactly this effect (lesson 3, s04).

CpG islands

Against that depleted background stand short stretches where CpG is common: CpG islands. Gardiner-Garden & Frommer (1987) found them at the 5′ ends of all housekeeping genes and many tissue-specific genes in the vertebrate sequences they screened, and discussed the idea that they persist because they are kept free of methylation in the germline, so their CpGs do not decay. Saxonov et al. (2006) later found that CpGs in high-CpG promoters do mutate less often, as expected if they are unmethylated in the germline. Their criteria, as the UCSC track gives them: length over 200 bp, G+C content of 50 % or more, and CpG observed/expected above 0.6. Takai & Jones (2002) noted that this definition also catches many Alu copies and proposed a stricter one (over 500 bp, G+C 55 % or more, observed/expected 0.65 or more) that picks out gene starts better. In the human genome 72 % of promoters fall in a high-CpG class (Saxonov et al. 2006). Any threshold is arbitrary, so always say which definition you used.

Where methylation sits and what it does

Jones (2012) summarises the main point: methylation is often called a silencing mark, but "the function of DNA methylation seems to vary with context". In a vertebrate the main contexts are these.

  • Promoters with a CpG island are normally unmethylated: in mammals methylation covers the genome except at CpG islands near promoters (Bewick et al. 2017). Methylation of a promoter island goes with silencing during X inactivation, imprinting and the silencing of transposons (Takai & Jones 2002). When a vertebrate promoter is methylated, the gene is more often a lineage- or tissue-specific one (Keller et al. 2016).
  • Gene bodies (exons and introns) are methylated like the rest of a mammalian genome (Bewick et al. 2017). Jones (2012) treats them as a context of their own, where methylation does not simply mean silence. In invertebrates, methylation sits mostly in the bodies of a subset of genes and goes with their expression (Keller et al. 2016); gene-body methylation is the form shared by plants and animals (Zemach et al. 2010).
  • Transposable elements carry most of the 5mC in mammalian DNA. Their promoters are inactive when methylated, and over time C to T mutations at the methylated sites destroy many copies (Yoder, Walsh & Bestor 1997). The repeat classes are in lesson 3, s02.
  • Satellites. The classical satellite DNA next to human centromeres is normally heavily methylated. In ICF syndrome, caused by mutations in DNMT3B, satellites 2 and 3 are almost unmethylated and the heterochromatin beside the centromeres of chromosomes 1, 9 and 16 becomes unstable (Xu et al. 1999). Satellites are introduced in lesson 3, s02.

One parent's copy: imprinting and X inactivation

Two cases use methylation to treat the two parental copies differently, and both matter for s04.

  • Genomic imprinting. For some genes only the mother's or only the father's copy is expressed. It is known in therian mammals (marsupials and placentals) and not in monotremes; marsupials have fewer imprinted loci (Renfree, Suzuki & Kaneko-Ishino 2013). In placentals the switch is usually a differentially methylated region whose methylation is set in the egg or sperm. In marsupials some imprinted genes have such regions (H19, IGF2, PEG10) and others appear to rely on histone marks instead (Renfree et al. 2008).
  • X inactivation. In female placental mammals one X is silenced, and the CpG islands of the silenced X are methylated, which is considered to stabilise the silence. Marsupials inactivate the father's X in all tissues. In the common wallaroo the CpG island of the X-linked G6PD gene was unmethylated in females and males, although the father's copy was completely silent, which suggests that island methylation is not needed to keep an X silent there (Loebel & Johnston 1996). Sex chromosomes in an assembly are covered in lesson 2, s12.

Why a zoologist cares

  • Age of wild animals. Methylation at some CpGs changes steadily with age, and models built on such sites, epigenetic clocks, estimate age from a skin biopsy, blood or even faeces. Reviews for ecologists: De Paoli-Iseppi et al. (2017) and Parrott & Bertucci (2019). Examples: 712 known-age bats of 26 species (Wilkinson et al. 2021); a pinniped clock from 171 blood and skin samples with a median error of 1.7 years (Robeck et al. 2023); "universal" mammal clocks from 11,754 methylation arrays of 59 tissue types in 185 species (Lu et al. 2023). A clock must be calibrated on animals of known age, and calibration animals can mislead: a clock built on laboratory house mice gave wild mice consistently higher epigenetic ages for a given body mass (Hanski et al. 2024). The pinniped and universal clocks were built with a methylation array that reads a fixed panel of conserved CpGs (37,492 in Robeck et al. 2023), not with long reads.
  • The history of repeats. CpG decay tells young repeat families from old ones, and the strength of the decay tells you whether the lineage methylated its repeats in the germline (box below; s05).
  • Gene regulation and parental conflict. Promoter methylation, imprinting and X inactivation can be read allele by allele from long reads of one animal (s04).
  • Comparisons between tissues, individuals and environments (s06), with the limits below.

How much of it is inherited?

Methylation is copied through cell divisions within one animal. Between generations of mammals most of it is erased twice: in the germ cells and again in the early embryo. Heard & Martienssen (2014) conclude that "these two rounds of epigenetic erasure leave little chance for inheritance of epigenetic marks". Imprints survive the embryo round but are reset in the germline, and some repeat copies escape erasure. Transgenerational inheritance is well documented in plants and in nematodes; in mammals proof of a purely epigenetic basis is "generally lacking", and many studies show only inter-generational effects, such as a mother's diet acting on the fetus she carries. Horsthemke (2018) adds that genetic, ecological and cultural inheritance confound such studies. For a zoologist: a methylation difference between two populations is a description of those animals in their environment and tissue, not by itself evidence of heritable adaptation. Section s06 deals with how to compare samples.
