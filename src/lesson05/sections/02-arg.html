02What an ancestral recombination graph is

With many genomes we can ask more than how fast two lineages meet. We can ask who is related to whom, at every position of a chromosome, and when their ancestors lived. The answer is not one family tree but a long series of trees that change along the sequence. Joined together, these trees form an ancestral recombination graph (ARG). This section explains what that object is. The next one, ARG tools, shows how programs try to estimate it from real data.

One position, one tree

A nucleotide is inherited from exactly one parent, so each position in a sample of chromosomes has a single line of descent back through time. Follow these lines from several sampled copies and they join at common ancestors. The result is a tree, as in lesson 4, but with more than two tips. It is called the local tree of that position. The tips here are haplotypes, single copies of a chromosome, so three diploid animals give six tips.

Neighbouring positions usually travelled together through the same ancestors, so they share a tree. But at a crossover, an offspring chromosome took its left part from one parental chromosome and its right part from the other. The two sides of that point, the breakpoint, have different ancestors from there on back.

Recombination cuts one branch and moves it

The tree on the right of a breakpoint is not a new, unrelated tree. It equals the tree on the left except for one branch, which is cut off and re-attached elsewhere. Computer scientists call this a subtree prune and regraft. Three things can happen:

  • The shape changes. The branch joins a different part of the tree, so the groups of closest relatives change.
  • Only an age changes. The branch joins the same branch again, higher or lower. Shape stays, age of one junction moves.
  • Nothing changes. The lineage comes back to the same ancestor. The recombination really happened, but no tree shows it. The same is true of a breakpoint in a stretch of an ancestor that passed nothing on to the sample.

What to notice. (1) Step through the breakpoints: each time, one branch moves and the rest of the tree stays. (2) The closest relative of a haplotype changes along the sequence, so "h2 is closest to h5" is true only of a stretch. (3) Root age jumps up and down although the history of the population is the same everywhere. (4) Raise the recombination rate: more trees, each covering less sequence. (5) Choose the small population: trees get shallow and there are fewer of them, because the lineages meet before many recombinations can happen.

Simple explanation with examples: why one family tree is not enough

Think of your own genome. Your pedigree has one shape, but your left half of chromosome 1 may come from your grandmother and the right half from your grandfather. Ask "who is my ancestor?" and the honest answer is "for which position?". Two cousins may share a grandmother on one stretch and share nothing recent on the next stretch.

The same is true in a population of voles. Take six chromosomes and write down their closest relatives at three places:

position 12 kb 47 kb 83 kb h1 closest h2 h2 h5 h3 closest h4 h6 h6

Each column is one local tree, seen from two tips (these rows are an illustration, not output of the simulation). Reading a table like this as "one tree for the species" would throw away most of what the genome records. Every local tree is a separate, though not independent, sample of the population's history. That is why many positions together say more than one gene tree.

Markers that do not recombine, such as mitochondrial DNA, are the exception: they have a single tree (s07).

From many trees to one graph

All these local trees can be laid on top of each other. Shared branches fall on the same line, and at each recombination one line splits in two going back in time, because one chromosome had two parents. This network is the ARG: an ancestral recombination graph encodes all local trees along the genome for a given sample. Going backwards in time, lineages merge at coalescence events and split at recombination events. Hudson described this process and Griffiths and Marjoram later gave the graph its name.

Tree sequences: the same object as two tables

Writing every local tree separately would repeat almost everything, because neighbouring trees are nearly identical. A tree sequence (or succinct tree sequence) avoids this. A node table lists each ancestor once with its age. An edge table lists each parent-child branch once, with the stretch of sequence over which that branch exists. To get the tree at a position, take the rows that cover it. The format also has tables for sites and mutations, so the variants of a data set can be kept on the trees.

The tools for this format are msprime, which simulates, and tskit, which stores and analyses. This is the same kind of toy in a few lines of Python:

import msprime
ts = msprime.sim_ancestry(samples=3, population_size=1000, sequence_length=100_000,
                          recombination_rate=1e-8, random_seed=5)
print(ts.num_trees, ts.num_nodes, ts.num_edges)   # one line of counts
for tree in ts.trees():                           # walk along the sequence
    print(tree.interval.left, tree.interval.right, tree.tmrca(0, 1))

Is a tree sequence an ARG? Words differ between authors. A full ARG holds the events themselves: every recombination and when it happened. A basic tree sequence holds their outcome, the local trees and which parts they share, and it does not say when a recombination happened. It can be extended to hold those events too. Relate gives a series of trees without shared nodes, and whether such a series is truly an ARG is, as one review puts it, a matter of definition.

One object, many questions

Once the local trees are there, questions that used to need separate methods become questions asked of one object:

Population size through time
The rate of coalescence at a time is inversely proportional to Ne at that time. So the density of coalescences in the ARG, slice by slice through time, gives Ne(t). A pair of haplotypes along the genome is the view PSMC has (lesson 4); an ARG holds this for every pair at once.
Split times
Compare coalescence within groups and between groups. Two populations separated at some time, with no gene flow since, cannot have a between-group ancestor younger than that time. Relate estimates split times this way, with cross-coalescence rates.
Selection
Lineages carrying a favoured allele coalesce unusually quickly, so the tree around that allele has a dense burst of young coalescences. Balancing selection works the other way and leaves unusually old ancestors. s06 covers these tests.

What to notice. (1) In "Two populations", no purple bar lies to the left of the dashed line: the youngest between-group age is always older than the true split, usually by a few hundred generations. In the other histories the two colours overlap, because the groups mean nothing. (2) Press New history several times: the estimate of N swings widely, because 100 kb holds few independent trees. A genome has far more. (3) The pale red band in the first figure marks the lowest stretch of mean pair age. There is one in every history, including those with no selection at all. A dip alone is not a sweep: a scan must ask how unusual it is against the rest of the genome.

Nobody observes an ARG. Everything above used a known history. Real data give only the variants, and they say little about any one tree. In humans the mutation and recombination rates are similar, so on average each mutation that could inform a tree comes with a recombination that changes it. Of the recombination events that change the graph, only a small proportion can be detected from data, and even then their timing and breakpoints are approximate. A count of recombinations made from sequences is often far below the true number. An ARG in a file is therefore an estimate. Treating one inferred ARG as the truth gives false certainty, and it can be biased if the true history differs from what the method assumed. s03 compares the tools with this in mind.
