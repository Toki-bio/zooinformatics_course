01From one genome to many

In lesson 4 one diploid genome gave one curve of population size through time. With many genomes you can ask more: who is related to whom, in which stretch of the chromosome, how recently, and whether a gene variant spread faster than chance allows. There are four families of methods for that. This section is a map: what each family reads, what data it needs, and a helper that lists which named tools can take the data you have. The details of each family come in the later sections.

New words for this section

Genealogy (tree)
The family tree of a set of chromosome copies at one place in the genome. Tracing two copies backwards, they meet in a common ancestor; the time of that meeting is the coalescence time (lesson 4, s01).
Recombination
The shuffling of the two parental copies when eggs and sperm are made. After a recombination the next stretch of the chromosome can have a slightly different tree, so the genealogy changes along the genome.
Ancestral recombination graph (ARG)
The trees of all stretches of the genome joined into one object. Section 02 explains it properly.
Phased data
Data in which every heterozygous site is assigned to one of the two copies of the chromosome (lesson 4, s05). Unphased data only say "this animal carries A and G here".
Linkage disequilibrium (LD)
The non-random association of alleles at two nearby sites on the same chromosome. It fades with distance, because recombination breaks the association.
Identity by descent (IBD)
A stretch of chromosome that two animals both inherited, unbroken by recombination, from one common ancestor, so the two copies are identical apart from new mutations (section 05).
Ancestral allele, polarisation
At a variable site, which of the two alleles is the old one and which is the new mutation. It is usually decided by comparing with a related species (an outgroup). Some tools need it, others do not.
SNP array
A chip that reads one fixed list of variable sites, not the whole genome. A whole-genome sequence reads all sites. What that difference does to the result is section 04.
Simple explanation with examples: why a second genome changes the question

One animal carries two copies of each chromosome, so there is one pair of copies to compare and one "time of common ancestor" per stretch of genome. Four animals carry eight copies, so there are 28 pairs, and the tree for each stretch can be drawn. Three new kinds of question become possible.

pairs 2 copies: 1 8 copies: 28 100 copies: 4,950 (n copies give n(n-1)/2 pairs) tree at each stretch of the genome, how all the copies connect shared a long stretch identical in two animals = a recent common ancestor decay how fast association between two nearby sites fades = a record of population size

Each family below reads one of these. They use the same genomes but look at different features, so they answer different questions and break in different ways. When two families agree, that is more convincing than when two tools of one family agree.

toolMethods that accept your data, with the reason
MethodFamilyFor your dataReason (source)

usable = the documentation or paper says this input is accepted. after fixing = usable once you change the input (phase it, subsample). not for this data = the source says the input is not supported, or (marked "our inference") the documentation describes only another input. "Sequences" in the reported scales are counted as copies of the chromosome, so animals x 2; that reading of the review's wording is ours.

What the helper cannot tell you. It only checks the input format and the reported size range. It does not know how many sites your data have, how good the phasing is, how diverged your animals are, or whether the animals are one random-mating population. GONE's own README warns that recent migrants from another population bias its estimate and produce an artefactual very recent drop; assumptions of this kind sit behind the other families too and return in section 08.
