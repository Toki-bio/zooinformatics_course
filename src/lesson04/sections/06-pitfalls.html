06Pitfalls and checks

A PSMC curve is the last link of a long chain: reads, variant calls, a callable mask, bins, a model with a time pattern, and finally a mutation rate and a generation time. Every link can bend the curve, and most of them bend it without changing the bootstrap band. This section lists what bends single-genome curves, shows the main effects on a toy genome you can spoil yourself, and gives the checks that tell a real feature from an artefact, with what they showed for our two rodent genomes.

Words used in this section

False negative (missed het)
A true heterozygous site that the pipeline calls homozygous, usually because too few reads carried the second allele, or because the site was filtered out. The share of true hets missed is the false-negative rate (FNR).
False positive (false het)
A site called heterozygous where the two genome copies are in fact the same: a sequencing error shared by many reads, a misaligned read from a similar copy elsewhere (a paralog), or an assembly error.
Artefact
A bump, dip or slope in the curve that comes from the data or the method, not from the history of the animals.
Sensitivity test
Running the same analysis again with one thing changed (fewer reads, another caller, a mask, another time pattern) to see which parts of the curve move. A feature that survives the reasonable alternatives is called robust.
Simple explanation with examples: why each error moves the curve in its own direction

PSMC reads the genome as a mosaic of pieces and dates each piece by how many heterozygous sites it carries (s01, s03): few hets = the two copies met their common ancestor recently; many hets = long ago. The curve is then built from how many pieces fall into each age class. So any error that changes the het count of a piece changes its apparent age.

true piece, 100 kb |....|.......|.....|...... 4 hets → recent ancestor missed hets (30 %) |............|............ 2 hets → looks even younger false hets added |.|..|..|....|.|...|.|... 8 hets → looks older collapsed duplicate |||.|||||..|||.|||. very many → looks very old run of homozygosity ........................ 0 hets → "ancestor yesterday"

Missing hets everywhere makes every piece look younger, so the whole curve slides toward the present and down; the PSMC README says this "has the same effect as smaller mutation rate". False hets sprinkled evenly matter most for the pieces that had few hets to begin with: the recent coalescences vanish from the record, and the recent population looks larger than it was. A collapsed duplication adds pieces that look extremely old. A run of homozygosity adds pieces whose ancestor lived a few generations ago.

A different mutation rate or generation time does not bend the curve; it slides the whole curve along both axes by one factor (s04). Everything in the table above changes its shape.

Try it

Spoil a toy genome and watch the curve bend

A toy genome of 2 Gb in 20,000 blocks of 100 kb. Each block gets one common-ancestor age (TMRCA) drawn from the true history (dashed line), and true hets in proportion to that age, at μ = 5.4 × 10-9 per base per generation. The estimate (solid blue line) dates each block from its observed het count, then turns the share of blocks in each age class into a coalescence rate and a size, as PSMC does in principle. It is not PSMC: real blocks are much shorter than 100 kb and PSMC finds them with an HMM; the grey line is what the toy estimator recovers from clean data. Spoil the data with the sliders.

What to notice. (1) False hets at only 0.05 per kb (5 per block, under 3 % of all hets) already lift the most recent part of the curve: the young blocks are the ones with few hets, and they no longer look young. (2) Missing 30 % of the hets moves the whole curve down and to the left, the same as a lower mutation rate; the shape survives. (3) ROH add blocks with no hets; in this estimator they only fill the youngest age class, and masking them changes the rest very little. Now add false hets as well: the "empty" ROH blocks get a few hets each, look a few thousand generations old, and a false recent decline appears; masking the called ROH removes that decline. Raise the false hets to 0.2 per kb or more and the ROH caller (under 0.2 het per kb over 300 kb) starts to miss the runs. (4) Collapsed duplications (here 6 false hets per kb in each duplicated block) lift the older part of the curve. (5) "New genome" redraws the history's random outcome: the recent end changes from genome to genome, the old part hardly at all.

The checks

  1. Subsample the coverage
    Keep a fraction of the reads, redo calling, mask and PSMC exactly as before, and compare. Two disjoint halves of the reads also give two independent call sets. The PSMC README suggests downsampling a high-coverage genome as the way to measure the false-negative rate: the drop in hets per callable kb at lower depth estimates it.
    samtools view -s · psmc_plot.pl -N
  2. Compare two callers or two call sets
    Clair3 and DeepVariant, or PASS-only and an allele-fraction filter (s02). Run PSMC on both. Where the curves differ, the result depends on the calls.
    bcftools isec
  3. Mask ROH, and a random region of the same size
    Remove the called ROH from the callable mask and rebuild the input. Masking anything removes data, so mask a random set of regions of the same total size as a control; only the difference between the two masks is the effect of ROH.
    bedtools subtract · bedtools shuffle
  4. Split the first time window
    Two extra runs with the first window split in two and in four. Hilgers et al. (2025): "if a peak is only observed with the default parameter setting, it is likely an artifact."
    psmc -p
  5. Enough bootstraps
    Bootstraps for every curve you show: we use at least 20 rounds; the README example uses 100.
    splitfa · psmc -b
  6. Compare individuals and species
    Two animals of one population should give the same curve within the bootstrap band; sister species should meet in their shared ancestral past (Nadachowska-Brzyska et al. 2016 saw four flycatcher species converge 1–2 million years ago). A curve that disagrees only for one genome points to that genome's data.
    same settings for all genomes
  7. Simulate the inferred history and infer it again
    Turn the curve into a simulation, generate genomes under it, and run PSMC on them with the same settings. If PSMC does not get back the history you put in, the feature you are interpreting is beyond what the method can resolve. Simulating an alternative (for example a structured population of constant size) shows whether it gives the same curve.
    psmc2history.pl · history2ms.pl · msprime

For the over-fitting rule, the RS lines of the last round hold, in their fifth column, the number of recombinations inferred in each interval (the code prints n_recomb × the interval's share; our reading of aux.c). Add them up over the intervals of each parameter: the README asks for about 10 or more.

Simulating under the inferred history

The PSMC package has its own route: psmc2history.pl extracts the fitted history, and history2ms.pl writes a command line for a coalescent simulator (the script writes it for msHOT-lite, a variant of Hudson's ms with recombination hotspots, or with -M for MaCS); ms2psmcfa.pl turns the simulated output into a .psmcfa. We did not use this route, so it is shown as the README describes it. msprime does the same in Python, and you control every step.

# PSMC package route (msHOT-lite must be installed). -n 25 = last round of a -N25 run; -L 30 Mb per chromosome, 100 chromosomes
utils/psmc2history.pl -n 25 x.psmc | utils/history2ms.pl -L 30000000 -r 100 > ms-cmd.sh
sh ms-cmd.sh | utils/ms2psmcfa.pl - > sim.psmcfa
psmc -N25 -t15 -r5 -p "4+25*2+4+6" -o sim.psmc sim.psmcfa
# msprime 1.4: one diploid under a step history (sizes and times in generations; numbers are an illustration)
import msprime
mu = 5.4e-9            # per base per generation, as used for scaling
r  = mu * 0.2          # recombination rate = mu x rho0/theta0 from the TR line of your .psmc
d = msprime.Demography()
d.add_population(name="A", initial_size=30_000)                    # size today
for t, n in [(8_000, 130_000), (25_000, 70_000), (80_000, 170_000)]:
    d.add_population_parameters_change(time=t, initial_size=n, population="A")
with open("sim.psmcfa", "w") as out:
    for i in range(1, 51):                                         # 50 chromosomes of 30 Mb
        ts = msprime.sim_ancestry(samples=1, demography=d, sequence_length=30_000_000,
                                  recombination_rate=r, random_seed=i)   # samples=1 = one diploid
        ts = msprime.sim_mutations(ts, rate=mu, random_seed=i)
        bins = ["T"] * (30_000_000 // 100)                        # 100-bp bins, T = no het
        for v in ts.variants():
            if v.genotypes[0] != v.genotypes[1]:
                bins[int(v.site.position) // 100] = "K"            # K = at least one het
        s = "".join(bins)
        out.write(f">sim{i}\n" + "\n".join(s[j:j+60] for j in range(0, len(s), 60)) + "\n")

Simulated genomes are perfect: no errors, no missed hets, no mask. Add the error rates you measured (false hets from the X control, the FNR from subsampling) to the simulated data if you want to know what they do to your curve. The 0.2 for ρ0/θ0 above is a placeholder; take yours from the TR line (s04).

Simple explanation with examples: what "simulate and re-infer" proves

Suppose your curve shows a sharp peak 30,000 years ago and a crash after it. Simulate genomes under a history without the peak, only with the crash, and run PSMC on them. If the peak comes back, PSMC makes it out of the crash, and your peak is not evidence of anything (this is how Hilgers et al. 2025 showed the default-pattern peaks are artefacts). If the peak does not come back, the data really demand it, under the model. Then try a different story that could produce the same data, such as a population split into connected groups at constant size: if that also reproduces your curve, one genome cannot tell the two stories apart.

In our projects

Species A (our ONT genome, one male) and the relative (public HiFi assembly, somewhat lower depth), both scaled with the placeholder μ = 5.4 × 10-9 per generation and one year per generation.

Reads and calls. Two disjoint halves of the species A reads (each with half the depth, on a test subset of the genome) gave nearly identical curves for the old part of the history; the full call set agreed with them only further back. In a figure of six call sets on the same subset (the full set and both halves, each PASS-only and allele-fraction filtered), all six coincided over the old part and disagreed in the youngest intervals. Over the whole genome, PASS-only calls and the allele-fraction 0.3–0.7 set agreed in the old part; for the relative, PASS-only gave about twice the Ne in the middle part: the recent and middle parts depend on the filter. False hets: on the male's X, about 1 % of the autosomal het rate remained after the allele-fraction filter, and Ts/Tv of the PASS hets was clearly below the 2.0–2.2 expected. The densest 100-bp bins (21 or more hets) were tandem-repeat alleles called as strings of SNVs (well under 1 % of the hets in both genomes).

Patterns. Splitting the first window left the species A curve unchanged except in the youngest intervals, including its recent low point and the bump before it; only the youngest interval ran away to values in the millions, i.e. it is not constrained. In the relative the default and split patterns agreed in shape beyond the youngest intervals (the peak height differed by about 15 %) and disagreed in the youngest ones. We do not interpret the most recent part of either curve.

ROH masks. Masking runs of homozygosity of at least 100 kb (or 500 kb) hardly moved PSMC: species A kept its recent low point and its bump, and the relative's peak changed by a few percent at most. MSMC2 behaved similarly. SMC++ did not: its most recent species A value rose about five-fold with ROH masked, and the relative's peak fell by about a third. An earlier quick test with a differently built mask had seemed to erase the species A bump; the rebuilt masks did not reproduce that, and the reason was not recorded.

Repeat masks and the random-mask control. Masking tandem repeats, simple repeats, very deep regions or dense het bins changed the relative's curve by 5 % or less. Masking all interspersed repeats (roughly half of the genome) cut its peak by about half, but a random mask of the same size alone lowered it by about a quarter: about half of the drop was simply less data.

Simulated nulls. The same idea, simulating a history without the feature, showed that cobraa's preference for a structured model on both genomes is matched by simulations without any structure (s05). Bootstraps: 20 rounds per curve.
