04From model units to years: mutation rate and generation time

PSMC never sees a year or an animal. It reads where the heterozygous sites lie along one genome and reports times and population sizes in its own units, measured against an unknown reference size. To turn them into years and numbers of individuals you supply two numbers from outside the genome: the mutation rate and the generation time. This section shows the arithmetic, where those two numbers come from, what happens to the curve when they change, and how to report the uncertainty they bring.

What PSMC reports

The .psmc output file (s03) holds, for every iteration, a TR line and a block of RS lines. The PSMC README says: "All these parameters are scaled to 2N0". The three numbers you need are:

θ0 (theta zero)
The second column of the TR line: the scaled mutation rate per bin. In population-genetics units θ = 4Nμ: four times the population size times the mutation rate. It is, roughly, how often two copies of the genome differ per base, which is why it carries the heterozygosity of the animal.
tk
Third column of each RS line: the start of time interval k, in units of 2N0 generations (0.5 means "N0 generations ago").
λk (lambda)
Fourth column of each RS line: the population size in interval k relative to N0 (λ = 2 means twice N0).
N0
A reference effective population size that is never known by itself. Every time and every size in the output is a multiple of it.

Li and Durbin (2011) and the PSMC README convert with a per-site, per-generation mutation rate μ and the bin size s (100 by default):

N0 = θ0 / (4 μ s)
generations ago Tk = 2 N0 tk years ago = Tk × g
Ne in interval k = λk N0

Here g is the generation time in years: the average age of the parents when their offspring are born. Notice what the formulas imply. θ0, tk and λk come from the genome and are fixed. μ sits in the denominator of N0, and N0 multiplies both times and sizes; g multiplies only the times.

Simple explanation with examples: from θ0 to a number of mice

Take a PSMC run like the one in s03, with a round example value of θ0 = 0.08 per 100-base bin. Assume μ = 5.4 × 10-9 per site per generation (an illustrative value, see below) and g = 1 year.

N0 = 0.08 / (4 × 5.4e-9 × 100) = 0.08 / 2.16e-6 = 37,037 an interval starting at t = 0.5: T = 2 × 37,037 × 0.5 = 37,037 generations = 37,037 years at g = 1 the same interval with λ = 1.5: Ne = 1.5 × 37,037 = 55,600 double μ to 1.08e-8: N0 = 18,519 → T = 18,519 years, Ne = 27,800 (both halved) double g to 2 years: N0 = 37,037 → T = 74,074 years, Ne = 55,600 (only time doubles)

The interval and the λ did not change. Only the ruler did. The genome itself fixes products such as 2N0μt; you can trade a larger population for a smaller mutation rate and the data look identical.

The README also gives a way to plot without μ at all: time as the pairwise divergence dk = 2μTk = tkθ0/s, and size as θk = 4Nkμ = λkθ0/s. Such a plot never has to be redrawn when somebody publishes a better mutation rate.

What to notice. (1) Change μ alone: the whole curve slides along the diagonal, older-and-larger for a smaller μ, younger-and-smaller for a larger one, and the shape is untouched. (2) Change g with a per-generation rate: the curve slides only sideways; population sizes stay. (3) Switch to "per year" and change g: now the times stay and only the sizes move, because a per-year rate already contains the generation time. (4) The 7.86 × 10⁻⁹ preset is 1.46 times the placeholder: every time and every size shrinks by the same 1.46. (5) The last preset is what psmc_plot.pl uses when you forget -u and -g: its defaults are the human values of Li and Durbin (2011). (6) With the grid on, the faint curves show the spread from plausible-but-unknown constants; compare it with the width of the bootstrap band in s03.

Where the mutation rate comes from

There are two kinds of estimates, and they answer slightly different questions.

  • Direct (pedigree) rates. Sequence parents and offspring (a trio) or lines kept for many generations (a mutation-accumulation experiment), and count new mutations that neither parent carried. The result is per site per generation: exactly what PSMC needs. It is a modern, short-term rate, measured in a few individuals, often of a laboratory strain.
  • Phylogenetic (long-term) rates. Divide the sequence divergence between two species by twice their split time, which itself comes from fossils or a dated tree (the factor 2 because both lineages accumulate changes: d = 2μT, as in the PSMC README). The result is per site per year, averaged over millions of years, and only as good as the calibration date.

The two can disagree. When the first trio studies gave a human rate of about 1.2 × 10-8 per generation (Kong et al. 2012), it was "approximately half" of the older value from fossil calibration (Scally and Durbin 2012). Halving μ doubles every date scaled with it, so the timing of human prehistory inferred from genomes had to be revised. Li and Durbin (2011) had used 2.5 × 10-8 per generation and 25 years; they noted the "uncertainty of the per-year mutation rate 1.0 × 10-9 (= 2.5 × 10-8/25)" as an important caveat.

Even for the laboratory mouse, three careful studies differ by a factor of two. For a wild species without its own estimate you borrow a rate from a relative, and the 40-fold spread across vertebrates is a reminder that the borrowed number is a guess. Bergeron et al. (2023) also found that generation time, age at maturity and fecundity are the main life-history traits behind that spread, so a rate measured in a fast-breeding species may not transfer to a slow one.

One more thing acts like a lower mutation rate: heterozygous sites you failed to call. Li and Durbin (2011) state that "missing heterozygotes uniformly at a probability p is equivalent to reducing the neutral mutation rate". Low coverage or strict filters (s02) therefore push the curve the same way a smaller μ would. psmc_plot.pl has -N (one false-negative rate) and -M "sample=0.1" (per sample) to correct for it, and the README calls an uncorrected low coverage "the most common pitfall when using PSMC".

Generation time

For a wild mammal the generation time is rarely measured. It is set from life-history data (age at first breeding, lifespan, how many years an animal breeds) and is longer than the age at first breeding whenever animals breed for more than one season. Two rules follow from the formulas:

  • With a per-generation μ, g moves only the time axis: years = generations × g. Population sizes do not depend on it.
  • With a per-year rate, convert first: μgen = μyr × g. Then the years no longer depend on g (it cancels), but the population sizes do: a longer generation time gives a smaller Ne.
per generation: years = 2 N0 t g = θ0 t g / (2 μ_gen s) Ne = λ θ0 / (4 μ_gen s)
per year: years = θ0 t / (2 μ_yr s) Ne = λ θ0 / (4 μ_yr g s)

Reporting the uncertainty

The bootstrap band from s03 shows how much the curve changes when you resample pieces of the genome. It says nothing about μ and g, which multiply every value at once. For a species without its own rate this second uncertainty is usually the larger one. Report it separately:

  1. State μ, its unit (per generation or per year), and where it comes from; state g and its basis. Say plainly if either is borrowed or a placeholder.
  2. Give the rescaling rule so readers can convert: "for another μ multiply all times and sizes by 5.4 × 10-9/μ; for another g multiply times by g".
  3. Show a small grid of scalings (as in the figure above), or a second axis in model units (divergence d and θ), next to the main figure.
  4. Make claims that do not depend on the constants: the order of events, ratios ("the trough is about 2.5 times below the old plateau"), and the comparison of two genomes that share the same μ and g. Absolute dates matched to a climate event need the constants and inherit their uncertainty.
  5. Never overlay curves from different papers without re-scaling them to the same μ and g.

The plotting script takes μ, g and the bin size as options. Its defaults are the human values (-u 2.5e-8, -g 25, -s 100), so always set them. It needs gnuplot; -R keeps the text tables (prefix.0.txt for the main run, one per bootstrap) whose first two columns are years and Ne in units of 104, which you can plot with any other program.

# one scaling: our placeholder mu (per site per generation) and g = 1 year
psmc_plot.pl -u 5.4e-09 -g 1 -s 100 -R sp_u5.4_g1 combined.psmc
# a grid of scalings for the same run (2 rates x 2 generation times)
for u in 4.6e-09 7.9e-09; do
  for g in 1 2; do
    psmc_plot.pl -u $u -g $g -s 100 -R sp_u${u}_g${g} combined.psmc
  done
done
# → sp_*.eps (add -p for PDF) and, with -R, sp_*.0.txt: years, Ne/10^4, ...

The other single-genome programs of s05 also work in mutation-scaled units: MSMC2 reports times and rates scaled by μ, and SMC++ takes μ as an argument of smc++ estimate and g as -g of smc++ plot. The same rules apply. Split times between two species (s07) are scaled the same way.

In our projects

All our species A curves were scaled with μ = 5.4 × 10-9 per site per generation and g = 1 year, and every figure says these are illustrative placeholders, not values verified for this species. The μ is the same number as the C57BL/6 laboratory-mouse estimate of Uchimura et al. (2015); no rate has been measured for species A or its close relatives, and we have no measured generation time. Re-scaling to the long-read mouse rate of 7.86 × 10-9 divides every time and size by 1.46. Our recommended display is a grid of μ 4.6 to 7.9 × 10-9 per generation and g 1 to 2 years.

The constants matter most for dates. Suppose a split time scaled with the placeholder constants comes out at 1 million years, while an independent dated phylogeny puts the same split at 2 million years. Both can be true only if the yearly rate is half the placeholder, 2.7 × 10-9: for example a per-generation rate of 5.4 × 10-9 with a generation time of 2 years. Whether such a generation time is realistic has to come from the species' life history; the arithmetic alone cannot decide it. Our own two-species estimates (s07) carry exactly this uncertainty.
