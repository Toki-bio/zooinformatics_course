01What one genome remembers

One diploid animal carries two copies of every autosome. Followed back through the generations, the two copies of any stretch of DNA meet in one ancestral copy. How long ago that happened depends on how large the population was: in a small population two copies find their common ancestor quickly, in a large one it takes a long time. Recombination has cut the genome into many short stretches, each with its own ancestor and its own date. Heterozygosity records those dates. This section builds the intuition that every method in this lesson rests on, before any program is run.

Coalescent theory from zero

Everything in this lesson rests on one idea, and it needs no mathematics beyond fractions. We will build it in five small steps, each with a picture first. Two words before we start. A gene copy is one copy of one stretch of DNA: a diploid animal carries two, so a population of N animals holds 2N copies. A generation here is one row of a picture: all the copies alive at one time.

Step 1. Forward in time: most lines die out

This simple rule is the Wright-Fisher model. Some copies are picked twice or more, and some are never picked, so their line ends there. Nothing about those copies was worse. It is chance alone, and this change by chance is called genetic drift. Rows are drawn sorted so that the lines do not cross; the place of a copy within a row means nothing.

Step 2. Turn it round: follow copies backward

Now forget everyone else. We have sampled a few copies today, and we ask only where they came from. Each copy has exactly one parent copy, so following it back gives one line, called a lineage. All the faint copies have no descendants in our sample, and we can ignore them. That is what saves the work: instead of simulating a whole population forward, we follow a few lines backward. When two lines reach the same parent they coalesce (they meet), and from there on they are one line. The model of this backward process is the coalescent (Kingman 1982), and Nordborg (2007) gives its starting point as "the realization that the genealogy is usually easier to model backward in time".

Step 3. Two lines meet: count it

The counting says what the formula says. If something has a chance of 1 in 10 each time, you wait 10 tries on average; at 1 in 40 you wait 40.

meeting chance per generation = 1/(2N)
average wait = 2N generations

So small populations meet sooner. With few parents to choose from, two lines soon land on the same one. Look too at the spread in the bars: most pairs wait less than the average, and a few wait far longer.

Step 4. More than two copies: short tips, long deep branches

Every pair of lines is a separate chance to meet. With 6 lines there are 15 pairs, so a meeting comes 15 times sooner than for one pair. Each meeting removes a line and the number of pairs falls quickly: 15, 10, 6, 3, 1. Meetings therefore come fast near the present and slowly at the end.

n lines make n(n − 1)/2 pairs
average wait with k lines ≈ 2N ÷ [k(k − 1)/2]
average height of the tree ≈ 4N (1 − 1/n)

The tree has short tips and long deep branches. The last two lines alone take 2N generations on average, while the whole tree stands 4N(1 − 1/n) high: that is more than half of the height, whatever the sample (for 6 copies 60 %, for 50 copies 51 %). Nordborg (2007) puts it the same way: the time with only two branches is "greater than half the expected total tree height". Adding more copies mostly adds short twigs near the present. This is also why two copies, the ones in one animal, already reach deep into the past.

Step 5. Mutations fall on the branches: differences measure time

The tree comes first and mutations are scattered on it afterwards, like rain on branches. If a mutation does not change how many offspring a copy leaves (it is neutral), it cannot change the tree, so the two can be treated separately (Nordborg 2007). A longer branch collects more mutations. Two copies differ at every mutation on the path that joins them, and that path has two arms, each as long as the time back to their ancestor. So the number of differences between two copies measures the time since they met. That is the reasoning behind the formula in Heterozygosity is a clock, below.

Simple explanation with examples: a class of pupils who each pick a parent

This is an analogy, not biology. Take a class of 20 pupils. Every pupil points at random at one pupil of last year's class and calls that pupil their "parent". Last year's pupils did the same with the class before, and so on.

Going forward: some of last year's pupils are pointed at twice, some not at all. Those not chosen have no "descendants" in this year's class. It works like surnames in a village: a surname dies out when a generation happens to have no sons to carry it, and after enough time one surname can be all that is left, though it was no better than the others.

Going backward: choose two pupils of this year's class, Ana and Ben. Ana has pointed at someone. The chance that Ben pointed at the same person is 1 in 20. If not, look one class further back and try again. On average they share a "parent" after 20 classes: sometimes after 1, sometimes after 60. In a class of 5 it takes about 5.

All the pupils: take the whole class of 20. There are 190 possible pairs, so someone meets almost at once. As the lines join, pairs get scarce, and the last two lines left take as long as any pair does, about 20 classes.

Marks on the way: suppose each pupil copies the family recipe and makes a small random change now and then. Ana's and Ben's recipes differ by the changes on both lines since the shared "parent". Few differences mean a recent meeting, many a distant one. Where the analogy stops: a person has two parents and a surname follows one line of them, whereas a gene copy really does have just one parent copy. That is why the model works so simply for gene copies.

What this model assumes

  • Constant size. The rows keep the same width. Relaxed further down in this section (When the population size changes), and estimating that change is the purpose of PSMC (s03).
  • Random mating. Every parent is equally likely. Related parents break this and leave long homozygous runs (s06; lesson 3, s09).
  • Neutrality. No copy has an advantage, so the mutations do not shape the tree. No section of this lesson relaxes this: the methods here treat the genome as neutral, and selection stays a caution when you read a result.
  • No structure. One population with no groups. The two demes setting above breaks this; what it does to a PSMC curve is in s03 and s06, and gene flow between species in s07.

The pictures above also follow one short stretch of DNA that is never cut. Recombination lifts that limit further down, in Recombination cuts the genome into many histories. Real populations are never this ideal; the size N that would make the ideal model fit is the effective size Ne, defined next, and from here on the text writes Ne instead of N.

Two copies, one ancestor

Coalescence
Two gene copies, traced back in time, "coalesce" in the generation where they come from the same single parental copy. From there back they share one history. The mathematical model of this backward process is the coalescent.
TMRCA
Time to the most recent common ancestor: how many generations back the two copies coalesce. From here on it is the TMRCA of the two copies inside one animal, the maternal and the paternal one, unless a section says otherwise.
Effective population size (Ne)
The size of an idealised population (random mating, constant size, every individual equally likely to be a parent) that would give the same rate of coalescence as the real one. It is not a head count of the animals alive, and methods based on the coalescent measure exactly this: Spence et al. (2018) define the effective size these methods infer as "the inverse coalescence rate for a pair of haplotypes".
Haplotype
One of the two copies of a chromosome (or of a stretch of it): the one from the mother or the one from the father.
Simple explanation with examples: two copies of one gene meeting in the past

Take one gene in one mouse. Its maternal copy came from one of the mother's two copies, which came from one of a grandparent's two copies, and so on. The paternal copy has its own path. Picture both paths going back through a population of 1,000 mice, that is 2,000 copies of the gene in each generation. In each generation the two paths pick their parental copy, and the chance that both land on the same copy is 1 in 2,000. On average that takes 2,000 generations; sometimes it happens after 50 generations, sometimes after 6,000.

Now make the population 100,000 mice: the chance per generation drops to 1 in 200,000, and the paths wander apart for about 200,000 generations on average. During all that time mutations accumulate on both paths. So the two copies of a gene in an animal from a large population differ at more sites: a large population leaves a heterozygous genome, a small one a homozygous genome.

small population mother's copy ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGG father's copy ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGG ancestor few generations back: 0 differences large population mother's copy ACGTCAGCATGACCGTAGACGTTACG father's copy ACGTTAGCATGACCTTAGACGTTAGG ancestor far back: 3 differences

A genome is not one gene. Every few hundred to few thousand bases, recombination in some ancestor switched which ancestral copy the stretch came from, so each stretch has its own ancestor and its own TMRCA. One genome therefore holds a large sample of TMRCAs, and their spread tells you when the population was large or small.

How fast two copies find their ancestor

Step 3 above gave the chance of meeting: one in 2Ne per generation, in a population of Ne diploid individuals. This is the starting point of the PSMC model (the method of s03), which measures time in units of 2N0 generations (N0 is a reference size that the program estimates, close to the most recent size) and sets the coalescence rate at time t to 1/λ(t), where N(t) = N0 λ(t) is the size at that time and λ the size relative to N0 (Li & Durbin 2011, model notes in the PSMC repository).

chance of coalescing per generation = 1 / (2 Ne) mean TMRCA = 2 Ne generations

The waiting time is very variable: with a constant size it follows an exponential distribution, so its spread is as large as its mean. With Ne = 10,000, the mean TMRCA is 20,000 generations, but half of all stretches coalesce within about 13,900 generations (2Ne × ln 2) and one in twenty takes longer than 60,000. That spread is not noise to be averaged away: it is the information. If the size changed over time, the coalescence rate changed with it, and the TMRCAs pile up in the periods when the population was small.

Heterozygosity is a clock

While the two copies are separate, each collects mutations. If the mutation rate is μ per base per generation and the TMRCA is T generations, the two copies have been evolving independently for 2T generations in total, so they differ at about 2μT of their bases. A stretch with a recent common ancestor has almost no heterozygous sites; a stretch with an old one has many. Averaged over the genome under a constant size this gives the classic expectation for the expected heterozygosity per base, written θ:

heterozygosity of a stretch ≈ 2 μ T genome average (constant size) ≈ 2 μ × 2 Ne = 4 Ne μ = θ

Run it backwards and one number already gives a size. Take a genome with 1.5 heterozygous SNVs per kb (0.0015 per base; a round example value). With an illustrative mutation rate of 5.4 × 10−9 per base per generation, Ne = 0.0015 / (4 × 5.4 × 10−9) ≈ 69,000; a genome with twice the heterozygosity gives twice that size. These are long-term averages over the whole history the genome samples, and they scale with the mutation rate you assume (s04). The rest of the lesson is about getting the history instead of one average.

Recombination cuts the genome into many histories

Two copies with a TMRCA of T generations are connected through 2T meioses. Each meiosis can place a crossover anywhere, so the longer ago the ancestor, the more often the stretch has been cut, and the shorter the piece that still shares that one ancestor. With a recombination rate of r per base per generation, the piece has an exponentially distributed length with mean 1/(2rT) bases (the PSMC model notes give this segment length as exponential with rate ρt in scaled units, which is the same thing).

By that arithmetic a mammal genome of a few gigabases is a mosaic of millions of such pieces. Li & Durbin (2011) put it this way: a diploid genome "harbors hundreds of thousands of independent loci, each with its own TMRCA between the two alleles", and the history can be read "by studying how the local density of heterozygous sites changes across the genome". The pieces are too short to see one by one: each holds only a few heterozygous sites. Methods like PSMC never cut the genome into pieces; they weigh all possible TMRCAs along the sequence at once with a hidden Markov model (s03).

When the population size changes

If Ne changed through time, the per-generation chance 1/(2Ne) changed with it. Going back from today, the chance that a stretch has not yet coalesced by generation T is the product of the "no" answers of all earlier generations, which for sizes constant within periods gives:

P(not yet coalesced by T) = exp( − Σ over periods of generations in the period before T / (2 Ne of that period) )

A period with a small Ne is a period with a high coalescence rate. A bottleneck therefore catches most stretches that are still separate when it starts, and a deep or long one catches nearly all of them. That has two consequences you can see in the toy below: the coalescence times pile up inside the bottleneck, and few stretches are left to tell anything about the time before it. A large recent population, in contrast, leaves few coalescences in the recent past, so the recent part of the history rests on little data.

What to notice. (1) Constant: the TMRCAs spread over two orders of magnitude around 2Ne = 100,000 generations; the chromosome is heterozygous almost everywhere, and the zoom shows pieces of a few hundred bases. (2) Bottleneck (Ne 3,000 from 2,000 to 20,000 generations): almost all coalescences fall inside the bottleneck, heterozygosity drops about ten-fold, the pieces become several kb long, and the histogram shows almost nothing older: the genome has forgotten most of what happened before. (3) Old bottleneck (Ne 5,000 from 20,000 to 200,000 generations): nearly every piece that is still separate at 20,000 generations coalesces soon after, so this genome says almost nothing about the large population before 200,000 generations, and its heterozygosity is low although the recent population is large. (4) Expansion: a large recent population means few recent coalescences, so the recent past is poorly sampled. (5) Press new animal: the 50-kb window heterozygosity jumps around between animals with the same history; one genome is one draw.

From the mosaic back to the history

The toy runs forward: history → TMRCAs → heterozygous sites. Demographic inference runs it backwards. PSMC (s03) reads the density of heterozygous sites along the genome, infers the distribution of TMRCAs, and turns it into a coalescence rate per period, reported as Ne. Three limits follow directly from the picture above:

  • It is a coalescence rate, not a census. The curve is the inverse of the rate at which two copies coalesce. It equals a population size only if the population was one randomly mating unit. In a population split into partly connected groups, a curve of changing size can appear even when no group ever changed size (Mazet et al. 2016 call the curve the "inverse instantaneous coalescence rate", IICR). Spence et al. (2018) add that a structured population has a larger effective size than a randomly mating one of the same census size. More in s06.
  • Sharp changes come out blurred. Li & Durbin (2011) simulated a ten-fold drop at 100,000 years and PSMC spread it "over the preceding 50,000 years".
  • The edges are thin. Few pieces coalesce very recently (recent pieces are long, so there are few of them) and few survive very far back. In Li & Durbin's simulations of a human-like history the estimate was precise between 20,000 years and 3 million years ago; more recently or further back "few recombination events are left" and power drops. The window for another species depends on its size history, mutation rate and generation time (s04).

Two things one genome cannot give at all: the history of the last few hundred generations in detail (that needs many individuals, so that many pairs of copies coalesce recently; s05), and the allele frequencies that population-genetic tests between species rely on (s07).

In our projects

One male each of two closely related rodents: species A (our long-read genome) and a close relative with a public HiFi chromosome-level assembly ("the relative" in the rest of this lesson). Counted over the callable sequence, the relative had about 1.7 times as many heterozygous SNVs per kb as species A, so by the formula above its long-term Ne is about 1.7 times larger at any mutation rate the two share. The heterozygosity is far from uniform: about three times as much of the species A genome as of the relative's lies in runs of homozygosity (50-kb window method; lesson 3, s09). Those runs are the pieces with very recent TMRCAs; the rest of the genome holds the older history that the following sections read.
